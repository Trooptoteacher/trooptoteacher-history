Section 1 — SOAPS: Primary Source Identification

LetterCategoryThe Question to AskWhy It Matters
SSpeakerWho created this source? What is their identity, role, and relationship to the subject?The speaker's position shapes what they can and cannot see, and what they are willing to say.
OOccasionWhen was this created? What specific event or moment produced this source?Timing determines what the creator knew, what pressures they faced, and what they were responding to.
AAudienceWho was this source created for? What did that audience already believe or fear?Audience shapes what was emphasized, what was left out, and what tone was chosen.
PPurposeWhat did the creator want this source to DO — persuade, document, justify, demand, commemorate?Purpose affects reliability. A source created to persuade is different evidence than one created to document.
SSubjectWhat is this source actually about — in one clear sentence?Separating subject from purpose prevents you from confusing what a source says with what it is trying to accomplish.

Section 2 — HIPP: Document Analysis for Argumentative Writing

HIPP vs. SOAPSSOAPS tells you WHAT a source is. HIPP asks WHY a source's features matter for your historical ARGUMENT. Always complete SOAPS first; use HIPP to build the argumentative connection.
LetterCategoryThe Question to AskThe Argumentative Move
HHistorical ContextWhat conditions, events, or tensions shaped this source's creation?"This source must be understood against the backdrop of _____, which means its claim about _____ should be interpreted as _____."
IIntended AudienceHow did the intended audience shape what was said — and what was left out?"Because the audience was _____, the author chose to _____ rather than _____, which affects its usefulness as evidence for _____."
PPurposeDoes the creator's purpose make this source more or less reliable for your specific argument?"Because this source was created to _____, it is stronger evidence for _____ than for _____."
PPoint of ViewHow does the creator's identity or position shape what they see and say?"As _____, the author's point of view means this source better captures _____ than _____."

Section 3 — Historical Thinking Skills

SkillDefinition (11th-grade level)The Core Question
[CAUSATION]Identifying and evaluating the multiple forces — political, economic, social, ideological — that produced a historical event or change, and weighing which causes were most significant.Why did this happen? What combination of forces made it possible?
[COMPARISON]Analyzing meaningful similarities and differences between two historical events, policies, or groups, and explaining what those similarities and differences reveal.How are these alike? How are they different? What does that reveal?
[CCOT]Tracking which aspects of American society, politics, or economy transformed across a time period — and which stayed the same — and explaining why.What changed? What stayed the same? Why did some things change while others did not?
[CHRONOLOGY]Placing events in accurate sequence and explaining how the timing and order of developments shaped what came next.In what order did this happen? How did sequence matter?
[CONTEXTUALIZATION]Situating a historical event, decision, or source within its broader moment — the conditions, prior events, and competing forces that shaped it and gave it meaning.What was happening in the world when this occurred? How did context shape the event?

Section 4 — Sourcing & Corroboration Practice

The Rule: Identification ≠ SourcingSaying "This was written for Congress" is identification. Sourcing is: "Because this was written for Congress, the author chose [X language], which makes this source more useful as evidence of [Y claim] than as evidence of [Z claim]." Always make the connection.
