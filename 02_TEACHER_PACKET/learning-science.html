The Research Behind This Schedule Cepeda et al. (2006) found that the optimal gap between study sessions scales with the retention interval — the further out the final test, the longer the ideal spacing. Agarwal et al. (2014) found that three spaced retrieval opportunities produced optimal retention measured up to 9 months after initial learning. This course builds exactly three retrieval opportunities for most major content: the original instruction, a spiral review 1–2 units later, and a cumulative review during the final unit. The bell ringers add additional interleaved retrieval at even shorter intervals, consistent with the short-gap component of optimal spaced practice.

Unit 2: Gilded Age

1870–1900  ·  Learning Science Supplement
🔄Unit 2 — Spiral Review: Reconstruction (Unit 1)

Administer at the START of Unit 2, before any new instruction begins. These questions retrieve content from prior units. Question 5 is always a Bridge Question connecting prior learning to the new unit. Show answers and discuss after students respond individually. Time: 8–10 minutes.

Spiral Review Question 1DOK 2
The Compromise of 1877 effectively ended Reconstruction by withdrawing federal troops from the South. What was the immediate political consequence for formerly enslaved people?
Response:
The withdrawal of federal troops removed the enforcement mechanism protecting Black civil and political rights. Without military protection, Southern states rapidly reinstated racial oppression through Black Codes, Jim Crow laws, and terrorist violence. African American political gains of Reconstruction — including elected officials at state and federal levels — were quickly reversed.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Connects directly to Unit 2's Gilded Age social structure and Jim Crow
Spiral Review Question 2DOK 1
Which three constitutional amendments were ratified during Reconstruction, and what did each one guarantee?
Response:
13th Amendment (1865): abolished slavery. 14th Amendment (1868): granted citizenship and equal protection to all persons born or naturalized in the U.S. 15th Amendment (1870): guaranteed the right to vote could not be denied on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: These amendments become the legal foundation challenged throughout the Gilded Age and Progressive Era
Spiral Review Question 3TNDOK 2
Andrew Johnson, the 17th President, was from Greeneville, Tennessee. Why did Congress override his vetoes during Reconstruction, and what does this reveal about the constitutional balance of power?
Response:
Congress overrode Johnson's vetoes of the Reconstruction Acts because his lenient approach allowed former Confederates to regain power quickly. Johnson's Tennessee roots and Democratic loyalties made him resistant to Radical Republican goals. Congress's ability to override illustrates the constitutional check on executive power and the authority of a two-thirds majority.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Establishes pattern of executive-legislative tension relevant throughout the course
Spiral Review Question 4DOK 2
What were Black Codes, and how did they attempt to recreate conditions of slavery despite the 13th Amendment?
Response:
Black Codes were laws passed by Southern states that severely restricted the rights of African Americans: year-long labor contracts, prohibitions on owning land, restricted movement, and criminalized 'vagrancy.' The 13th Amendment's exception for punishment for crime was exploited through convict leasing, which effectively re-enslaved many Black men through the criminal justice system.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Connects to Unit 11 convict leasing content, including Coal Creek Wars in Tennessee
🌉 Spiral Review Question 5DOK 3
BRIDGE QUESTION: In what two specific ways did the failure of Reconstruction directly create conditions for the Gilded Age economy you are about to study?
Response:
Sample: (1) Sharecropping kept millions of Southern workers in debt cycles, providing cheap agricultural products that fed Northern industrialization without benefiting Southern workers. (2) The abandonment of land redistribution meant formerly enslaved people entered the industrial economy without capital, creating a large pool of low-wage labor that industrialists exploited.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Direct bridge to Unit 2 — students carry this understanding forward
Response:

Unit 3: Progressive Era & Imperialism

1890–1920  ·  Learning Science Supplement
🔄Unit 3 — Spiral Review: Gilded Age + Reconstruction (Units 1–2)

Administer at the START of Unit 3, before any new instruction begins. These questions retrieve content from prior units. Question 5 is always a Bridge Question connecting prior learning to the new unit. Show answers and discuss after students respond individually. Time: 8–10 minutes.

Spiral Review Question 1DOK 2
What was the significance of the Supreme Court's ruling in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), and how did it formalize racial inequality in the post-Reconstruction era?
Response:
Plessy v. Ferguson established the 'separate but equal' doctrine, ruling that racial segregation was constitutional as long as separate facilities were nominally equal. It gave constitutional legitimacy to Jim Crow segregation laws across the South and remained the law of the land until Brown v. Board (1954), representing the Supreme Court's abandonment of Reconstruction-era protections.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Establishes the legal segregation that Progressive-Era civil rights leaders fought against
Spiral Review Question 2DOK 2
Name three specific problems of the Gilded Age that Progressive Era reformers set out to fix. For each problem, identify the reform that addressed it.
Response:
Sample answers: (1) Monopolistic corporations → Sherman Antitrust Act enforcement under TR; (2) Unsafe food and drugs → Pure Food and Drug Act (1906); (3) Political corruption → Direct election of senators (17th Amendment, 1913); (4) Child labor → Progressive-era state laws. Students should match specific reform to specific problem.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Connects Gilded Age problems to Progressive solutions — demonstrates cause and effect across units
Spiral Review Question 3DOK 3
How did railroad expansion — a defining feature of the Gilded Age — both enable American imperialism and create domestic economic tensions that fueled the Progressive movement?
Response:
Railroads created the industrial capacity that made imperialism possible: they moved goods, military supplies, and troops. They also concentrated wealth in a few hands, generated labor exploitation producing strikes and reform demands, and gave rise to the ICC (1887) — the first federal regulatory agency, a precedent for Progressive Era regulation.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Cross-unit synthesis: Unit 2 railroads → Unit 3 regulation and imperialism
Spiral Review Question 4DOK 3
The Populist Party platform of 1892 demanded a graduated income tax, direct election of senators, and government ownership of railroads. Which of these demands were eventually adopted?
Response:
All three were partially adopted: (1) Graduated income tax — 16th Amendment (1913); (2) Direct election of senators — 17th Amendment (1913); (3) Government railroad ownership was not adopted, but ICC and later regulation created significant federal control. Populist ideas, initially dismissed as radical, became mainstream Progressive policy within two decades.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Shows political ideas maturing across movements — critical historical thinking
🌉 Spiral Review Question 5TNDOK 3
BRIDGE QUESTION: Harry T. Burn of Niota, McMinn County, Tennessee cast the deciding vote ratifying the 19th Amendment in 1920. How does his vote represent the culmination of a reform tradition that began with the Reconstruction amendments studied in Unit 1?
Response:
The 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments established that constitutional amendments could expand voting rights. Suffragists argued the 14th Amendment's equal protection clause already guaranteed women the vote. Harry T. Burn's vote — influenced by his mother's letter — completed a 50-year arc from the Reconstruction amendments' expansion of Black male suffrage to women's universal suffrage.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Tennessee content bridge connecting Unit 1 amendments to Unit 3 suffrage

Unit 4: WWI & the Roaring Twenties

1914–1929  ·  Learning Science Supplement
🔄Unit 4 — Spiral Review: Progressive Era, Gilded Age + Reconstruction (Units 1–3)

Administer at the START of Unit 4, before any new instruction begins. These questions retrieve content from prior units. Question 5 is always a Bridge Question connecting prior learning to the new unit. Show answers and discuss after students respond individually. Time: 8–10 minutes.

Spiral Review Question 1DOK 2
What were three specific failures of Reconstruction that left African Americans without legal protection by 1900?
Response:
Sample: (1) Compromise of 1877 withdrew federal troops, ending enforcement of Reconstruction laws; (2) Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) legalized 'separate but equal'; (3) Poll taxes, literacy tests, and grandfather clauses disenfranchised Black voters. Students may also cite failure to redistribute land, leaving Black families without economic independence.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Establishes racial injustice context for WWI-era Great Migration
Spiral Review Question 2DOK 2
How did Progressive Era reforms expand the role of the federal government? Name two specific laws or amendments.
Response:
Answers may include: 16th Amendment (federal income tax), 17th Amendment (direct election of senators), 18th Amendment (Prohibition), 19th Amendment (women's suffrage), Sherman Antitrust Act enforcement, Pure Food and Drug Act (1906), Federal Reserve Act (1913), Clayton Antitrust Act (1914). The Progressive Era established the precedent of federal intervention in the economy and society.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Government expansion established the framework Wilson used to mobilize for WWI
Spiral Review Question 3TNDOK 3
The Scopes Trial took place in Dayton, Rhea County, Tennessee in 1925. What larger tension in American society did it represent, and how does that tension connect to the cultural conflicts of the Roaring Twenties?
Response:
The Scopes Trial represented the clash between modernism (scientific thinking, urban values, secularism) and fundamentalism (religious tradition, rural values, biblical literalism) — the defining cultural conflict of the 1920s: prohibition vs. jazz culture, immigration restriction vs. cosmopolitan cities, KKK revival vs. Harlem Renaissance. Tennessee was the battleground for a national identity crisis.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Tennessee required content connects to the cultural contradictions of the 1920s
Spiral Review Question 4DOK 3
How did American imperialism in the Progressive Era — specifically in the Caribbean and Pacific — create entanglements that contributed to U.S. entry into WWI?
Response:
American imperialism expanded U.S. interests requiring naval protection, driving naval buildup. It also created rivalries with European powers competing for influence. The Monroe Doctrine and its Roosevelt Corollary defined a 'sphere of influence' that Germany's unrestricted submarine warfare directly threatened. American financial interests in European loans made the Allies' defeat economically catastrophic.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Causal bridge from Unit 3 imperialism to Unit 4 WWI
🌉 Spiral Review Question 5TNDOK 3
BRIDGE QUESTION: W.C. Handy, the 'Father of the Blues,' made Beale Street in Memphis, Tennessee a center of African American musical culture. How did the Great Migration — itself caused by conditions stretching back to Reconstruction's failure — carry this tradition northward to create the Jazz Age of the 1920s?
Response:
The Great Migration moved approximately 6 million African Americans from the rural South to Northern cities, escaping Jim Crow laws — the direct legacy of Reconstruction's failure. Memphis was both an origin point and a cultural hub. Beale Street's Blues tradition traveled north with migrants to Chicago (Chicago Blues), New York (Harlem Renaissance jazz), and other cities. The Jazz Age was therefore a direct cultural product of Reconstruction's failure, mediated through Tennessee's musical heritage.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Tennessee content: connects Units 1, 2, 3, and 4 through music and migration
Response:

Unit 5: Great Depression & New Deal

1929–1941  ·  Learning Science Supplement
🔄Unit 5 — Spiral Review: WWI, Progressive Era + Earlier Units

Administer at the START of Unit 5, before any new instruction begins. These questions retrieve content from prior units. Question 5 is always a Bridge Question connecting prior learning to the new unit. Show answers and discuss after students respond individually. Time: 8–10 minutes.

Spiral Review Question 1DOK 3
What were the four main causes of the Great Depression? For each cause, identify one earlier historical decision that made it more likely.
Response:
Main causes: (1) Stock market speculation — enabled by laissez-faire Gilded Age banking attitudes; (2) Overproduction — rooted in WWI wartime production that continued after demand collapsed; (3) Bank failures — 'wildcat banking' practices allowed since the Gilded Age; (4) International debt and tariff wars — products of the punitive WWI peace settlement and protectionist policies. Students should connect each cause to prior units.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Synthesizes economic history across Units 2–4 to explain Unit 5's Depression
Spiral Review Question 2TNDOK 2
The TVA was created on May 18, 1933. What made Tennessee specifically the focus of a federal economic development program, and what had Wilson Dam at Muscle Shoals already demonstrated?
Response:
Tennessee's valley region was among the most economically devastated in America — rural poverty severe, only ~3% of farms had electricity. Wilson Dam (built during WWI to make nitrogen for explosives) demonstrated that federal hydroelectric power generation was technically feasible and could serve both military and civilian purposes. The TVA built on this foundation to create the largest government economic development project in American history.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Tennessee required content — TVA; connects WWI industrial legacy to New Deal
Spiral Review Question 3DOK 3
How did the Progressive Era's expanded role of the federal government make the New Deal politically possible?
Response:
Key precedents: (1) 16th Amendment (income tax) gave revenue for large programs; (2) Federal regulatory agencies (ICC, FTC, FRB) established government could intervene in markets; (3) WWI mobilization proved government could coordinate the entire economy; (4) The precedent of government intervention in utilities made TVA-style ownership less radical. Without Progressive Era expansion of authority, FDR's New Deal would have been constitutionally unprecedented.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Traces the arc of government expansion from Unit 3 through Unit 5
Spiral Review Question 4DOK 3
The Dust Bowl devastated the Great Plains while the Depression devastated the economy. What did both crises have in common as causes?
Response:
Both resulted from unchecked exploitation: the Dust Bowl from decades of over-plowing that destroyed native grasslands (laissez-faire land use); the Depression from unregulated financial speculation (laissez-faire banking). Both required massive federal intervention — the Soil Conservation Service and the FDIC, SEC, and banking reforms. Both marked a fundamental shift in American acceptance of federal regulation as necessary and legitimate.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Cross-unit theme: the limits of laissez-faire capitalism
🌉 Spiral Review Question 5DOK 3
BRIDGE QUESTION: African Americans were disproportionately excluded from New Deal programs. Given Reconstruction's failure, Gilded Age racial violence, and Progressive Era limitations, why is this pattern not surprising — and why does it matter for understanding WWII-era civil rights demands?
Response:
Southern Democratic congressmen whose votes FDR needed refused to support programs disrupting the racial caste system. This same dynamic had ended Reconstruction, allowed Plessy v. Ferguson, and limited Progressive reform. The exclusion from New Deal benefits — combined with service in a segregated WWII military — intensified African American demands for civil rights, directly setting up Unit 7's movement.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Critical race-and-policy thread connecting Units 1, 3, 5, and 7
Response:

Unit 6: World War II

1939–1945  ·  Learning Science Supplement
🔄Unit 6 — Spiral Review: New Deal, Depression + Earlier Units

Administer at the START of Unit 6, before any new instruction begins. These questions retrieve content from prior units. Question 5 is always a Bridge Question connecting prior learning to the new unit. Show answers and discuss after students respond individually. Time: 8–10 minutes.

Spiral Review Question 1TNDOK 2
Why was Oak Ridge, Tennessee chosen as the primary uranium enrichment site for the Manhattan Project, and what was the essential role of TVA electricity?
Response:
Oak Ridge was chosen for: remote location (secrecy), Tennessee River valley (abundant cooling water), Appalachian ridges (natural containment), and crucially the TVA — FDR's New Deal investment — provided massive amounts of cheap electricity that uranium enrichment processes required. Without the TVA, the Manhattan Project's Oak Ridge facilities could not have been powered.
📍 Cross-Unit Connection: Required Tennessee content; directly connects Unit 5 TVA to Unit 6 Manhattan Project
Spiral Review Question 2DOK 3
How did WWI's outcome — specifically the Treaty of Versailles — directly create the conditions that made WWII possible?
Response:
The Treaty imposed punishing terms on Germany: 'war guilt' clause, massive reparations, territorial losses, military restrictions. Spiral Review Question 1DOK 3
Reagan's economic policy ('Reaganomics') cut taxes, reduced regulation, and reduced some social programs. How does this approach differ from New Deal philosophy, and what historical arguments support each?
Response:
New Deal philosophy (Unit 5): government must actively intervene to correct market failures, provide safety nets, and stimulate the economy through spending — Keynesian economics. Reaganomics: markets self-correct when government steps back; high taxes and regulation reduce growth; supply-side tax cuts stimulate investment. Reagan's philosophy was the mirror image of FDR's. Both responded to specific economic conditions: FDR's New Deal addressed the Depression; Reagan's policies addressed stagflation. Students should recognize both as responses to specific crises.

Spiral Review Question 2TNDOK 3

Response:
Baker represented a Republican politics prioritizing institutional integrity over partisan loyalty — his Watergate question was asked of a Republican president. As Reagan's Chief of Staff during Iran-Contra, he again served as a stabilizing force. His Tennessee background placed him in the moderate conservative tradition: fiscally conservative, supportive of TVA, committed to constitutional governance. His career bridges civic republicanism of earlier American political culture with the ideological conservatism of the Reagan era.
