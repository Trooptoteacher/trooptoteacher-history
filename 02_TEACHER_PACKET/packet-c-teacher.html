Section 1 — Teaching Difficult History within Tennessee Law

This section is teacher-facing onlyDo not reproduce this section on student handouts or in student-accessible digital materials.
Teacher Legal Sidebar — TCA § 49-6-1019 and Teaching Contested History Internal teacher guidance

What Tennessee Law Requires

Tennessee Code Annotated § 49-6-1019 prohibits instruction that promotes specific concepts, including: teaching that one race or sex is inherently superior to another; teaching that an individual bears personal responsibility for historical wrongs committed by others of the same race or sex; and teaching that requires students to adopt or affirm personal beliefs as a condition of class participation. This law applies to classroom instruction and to supplemental instructional materials.

This statute does not prohibit factual, evidence-based instruction about historical events involving race, inequality, violence, or injustice. Teaching the history of slavery, segregation, the Civil Rights Movement, and related content is required by Tennessee Academic Standards.

The Design Principle: Analytical, Not Prescriptive

Every TroopToTeacher Technologies LLC primary source activity is framed analytically: students examine the source's Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, and Point of View — they do not adopt the source's perspective as their own. This framing is consistent with § 49-6-1019 because it treats sources as objects of historical inquiry, not as advocacy documents.

Four Facilitation Moves

1. Use neutral analytical stems. Replace "How does this make you feel?" with "What does this source reveal about the perspective of its author?" Replace "Who is to blame?" with "What forces and decisions led to this outcome?"

2. Establish shared ground rules before difficult content. At the start of units with sensitive primary sources: co-create with students a set of norms for historical inquiry. Suggested norm: "We examine sources as historians. We evaluate evidence. We distinguish between historical responsibility and personal blame."

3. Use SOAPS as a buffer. When analyzing primary sources involving racial violence or injustice, SOAPS keeps the analytical frame front and center. Students analyze why the source says what it says rather than reacting to content directly.

4. Close with a structured exit ticket. After lessons on difficult history, use a written exit ticket with a factual prompt: "Name one thing you learned today. Name one question you still have. Name one source you would want to examine further." This reinforces the analytical frame and gives you insight into where students need follow-up.

When Students Ask Personal Questions

If a student asks "Do you think what happened was wrong?" or a similar personal-belief question, an appropriate professional response is: "As historians, we examine evidence and weigh arguments — I'm not going to tell you what to believe, and you're not required to believe anything in particular to succeed in this class. What I will ask you to do is to support whatever position you take with historical evidence."

For specific instructional decisions, consult your district's legal counsel and applicable State Board implementing rules. This sidebar reflects TroopToTeacher Technologies LLC's good-faith reading of TCA § 49-6-1019 as of April 2026 and does not constitute legal advice.

Section 2 — Advanced Sourcing: The Full Analytical Move

Sourcing Skill — What It Requires and How to Model It

Advanced document analysis requires sourcing credit: a student must explain how or why a document's historical situation, audience, purpose, or point of view is relevant to the argument — not simply identified. The critical word is "relevant to" — not just identified.

The Three-Part Move

  1. Name the feature: "Because [HIPP feature]…"
  2. Explain the effect: "…the author chose to / had to / was unable to…"
  3. Connect to the argument: "…which makes this source [more/less useful / stronger / weaker] as evidence for [specific claim]."

Teacher Modeling Example — Eisenhower Farewell Address

Student identification (no credit): "This speech was written by President Eisenhower."

Weak sourcing (partial credit): "Because Eisenhower was a general, he understood military power."

Full sourcing (full credit): "Because Eisenhower was a five-star general and former Supreme Allied Commander, his warning about the military-industrial complex carries a credibility that a civilian president's warning would not — a hostile Congress or public would find it much harder to dismiss as naïve or uninformed. This makes the source more useful as evidence that anxiety about militarization existed even within the national security establishment, not just among progressive critics."

Common Student Error to Address

Students frequently write "This source was created to persuade" and then stop. The analytical error is stopping at identification. When you see this, redirect: "Persuade whom? And how does persuading that particular audience shape what the author included or excluded?"

Section 3 — Using SOAPS + HIPP + Historical Thinking Labels Together

Integration Guide — Three Tools, One Sequence
Step 1 — SOAPS
When: First encounter with any primary source
What students do: Identify Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject
Goal: Know what the source is before deciding how to use it
Time: 5–8 minutes individually or 3 minutes as a class
Step 2 — HIPP
When: When building an argument with evidence
What students do: Connect H/I/P/P features to their specific claim
Goal: Know why this source is useful (or limited) for THIS argument
Time: 8–12 minutes for full analysis; 3–5 minutes for one element
Step 3 — Skill Label
When: When writing the response
What students do: Apply the [SKILL] move — explain causation, compare, trace CCOT, etc.
Goal: Write as a disciplinary historian, not just a summarizer
Time: Embedded in the writing process

Where the overlap is intentional

Audience and Purpose appear in both SOAPS and HIPP. This is by design. SOAPS asks students to identify audience (who?). HIPP asks students to explain the argumentative consequence of that audience (so what?). The overlap is by design: SOAPS asks students to identify audience (who?). HIPP asks students to explain the argumentative consequence of that audience (so what?). Students practice identification before they are expected to analyze.

Differentiation notes

  • Version A / Below-level / ELL: Assign SOAPS only; do not require full HIPP connection; provide sentence stems for SOAPS analysis
  • Version B / On-level: Assign SOAPS + one HIPP element with the argumentative connection; use sentence stems as optional support
  • Version C / Advanced / APUSH: Full HIPP analysis required; argumentative connection sentence for all four elements; no stems provided

Section 5 — Differentiating the Student Packets

Suggestions for Using Packet A and Packet B with Diverse Learners

Packet A (Skills & Practice) — Differentiation Options

  • Below-level / ELL: Complete SOAPS Practice 1 only (Lincoln). Provide the completed SOAPS table for Practices 2 and 3 as a model. For HIPP, assign Practice 3 (short response) only, with sentence stem provided: "Because [HIPP feature], this source [is more/less useful for arguing] _____ because _____."
  • On-level: Complete all three SOAPS practices and one HIPP practice. Assign Corroboration Prompt 1 as the summative task for the packet.
  • Advanced: Complete all practices. Add: "Select any two sources from different units and write a corroboration paragraph showing how they complicate each other's account of a common theme."

Packet B (Inquiry & Writing) — Differentiation Options

  • Below-level / ELL: Limit source selection to 2 sources (not "at least 2"). Provide the completed good-argument checklist with the first item pre-checked as a model. Allow product choice to be limited to short essay with graphic organizer pre-filled. Provide thesis sentence frame: "Based on [Source 1] and [Source 2], I argue that _____ because _____."
  • On-level: Standard packet — full task as written. Three sources recommended; product choice open.
  • Advanced: Add cross-unit synthesis requirement: "Your argument must connect your unit's question to a pattern from at least one other unit in the course. Explain what this connection reveals."

ELL-Specific Supports (use with Version A / ELL Track)

  • Pair each inquiry task with the corresponding vocabulary list from the ELL Support Packet (ell-support.html)
  • Allow students to annotate sources in Spanish; require the final written argument to be in English
  • Provide SOAPS sentence frames: "The speaker is _____, who is important because _____. This was created in _____, when _____ was happening."
  • Scaffold the corroboration requirement: "Source A says _____. Source B says _____. They agree/disagree because _____."

SWD-Specific Supports

  • Chunk Packet A into three separate sessions (one per skill section)
  • For Packet B, allow extended time; reduce source selection to two sources
  • Provide audio versions of primary sources through the district's assistive technology tools
  • Allow dictation or speech-to-text for the draft space in Packet B
  • Reference Appendix N (Accommodations Guide) for IEP-specific modifications
