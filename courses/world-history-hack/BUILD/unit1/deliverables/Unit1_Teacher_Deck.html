W.01

Absolutism & the Divine Right of Kings

Describe the concept of the divine right of kings as well as the features, strengths, and weaknesses of European absolutism, including Louis XIV, Versailles, and Peter the Great. (C, H, P)
I can…
  • define absolutism and the divine right of kings
  • describe the strengths and weaknesses of absolute monarchy using evidence
  • compare the reigns of Louis XIV at Versailles and Peter the Great in Russia
W.01

Hook

If a king says God Himself chose him to rule, who is left with the authority to tell him no?

Key terms

absolutism /AB-suh-loo-tiz-um/
ES: absolutismo		a system in which a monarch holds total, centralized power over the state
divine right /duh-VINE ryte/
ES: derecho divino		the belief that a monarch's authority to rule comes directly from God
Versailles /vair-SY/
ES: Versalles		Louis XIV's vast palace, used to control the French nobility by keeping them at court
boyar /boh-YAR/
ES: boyardo		a Russian noble whose independent power Peter the Great worked to reduce
westernization /wes-ter-nih-ZAY-shun/
ES: occidentalización		adopting western European customs, technology, and institutions, as Peter the Great pushed in Russia

W.02

Limiting the Monarch: Magna Carta & the English Bill of Rights

Compare documents that establish limits on government in response to absolute monarchy (e.g., the Magna Carta and the English Bill of Rights). (H, P)
I can…
  • compare the Magna Carta and the English Bill of Rights
  • explain how these documents limited the monarch and built a constitutional monarchy
  • contrast England's constitutional monarchy with continental absolute monarchies
W.02

Hook

What if a single piece of paper could tell a king, 'Even you must obey the law'?

Key terms

Magna Carta /MAG-nuh KAR-tuh/
ES: Carta Magna		a 1215 English charter forcing the king to accept that he too was under the law
English Bill of Rights /.../
ES: Declaración de Derechos inglesa		a 1689 document giving Parliament key powers and listing rights the monarch could not violate
constitutional monarchy /kon-stih-TOO-shun-ul MON-ar-kee/
ES: monarquía constitucional		a monarchy whose power is limited by law and a governing body such as Parliament
rule of law /rool uv law/
ES: estado de derecho		the principle that everyone, including the ruler, is subject to the law
Parliament /PAR-luh-ment/
ES: Parlamento		England's law-making body, whose power over taxes and laws grew as the monarch's shrank
W.02

Primary source — analyze with HIPPO

“Clause 39 of Magna Carta: no free man shall be imprisoned or stripped of rights 'except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land.'”
Magna Carta (1215) - BL Cotton MS Augustus II 106. Unknown, 1215. Wikimedia Commons. Public domain. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Magna_Carta_(1215)_-_BL_Cotton_MS_Augustus_II_106.jpg

W.03

The Enlightenment: Reason, Rights & Government

Compare the major ideas of philosophers during the Age of Enlightenment, such as Charles-Louis de Montesquieu, Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau. (C, E, H, P)
I can…
  • identify the causes of the Enlightenment, including the emphasis on human reason
  • compare the major ideas of Montesquieu, Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau
  • connect each thinker's key work to its central argument
W.03

Hook

If people could redesign government from scratch using only reason, what would they build?

Key terms

Enlightenment /en-LY-ten-ment/
ES: la Ilustración		a 1600s–1700s movement applying reason to human nature, society, and government
natural rights /NACH-ur-ul rytes/
ES: derechos naturales		rights (life, liberty, property) that Locke argued all people have simply by being human
social contract /SOH-shul KON-trakt/
ES: contrato social		the idea that government is an agreement between rulers and the people
separation of powers /.../
ES: separación de poderes		Montesquieu's idea of dividing government into branches so no one part dominates
popular sovereignty /POP-yuh-lur SOV-rin-tee/
ES: soberanía popular		Rousseau's idea that legitimate authority rests with the general will of the people
W.03

Primary source — analyze with HIPPO

“Locke, Second Treatise of Government: all people have rights to 'life, liberty, and property,' and government exists to protect them.”
Portrait of John Locke. Godfrey Kneller, 1697. Wikimedia Commons. Public domain. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Godfrey_Kneller_-_Portrait_of_John_Locke_(Hermitage).jpg

Image drops in from the primary-source bank at build; citation shown above.

  • Locke: what are natural rights and the purpose of government?
  • Montesquieu vs. Rousseau: separation of powers vs. the general will?
  • Which idea shows up in modern governments?

W.04

The English Civil War & Constitutional Monarchy

Examine the causes and consequences of the English Civil War. (C, G, H, P)
I can…
  • describe the causes of the English Civil War and the roles of key people (James I, Charles I, Cromwell, Charles II)
  • explain the consequences, including the creation of a constitutional monarchy (William and Mary, James II)
  • connect the conflict to the Glorious Revolution and the English Bill of Rights
W.04

Hook

What happens to the idea of divine right the moment a people put their own king on trial?

Key terms

English Civil War /.../
ES: Guerra Civil inglesa		the 1640s conflict between supporters of the king and of Parliament
Oliver Cromwell /OL-ih-ver KROM-wel/
ES: Oliver Cromwell		Parliament's general who led the overthrow of Charles I and ruled the Commonwealth
Charles I /.../
ES: Carlos I		the king whose clashes with Parliament led to civil war and his execution in 1649
Glorious Revolution /GLOR-ee-us rev-uh-LOO-shun/
ES: Revolución Gloriosa		the 1688 replacement of James II with William and Mary, who accepted limits on royal power
constitutional monarchy /kon-stih-TOO-shun-ul MON-ar-kee/
ES: monarquía constitucional		a monarchy limited by law and Parliament
W.04

Primary source — analyze with HIPPO

“The 1649 execution of Charles I was the first time a reigning English king was tried and put to death by his own subjects.”
The Execution of Charles I. Unknown, c. 1649. Wikimedia Commons. Public domain. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Execution_of_Charles_I.png

W.08a

From the Enlightenment to the Declaration of Independence

Identify how the ideas of the Enlightenment inspired Thomas Jefferson and the Declaration of Independence. (C, G, H, P)
I can…
  • identify Enlightenment ideas (natural rights, consent of the governed) in the Declaration of Independence
  • connect Locke's ideas to Jefferson's wording
  • explain how Enlightenment thought crossed from Europe into a real revolution
W.08a

Hook

When Jefferson wrote 'Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness,' whose century-old ideas was he borrowing?

Key terms

Declaration of Independence /.../
ES: Declaración de Independencia		the 1776 document declaring the American colonies free and stating their guiding principles
Thomas Jefferson /.../
ES: Thomas Jefferson		the Declaration's main author, who drew heavily on Enlightenment thought
unalienable rights /un-AY-lee-un-uh-bul rytes/
ES: derechos inalienables		rights that cannot be taken away — Jefferson's version of Locke's natural rights
consent of the governed /.../
ES: consentimiento de los gobernados		the principle that a government's authority comes from the people it rules
self-evident /self-EV-ih-dent/
ES: evidente por sí mismo		a truth so obvious that reason alone proves it — Enlightenment language in the Declaration
W.08a

Primary source — analyze with HIPPO

“Declaration of Independence (1776): 'We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal... endowed... with certain unalienable Rights... Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness.'”
Engrossed Declaration of Independence, front. Unknown, 1776. Wikimedia Commons. Public domain. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Engrossed_Declaration_of_Independence,_front.jpg
