GC.01 — Enlightenment Roots of American Government
Examine the influences of leading European thinkers (e.g., John Locke, Charles-Louis Montesquieu, Thomas Hobbes) and other roots of American government (e.g., Greek democracy, Roman Republic, Magna Carta). (C, E)
SSP 02CONSTITUTIONAL PRINCIPLESC · E

Verbatim learning targets (source of truth)

Read and understand primary source documents (particularly John Locke’s and Jean Jacques Rousseau’s writings) that the Framers used as references in creating the American political system. Explain the Social Contract Theory and make a connection between it and the overall purpose of government, particularly, the U.S. Government.

Success criteria

  • state the key idea of the standard in your own words with an accurate example;
  • use at least one primary source to support your explanation;
  • connect the idea to how it limits or empowers government today.
CFU key DOK 2

According to John Locke, where does a government's rightful authority come from?

Correct: B — The consent of the people being governed

Why: DOK 2: Locke's social-contract theory holds that legitimate government rests on the consent of the governed to protect natural rights — a direct root of the Declaration's 'consent of the governed.'

Retrieval item key DOK 1
Which thinker is most associated with natural rights and the social contract? → B (John Locke)

Dimension crosswalk

  • C: How Enlightenment and classical ideas shaped a shared political culture of liberty.
  • E: Locke's inclusion of property among the natural rights government protects.

CFU key DOK 3

The Declaration lists the colonists' natural rights and then a long list of grievances against the king. Why did the writers put these two parts together?

Correct: B — To show that when a government repeatedly violates the people's rights, the people may justly alter or abolish it

Why: DOK 3: The structure is an argument — universal rights (major premise) + the king's specific violations (evidence) → the conclusion that the colonies may rightfully separate. Grievances turn principle into justification.

Retrieval item key DOK 1

CFU key DOK 2

Under the Articles of Confederation, which power did the national government LACK that caused serious problems?

Correct: B — The power to tax the states and citizens directly

Why: DOK 2: The Articles denied Congress the power to tax; it could only request money from the states. Without reliable revenue, the national government could not pay debts or respond to crises like Shays's Rebellion.

Retrieval item key DOK 1

CFU key DOK 3

The Great Compromise settled which major disagreement at the Constitutional Convention?

Correct: B — How states should be represented in Congress — by population or equally

Why: DOK 3: Large states favored the Virginia Plan (representation by population); small states favored the New Jersey Plan (equal representation). The Great Compromise combined both — a population-based House and an equal-representation Senate.

Retrieval item key DOK 1
The Federalist Papers were written to: → A (persuade people to ratify the Constitution)

CFU key DOK 2

The Preamble begins with the words 'We the People.' Which principle of government does this phrase express?

Correct: B — Popular sovereignty — government power comes from the people

Why: DOK 2: 'We the People' declares that the Constitution's authority flows from the people themselves — the principle of popular sovereignty — not from a king or a single state.

CFU key DOK 3

In Federalist No. 51, Madison writes that 'ambition must be made to counteract ambition.' Which constitutional design does this describe?

Correct: B — Checks and balances that let each branch limit the others

Why: DOK 3: Madison's point is structural — because people seek power, the Constitution pits the branches against each other so each checks the others' ambition. That is the logic of checks and balances.

Retrieval item key DOK 1

CFU key DOK 2

Why did the Framers make the amendment process in Article V deliberately difficult?

Correct: B — So the Constitution could adapt over time, but only with broad agreement across the country

Why: DOK 2: Requiring supermajorities to propose (2/3 of Congress) and ratify (3/4 of states) means change is possible but requires broad national consensus — balancing stability with flexibility.

Retrieval item key DOK 1

CFU key DOK 3

The First Amendment begins 'Congress shall make no law…' Why does the Bill of Rights phrase protections as limits on the government rather than as gifts to the people?

Correct: B — Because the Framers saw rights as belonging to people already — the Constitution's job is to stop government from violating them

Why: DOK 3: The Bill of Rights reflects natural-rights thinking: rights pre-exist government. Framing them as prohibitions ('shall make no law') limits government power rather than granting rights it could later revoke.

Retrieval item key DOK 1
The Bill of Rights is the name for: → A (the first ten amendments to the Constitution)

CFU key DOK 2

What is the main difference between a direct democracy and a republic (representative democracy)?

Correct: B — In a direct democracy citizens vote on laws themselves; in a republic they elect representatives to make laws

Why: DOK 2: In a direct democracy the people vote on laws directly; in a republic (representative democracy) the people elect representatives who make laws on their behalf — the model the Framers chose.
