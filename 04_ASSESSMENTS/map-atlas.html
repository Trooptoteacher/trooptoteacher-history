Reconstruction TN

1865–1877
Standards: US.REC · US.03 SSPs: SSP.04 · SSP.05EOC Frequency: US.03 = 3/5 years 🟠
Essential Question When a society has been divided by war and defined by the most profound moral injustice in its history, what does true reunification require — and who has the power and the obligation to demand it?
StandardDescriptionSSPEOC Freq.
US.RECAnalyze the political, economic, and social goals of Reconstruction; evaluate the achievements and failures; connect to the 13th, 14th, and 15th AmendmentsSSP.05Embedded in all units
US.03Analyze the collapse of Reconstruction including the Compromise of 1877, the rise of Jim Crow laws, Plessy v. Ferguson, and the impact on African American political and economic lifeSSP.043/5 years 🟠

Reconstruction (1865–1877) was the most ambitious attempt in American history to fulfill the democratic ideals of the Declaration of Independence. Following a Civil War that killed approximately 620,000 Americans and destroyed the slave economy of the South, the federal government faced an unprecedented challenge: how to reintegrate eleven states that had committed treason, provide for four million newly freed people, and reconstruct a shattered society on a more just foundation.

The Freedmen's Bureau, established in March 1865, was the federal government's first major social welfare agency. It provided food, clothing, medical care, and crucially — schools — to freed people and white refugees in the South. In Tennessee, the Bureau established hundreds of schools, including the precursors to historically Black colleges. Andrew Johnson of Greeneville, Tennessee, who assumed the presidency after Lincoln's assassination, opposed Radical Reconstruction and vetoed the Reconstruction Acts — only to be overridden by Congress in a constitutional confrontation that led to his impeachment in 1868.

Text-Dependent Analysis Questions

  1. Sourcing (DOK 2): Douglass was formerly enslaved. How does his personal experience shape his authority to evaluate Lincoln's legacy? Why might his perspective differ from that of white Northerners giving similar speeches?
  2. Contextualization (DOK 2): This speech was delivered in 1876 — just as the Compromise of 1877 was being negotiated to end Reconstruction. What was happening politically when Douglass spoke these words?
  3. Close Reading (DOK 3): Douglass calls freed people "step-children" of Lincoln rather than "our man or our model." What does this metaphor reveal about the limits of Lincoln's commitment to racial equality?
  4. Evaluation (DOK 3): Douglass ultimately praises Lincoln despite his critique. Using evidence from this speech and the bar chart above, argue whether Reconstruction fulfilled or betrayed the promises implied by emancipation.

The Gilded Age TN

1870–1900

The Gilded Age (roughly 1870–1900) takes its name from an 1873 novel by Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner — "gilded" meaning a thin layer of gold covering something far less valuable underneath. It was an era of spectacular industrial growth, technological revolution, and breathtaking inequality. The United States became the world's largest industrial economy, but the benefits were distributed with profound injustice.

George Jordan of Williamson County, Tennessee, exemplifies the overlooked heroes of this period. A Buffalo Soldier in the 9th U.S. Cavalry, Jordan earned the Medal of Honor for gallantry at Tularosa, New Mexico Territory in 1880 — one of 25 African American soldiers to receive the Medal of Honor in the Indian Wars. The Buffalo Soldiers are required Tennessee content.

Progressive Era & Imperialism TN

1890–1920

The Progressive Era was America's first sustained effort to use government power to correct the abuses of industrial capitalism. Reformers — the muckrakers — used investigative journalism to expose corruption, unsafe conditions, and exploitation. Upton Sinclair's The Jungle (1906) exposed conditions in meatpacking plants. Ida Tarbell destroyed Standard Oil's reputation. Lincoln Steffens documented municipal corruption.

In Tennessee, Ida B. Wells of Memphis led the nation's anti-lynching campaign through investigative journalism, becoming one of the most important civil rights advocates of the era. Harry T. Burn of Niota, McMinn County, cast the deciding vote ratifying the 19th Amendment on August 18, 1920 — making Tennessee the 36th and final state needed for ratification, earning it the nickname the "Perfect 36." Burn was 24 years old and changed his vote after receiving a letter from his mother urging him to "be a good boy."

The United States entered World War I on April 6, 1917, after years of neutrality. The immediate triggers were German unrestricted submarine warfare — including the sinking of the RMS Lusitania in 1915, killing 1,198 passengers including 128 Americans — and the Zimmermann Telegram, in which Germany secretly offered Mexico an alliance and the return of Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. President Wilson asked Congress to declare war to "make the world safe for democracy."

Alvin C. York of Pall Mall, Fentress County, Tennessee, is the most decorated American soldier of World War I. On October 8, 1918, in the Argonne Forest, Sergeant York single-handedly killed 28 German soldiers and captured 132 more, for which he received the Medal of Honor, the Croix de Guerre, and numerous other decorations. The Scopes Trial of 1925 in Dayton, Rhea County, Tennessee became a national spectacle when biology teacher John Scopes was prosecuted for teaching evolution — a battleground between scientific modernism and religious fundamentalism that defined the cultural conflicts of the 1920s.

World War II TN

1939–1945
Standards: US.45–US.58 (estimated)SSPs: SSP.01 · SSP.04 · SSP.05 · SSP.06Tennessee: Oak Ridge · Camp Forrest · Cordell Hull · Fort Campbell
Essential QuestionWhen a democracy goes to war to defeat fascism and racism abroad, what obligations does it have to confront injustice at home — and who pays when it doesn't?

The United States entered World War II on December 8, 1941, the day after Japan's surprise attack on Pearl Harbor, Hawaii. The war would mobilize 16 million Americans into military service, transform the American economy into the "Arsenal of Democracy," and ultimately reshape the global order. It also forced a confrontation with deep contradictions: America fought against Nazi racial ideology while maintaining a segregated military and imprisoning 120,000 Japanese Americans under Executive Order 9066.

Tennessee's role in WWII was extraordinary. The Manhattan Project's Oak Ridge facility, built in secrecy in Anderson County in 1942, employed 75,000 workers to enrich uranium for the atomic bomb — while most workers did not know what they were building. The TVA's electricity powered the enrichment process; without the New Deal's TVA, the Manhattan Project could not have been located at Oak Ridge. Camp Forrest in Tullahoma, Coffee County, held German POWs. Cordell Hull of Pickett County, Tennessee served as Secretary of State from 1933–1944 — the longest-serving in history — and received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1945 for his foundational role in creating the United Nations.

Cold War & Civil Rights TN

1945–1968
Standards: US.59–US.82SSPs: SSP.01 · SSP.04 · SSP.05Tennessee: Nashville Sit-ins · Clinton High · Tent City · Highlander · Lorraine Motel Memphis
Essential QuestionWhen a society's laws contradict its founding ideals — and peaceful protest, legal argument, and legislative action have all failed — what forms of civil disobedience are morally justified?

The Civil Rights Movement of 1954–1968 was the most important domestic moral struggle in twentieth-century American history — a direct continuation of the unfinished work of Reconstruction. Beginning with the Supreme Court's unanimous ruling in Brown v. Board of Education (1954), and accelerating through the Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56), the Nashville Student Movement (1960), Freedom Rides, Birmingham campaign, March on Washington, and Selma voting rights marches, Black Americans and their allies dismantled the legal architecture of Jim Crow segregation built after the Compromise of 1877.

Tennessee was central to this movement. The Nashville Student Movement (1960) — led by students from Fisk, Tennessee A&I, Meharry, and American Baptist College, trained in nonviolent direct action by Reverend James Lawson — was the most sophisticated and successful sit-in campaign in the South, desegregating Nashville's lunch counters by May 1960. Clinton High School in Anderson County became the first desegregated public high school in the South when 12 Black students enrolled in September 1956 — the "Clinton Twelve." The school was bombed in 1958. Tent City in Fayette and Haywood Counties (1960) housed Black voter registration workers evicted by white landowners. The Highlander Folk School in Grundy County trained Rosa Parks, Martin Luther King Jr., and hundreds of civil rights leaders. Dr. King was assassinated at the Lorraine Motel in Memphis on April 4, 1968, while supporting striking sanitation workers whose signs read "I AM A MAN."

Vietnam, Watergate & Crisis TN

1965–1980
Standards: US.66–US.68, US.83–US.88SSPs: SSP.04 · SSP.05Tennessee: Fort Campbell · Howard Baker · Watergate
Essential QuestionWhen elected officials abuse power and institutions fail, what does a democracy require of its citizens and its other institutions to survive — and what happens when no one asks the hard questions?

The Vietnam War, the Watergate scandal, and the crises of the 1970s (energy crisis, stagflation, Iranian hostage crisis) represented the most sustained period of institutional failure and public disillusionment in modern American history. Beginning with the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964) — a constitutionally questionable authorization that gave President Johnson near-unlimited war powers — the United States escalated military involvement in Vietnam from advisors to over 536,000 troops by 1968. The Tet Offensive of January 1968, in which North Vietnamese forces simultaneously attacked 100+ South Vietnamese cities, destroyed public confidence that the war was being won.

Tennessee's Howard Baker, Republican Senator from Huntsville, Tennessee, served on the Senate Watergate Committee (1973–74) and asked the question that defined the constitutional crisis: "What did the President know, and when did he know it?" Baker's willingness to pursue accountability against a president of his own party became one of the defining acts of institutional courage in American history. Fort Campbell's 101st Airborne Division conducted multiple Vietnam deployments, including the Battle of Hamburger Hill (May 1969) — 72 soldiers killed and 372 wounded over 10 days to take a hill that was then abandoned.
