APPENDIX G: CONSTITUTION QUICK REFERENCE

Key Provisions, Amendments, and Course Examples

THE ORIGINAL CONSTITUTION — THREE BRANCHES

Article I — Congress (Legislative Branch) Congress makes the laws. Senate (100 members, 6-year terms) and House (435 members, 2-year terms). Key powers: levy taxes; regulate interstate commerce; declare war; approve federal budget; impeach officers. Course examples: Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (Unit 8); AUMF (Unit 10); impeachments of Johnson, Clinton, Trump (Units 1, 9, 10); New Deal legislation (Unit 5).

Article II — President (Executive Branch) The president enforces the laws; commands the military; conducts foreign policy; nominates federal judges; can veto legislation. Course examples: War powers debate (Units 4, 6, 7, 8, 10); Nixon resignation (Unit 8); executive orders EO 9066 and EO 9981 (Unit 6); Reagan's Iran-Contra (Unit 9).

Article III — Courts (Judicial Branch) Federal courts interpret the law; the Supreme Court is the final authority on what the Constitution means. Judges serve for life. Course examples: Plessy v. Ferguson (Unit 1); Brown v. Board (Unit 7); Korematsu (Unit 6); United States v. Nixon (Unit 8); Dobbs (Units 8-10).

How a Bill Becomes Law: 1. Introduced in either chamber 2. Referred to committee → markup → committee vote 3. Full chamber debate and vote 4. Same process in the other chamber 5. Conference committee if versions differ 6. Both chambers vote on final version 7. President signs (law) or vetoes 8. Congress can override veto with 2/3 vote in both chambers

The Electoral College: 538 electors; 270 needed to win; most states winner-take-all by popular vote. Possible to win presidency while losing national popular vote. Course examples: Bush v. Gore (2000) — Gore won popular vote, lost Electoral College [TN].

SAMPLE ITEMS BY UNIT

Unit 1 — Reconstruction

Item 1-A-1 (Multiple Choice | DOK 2) The Compromise of 1877 ended Reconstruction by: A) Granting full citizenship rights to all formerly enslaved people B) Withdrawing federal troops from the South and ending federal civil rights enforcement C) Establishing the Freedmen's Bureau as a permanent agency D) Requiring Southern states to ratify the Fourteenth Amendment before readmission

Answer: B

Item 1-D-1 (4-Point Written Response | DOK 4) "Reconstruction failed not because its goals were wrong, but because the federal government lacked the sustained will to enforce its own constitutional amendments." Using evidence from what you have studied, evaluate this claim. Do you agree, partially agree, or disagree?

Scoring guide: 4 pts = Clear claim; 3+ specific pieces of evidence; genuine engagement with counterarguments; 3 pts = Clear claim; 2 pieces of evidence; 2 pts = Attempted claim; 1-2 pieces of evidence; 1 pt = Evidence without clear claim; 0 pts = Off topic.

Unit 5 — Great Depression and New Deal

Item 5-A-1 (Multiple Choice | DOK 2) Which of the following best explains why the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) was controversial when established in 1933? A) The TVA raised electricity prices for Tennessee residents B) Private utility companies argued that the TVA's government backing gave it unfair competitive advantages C) The TVA used environmentally harmful practices that damaged the Tennessee River D) The TVA failed to provide electricity to rural Tennessee farms

Answer: B

Item 5-C-1 (2-Point Written Response | DOK 3) Explain why the Social Security Act (1935) is considered a landmark piece of legislation. Include BOTH what it achieved AND one significant limitation of the original Act.

Scoring guide: 2 pts = Accurate achievement (old-age insurance/safety net principle) AND accurate limitation (domestic/agricultural worker exclusion; racially motivated); 1 pt = One element only; 0 pts = Significant inaccuracies.

Unit 7 — Cold War and Civil Rights

Item 7-C-1 (2-Point Written Response | DOK 3) Explain what happened at Nashville City Hall on April 19, 1960, and why this moment was significant for the Civil Rights Movement.

Scoring guide: 2 pts = Accurate description (students marched; Diane Nash confronted Mayor West; West agreed discrimination was wrong; lunch counters desegregated) AND significance (first major sit-in victory; Nashville model carried nationally by movement's leaders); 1 pt = One element only.

Unit 8 — Vietnam and Watergate

Item 8-A-1 (Multiple Choice | DOK 2) What was Senator Howard Baker of Tennessee's most significant contribution to the Watergate investigation? A) He provided the key evidence that led to Nixon's impeachment B) He asked "What did the president know, and when did he know it?" — establishing the standard for investigating executive misconduct C) He voted to convict Nixon in the Senate trial D) He appointed the special prosecutor who investigated Nixon

Answer: B

Unit 10 — 21st Century America

Item 10-C-1 (2-Point Written Response | DOK 3) Explain what happened on January 6, 2021, and why historians and legal scholars consider this event constitutionally significant.

Scoring guide: 2 pts = Accurate description (mob attacked Capitol; Congress certifying Electoral College results; Capitol breached) AND constitutional significance (first attempt to prevent peaceful transfer of presidential power through violence; peaceful transfer is foundational to republican government); 1 pt = One element only.

APPENDIX L: DISCUSSION PROTOCOL CARDS

For Classroom Discourse Structures

Print on cardstock and laminate. One set per classroom.

CARD L-1: THINK-PAIR-SHARE

THINK (2-3 min): Write your individual response. Use evidence. Don't talk yet.

PAIR (3 min): Share with your partner. Listen without interrupting. Find: one agreement AND one disagreement.

SHARE: Share your PARTNER's idea — not just your own. Build on what others say.

Sentence starters: - "Building on what ___ said..." - "I agree/disagree because the evidence shows..." - "I want to push back on that — the document says..."

CARD L-2: SOCRATIC SEMINAR

Prepare: Read the assigned text. Write TWO text-based questions. Write your opening position (1-2 sentences).

Inner circle: Discuss. Refer to the text. Listen before speaking. Outer circle: Observe and take notes. Be ready to give feedback.

Accountable Talk Stems: - "The text says... which makes me think..." - "Building on what ___ said..." - "I want to push back — the document actually..." - "What would someone who disagrees say about..."

After: Written reflection — How did the seminar change your thinking?

CARD L-3: STRUCTURED ACADEMIC CONTROVERSY (SAC)

Step 1 — Advocate Position A (5-7 min): Make the STRONGEST possible case for Position A. Use evidence.

Step 2 — Advocate Position B (5-7 min): Switch. Make the STRONGEST possible case for Position B.

Step 3 — Drop Advocacy (5 min): Set aside assigned positions. Discuss honestly: - What was the strongest argument from each side? - What evidence would change your mind?

Step 4 — Consensus: What position does the evidence best support? Write a joint statement.

CARD L-4: FISHBOWL

Inner Circle (4-5 students): Discuss the question. Use evidence. One empty chair — any outer circle student may "tap in."

Outer Circle: Observe. Take notes on: What claims were made? What evidence was used? What questions were NOT answered?

Debrief: Outer circle analyzes the inner circle's discussion using the observation questions above.

CARD L-5: GALLERY WALK

  1. Move through stations (3-4 min each)
  2. At each station: analyze, discuss, add a sticky note
  3. Respond to other groups' notes using a different color
  4. Return to seats; discuss patterns across all stations

Reflection: What patterns did you notice? What new questions do you have?

CARD L-6: ACCOUNTABLE TALK STEMS (Post Permanently)

To BUILD on an idea: - "Building on what ___ said..." - "To add to that point..." - "I agree with ___ because..."

To RESPECTFULLY DISAGREE: - "I see it differently — the evidence shows..." - "I want to push back on that — actually..." - "That's one interpretation, but another way is..."

To ASK for CLARIFICATION: - "Can you say more about why you think...?" - "What evidence supports that?" - "I'm not sure I understand — can you explain...?"

To CONNECT to TEXT: - "The document says... which means..." - "According to [source]..." - "The evidence that supports this is..."

To SYNTHESIZE: - "What I'm hearing is that we agree on... but disagree on..." - "To summarize what we've discussed..." - "The key question we haven't resolved is..."

MASTER SENTENCE FRAME BANK

Explaining causes: - "__ happened because __." - "One cause of __ was __. Another cause was __." - "__ led to __, which led to __."

Analyzing primary sources: - "The author of this source was __, who wrote it for __." - "The purpose of this source was to __." - "This source shows that __. This is important because _____."

Constructing arguments: - "I believe __ because the evidence shows __." - "The most important reason is __ because __." - "Some people argue __. However, the evidence shows __."

Tennessee connections: - "Tennessee was involved in this event because __." - "The Tennessee connection to this topic is __." - "__ happened in Tennessee, which is important because __."

Multiple perspectives: - "From one perspective, __. From another perspective, __." - "__ would argue __. On the other hand, __ would argue __."

Writing conclusions: - "In conclusion, __ because __." - "This shows that __." - "The lasting significance of __ is _____."

TIERED VOCABULARY FRAMEWORK

Tier 1 (High-frequency words in new context): Words students may know casually but need to understand in historical context. Examples: collapse, relief, reform, federal, unemployment, surrender, escalation, recession

Tier 2 (Domain-specific academic vocabulary): Words needed to discuss history at an academic level. Examples: segregation, containment, détente, securitization, appeasement, civil disobedience, stagflation

Tier 3 (Advanced vocabulary for higher proficiency): Specialized terms used by historians and legal scholars. Examples: eugenics, coram nobis, preclearance, habeas corpus, laissez-faire, proletariat
