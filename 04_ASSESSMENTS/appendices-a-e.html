APPENDIX A: GLOSSARY

All Key Terms — Student-Friendly Definitions

Alphabetical Order | Units 1–10

How to Use This Glossary: - Terms appear in bold with the unit(s) where they are introduced - Pronunciations appear in (parentheses) for difficult words - Cross-references appear as see also - Tennessee-specific terms are marked [TN] - Terms required by TCA § 49-6-1006 are marked [BH] (Black History) - Terms required by TCA § 49-6-1028(b) are marked [CR] (Constitutional Republic / Capitalism)

A

AAA (Agricultural Adjustment Administration) | A New Deal agency (1933) that paid farmers to reduce production of major crops to raise prices. It helped large farm operators but discriminated against Black tenant farmers and sharecroppers. Struck down by the Supreme Court in 1936. | Unit 5 [BH]

Abolitionism | (ab-uh-LISH-uh-niz-um) | The movement to end slavery in the United States. Abolitionists used moral arguments, political action, and support for the Underground Railroad to fight slavery before and during the Civil War. | Unit 1

Abu Ghraib | (ah-boo GRAYB) | A U.S.-run prison in Iraq where photographs revealed in 2004 showed American soldiers abusing Iraqi detainees. The scandal was a major blow to U.S. credibility and sparked debate about military conduct and the use of "enhanced interrogation." | Unit 10

Affordable Care Act (ACA, 2010) | Also called "Obamacare." The most significant expansion of health insurance coverage since Medicare/Medicaid (1965). Signed March 23, 2010. Extended coverage to approximately 20 million previously uninsured Americans. | Unit 10

AFL (American Federation of Labor) | A federation of craft unions founded in 1886 by Samuel Gompers that organized skilled workers by their specific trade (carpenters, cigar makers, etc.) rather than industry-wide. Distinguished from the later CIO's industrial unionism. | Unit 2

AIDS Crisis | The Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome epidemic that emerged in the early 1980s. The Reagan administration's delayed response — failing to treat AIDS as a public health emergency during critical early years — has been widely criticized. The crisis disproportionately affected gay men, Black Americans, and hemophiliacs. | Unit 9 [BH]

Alamo, The | see Spanish-American War; Imperialism | Unit 3

America First Committee | An isolationist organization founded September 1940 that opposed U.S. entry into World War II. Charles Lindbergh was its most prominent speaker. It disbanded four days after Pearl Harbor. | Unit 6

American Liberty League | A conservative organization funded primarily by wealthy businessmen (including the du Pont family) that opposed the New Deal as socialist and unconstitutional. | Unit 5 [CR]

Annexation | (an-ek-SAY-shun) | The formal incorporation of territory into a country. The U.S. annexed Hawaii (1898), Puerto Rico, and the Philippines after the Spanish-American War. | Unit 3

Anti-trust | Laws or actions that prevent monopolies and promote competition in the marketplace. Theodore Roosevelt used anti-trust law to break up large corporate trusts. | Unit 3 [CR]

Appeasement | (uh-PEEZ-ment) | A policy of giving in to the demands of an aggressive power hoping it will stop. The Munich Agreement (1938), in which Britain and France gave Hitler the Sudetenland, is the defining example of failed appeasement. | Unit 6

Armistice | (AR-mis-tis) | A formal agreement to stop fighting, usually temporarily while a peace treaty is negotiated. World War I ended with an armistice on November 11, 1918, at 11:00 AM. | Unit 4

Arsenal of Democracy | FDR's term for the United States' role in supplying Britain and Allied nations with military equipment through the Lend-Lease Act (1941), before the U.S. entered WWII. | Unit 6

Assimilation | The process by which immigrants or minority groups adopt the culture, language, and customs of the majority society. U.S. government policy forced assimilation on Native Americans through boarding schools. | Unit 2

AUMF (Authorization for Use of Military Force, 2001) | Congressional resolution signed September 18, 2001, authorizing the president to use force against those who "planned, authorized, committed, or aided" the 9/11 attacks. Its broad language was still cited to justify military operations more than 20 years later. | Unit 10 [CR]

Axis powers | (AK-sis) | The alliance of Germany, Italy, and Japan during World War II. | Unit 6

B

Baker, Howard (1925–2014) | Republican senator from Huntsville, Tennessee (1967–1985). As ranking minority member of the Senate Watergate Committee, he asked the defining question: "What did the president know, and when did he know it?" He later served as Reagan's Chief of Staff (1987–1988). | Units 8, 9 [TN] [CR]

bank run | When large numbers of depositors simultaneously try to withdraw their savings from a bank, often causing it to fail. The FDIC, created in 1933, effectively ended bank runs by insuring deposits. | Unit 5

Berlin Wall | A barrier built in August 1961 dividing communist East Berlin from democratic West Berlin. Its fall on November 9, 1989 is the defining symbol of the Cold War's end. | Unit 9

Black Codes | Laws passed by Southern states after the Civil War (1865–1866) that severely restricted the rights of freed Black Americans — limiting their movement, employment, and legal rights. They effectively re-created conditions of servitude. | Unit 1 [BH]

Black Lives Matter | A social movement founded in 2013 by Alicia Garza, Patrisse Cullors, and Opal Tometi. Gained national prominence following police killings including those of Michael Brown (2014), Eric Garner (2014), and George Floyd (2020). | Unit 10 [BH]

Black Tuesday (October 29, 1929) | The day of the catastrophic stock market crash that triggered the Great Depression. Approximately 16 million shares were traded as prices collapsed. | Unit 5

Bolshevik Revolution (1917) | The communist revolution in Russia that overthrew the tsar and established the Soviet Union. It created the fear of global communism that drove the Red Scare in the United States. | Unit 4

Bonus Army (1932) | Approximately 17,000 WWI veterans who marched on Washington to demand early payment of service bonuses. Hoover ordered the Army (under MacArthur) to drive them out, burning their camps. | Unit 5

Brown v. Board of Education (1954) | The Supreme Court decision (347 U.S. 483) declaring racial segregation in public schools unconstitutional. Chief Justice Warren wrote for a unanimous 9-0 Court that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." | Unit 7 [BH] [CR]

bully pulpit | Theodore Roosevelt's term for the president's ability to use the visibility and influence of the office to advocate for policies and shape public opinion. | Unit 3

Bush v. Gore (2000) | 531 U.S. 98; the Supreme Court decision (5-4) that effectively decided the 2000 presidential election by stopping the Florida recount. Tennessee's Al Gore won the popular vote but lost the Electoral College. | Unit 10 [TN] [CR]

C

Camp David Accords (1978) | The framework for peace between Egypt and Israel negotiated by President Carter. Egyptian President Anwar Sadat and Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin signed on September 17, 1978. The first peace agreement between Israel and an Arab nation. | Unit 8

capitalism | (KAP-uh-tul-iz-um) | An economic system in which private individuals and businesses own the means of production, operate for profit, and compete in free markets. The United States' economic system. See also free enterprise, supply-side economics, Keynesian economics. | All units [CR]

Carnegie, Andrew (1835–1919) | Scottish immigrant who became America's most powerful steel magnate in the Gilded Age. Built Carnegie Steel (later U.S. Steel) into the world's largest industrial enterprise. Gave away most of his fortune in his later years (Carnegie libraries, etc.). | Unit 2

CCC (Civilian Conservation Corps) | A New Deal work-relief program (1933–1942) that employed young men in national parks and forests. Racially segregated. | Unit 5 [BH]

civil disobedience | The deliberate, nonviolent refusal to obey unjust laws as a form of protest, while willingly accepting the legal penalty. Associated with Thoreau, Gandhi, and the Civil Rights Movement. | Unit 7

Civil Rights Act of 1964 | The landmark federal law prohibiting discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in public accommodations and employment. Signed July 2, 1964. Created the EEOC. | Unit 7 [BH] [CR]

Civil Service Reform | The replacement of the "spoils system" (giving government jobs to political supporters) with a merit-based examination system. The Pendleton Civil Service Act (1883) began this process. | Unit 2

Clayton Antitrust Act (1914) | Strengthened anti-trust law by specifically prohibiting price discrimination, exclusive dealing, and interlocking corporate directorates. Also stated that labor unions were not illegal combinations. | Unit 3

Cold War | The state of geopolitical tension, ideological competition, arms race, and proxy conflict between the United States and Soviet Union lasting approximately 1947–1991. "Cold" because the nuclear-armed superpowers never directly fought each other. | Units 7, 8, 9

collective bargaining | The negotiation of wages, hours, and working conditions between organized workers (a union) and their employer as a group, rather than individually. Guaranteed by the Wagner Act (1935). | Units 2, 5 [CR]

Compromise of 1877 | The deal that ended Reconstruction: Republicans kept the presidency (Hayes) in exchange for withdrawing federal troops from the South and ending federal enforcement of Black civil rights. | Unit 1 [BH]

Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) | A federation that organized workers across entire industries regardless of skill level, unlike the AFL's craft unionism. United auto workers, steel workers, and other industrial workers in the 1930s–40s. | Unit 5

containment | (kun-TAYN-ment) | The U.S. Cold War strategy of preventing the spread of Soviet communism to new countries. Articulated by George Kennan; implemented through the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, and NATO. | Unit 7 [CR]

Contract with America (1994) | A document signed by 367 Republican House candidates pledging specific legislative action if elected. Helped Republicans gain control of Congress in 1994; Newt Gingrich became Speaker. | Unit 9 [CR]

Coolidge, Calvin (1872–1933) | President 1923–1929. "Silent Cal." Presided over the economic boom of the 1920s. "The business of America is business." | Unit 4

court-packing plan (1937) | FDR's proposal to add up to six new Supreme Court justices for each justice over age 70 who did not retire. Defeated in Congress as an attack on judicial independence. | Units 5, 9 [CR]

"crisis of confidence" | President Carter's July 15, 1979 characterization of the American national mood — a loss of faith in government and institutions produced by Vietnam, Watergate, inflation, and the oil crisis. | Unit 8

Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962) | The thirteen-day confrontation between the U.S. and Soviet Union over Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba. The most dangerous moment of the Cold War; resolved when the Soviets removed the missiles. | Unit 7

D

D-Day (Operation Overlord, June 6, 1944) | The Allied amphibious invasion of Normandy, France — the largest such operation in history. Approximately 156,000 Allied troops participated on the first day. It established the Western Front that led to Germany's defeat. | Unit 6

Dawes Act (1887) | A federal law that broke up Native American tribal lands and distributed them as individual allotments to Native families, with "surplus" land sold to white settlers. It destroyed tribal culture and was a major cause of Native land loss. | Unit 2

deficit spending | (DEF-ih-sit) | When a government spends more than it collects in taxes, borrowing the difference. Keynes argued for deficit spending during recessions; Reagan's tax cuts produced large deficits. | Units 5, 9 [CR]

deregulation | Reducing or removing government regulations that control an industry. Reagan pursued deregulation of airlines, financial services, and telecommunications. | Unit 9 [CR]

détente | (day-TAHNT) | A relaxation of tension between the United States and Soviet Union. Nixon and Kissinger pursued détente through the SALT I treaty (1972) and Nixon's China visit (1972). | Units 8, 9

Diane Nash (b. 1938) | Fisk University student from Chicago who became the strategic leader of the Nashville Student Movement. Her confrontation of Mayor Ben West on April 19, 1960 was pivotal in desegregating Nashville's lunch counters. Co-founded SNCC and organized the Freedom Riders. | Unit 7 [TN] [BH]

Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022) | 597 U.S. 215; the Supreme Court decision overturning Roe v. Wade (1973), returning abortion regulation to state legislatures. The most significant reversal of a previously established constitutional right in modern history. | Units 8, 9, 10

Dollar Diplomacy | President Taft's foreign policy of using American economic investment (rather than military force) to extend U.S. influence — particularly in Latin America. | Unit 3

Double V Campaign | An African American campaign during WWII calling for "double victory" — victory against fascism abroad AND victory against racial discrimination at home. Launched by the Pittsburgh Courier in February 1942. | Units 6, 8 [BH]

Du Bois, W.E.B. (1868–1963) | (doo BOYSS) | Scholar, civil rights leader, and co-founder of the NAACP. Challenged Booker T. Washington's accommodationist approach, arguing for immediate political equality and full civil rights. Author of The Souls of Black Folk (1903). | Units 2, 3 [BH]

Dust Bowl | The environmental disaster on the southern Great Plains in the 1930s, caused by severe drought combined with destructive agricultural practices. Approximately 3.5 million people abandoned the region. | Unit 5

E

Eisenhower, Dwight D. (1890–1969) | Supreme Allied Commander in WWII; President 1953–1961. His farewell address warned against the "military-industrial complex." Sent the 101st Airborne to Little Rock (1957) to enforce desegregation. | Units 7, 8

Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) | The federal regulatory agency established December 2, 1970 to enforce environmental law. Created by President Nixon under pressure from the environmental movement following the first Earth Day. | Unit 8

escalation | (es-kuh-LAY-shun) | A gradual increase in the intensity or extent of a conflict. LBJ's escalation of the Vietnam War brought U.S. troop levels from 16,000 advisors (1963) to 536,000 combat troops (1968). | Unit 8

eugenics | (yoo-JEN-iks) | The pseudoscientific belief that human populations could be "improved" by selective breeding. Eugenics beliefs influenced immigration restriction laws in the 1920s. | Unit 4

Executive Order 9066 (1942) | The order signed by President Roosevelt on February 19, 1942, authorizing the military to forcibly relocate approximately 120,000 Japanese Americans to internment camps. | Unit 6 [CR]

F

fascism | (FASH-iz-um) | A political ideology combining extreme nationalism, authoritarian one-party rule, glorification of military power, and rejection of both liberal democracy and socialism. Mussolini's Italy and Hitler's Germany were fascist states. The antithesis of both capitalism and constitutional democracy. | Units 6 [CR]

FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation) | Created by the Glass-Steagall Act (1933). Insures bank deposits (currently up to $250,000). Ended the bank runs that had caused thousands of bank failures during the Depression. Still insures your bank deposits today. | Unit 5

Febb Burn letter | A letter written by Phoebe "Febb" Burn to her son, Tennessee state representative Harry T. Burn, urging him to vote for ratification of the 19th Amendment. Held at the Tennessee State Library and Archives. When Burn changed his vote, he provided the deciding vote for ratification in August 1920. | Unit 3 [TN]

Federal Reserve | The central banking system of the United States, created in 1913. Made a catastrophic error during the Depression by raising interest rates and contracting the money supply. Manages monetary policy today. | Units 3, 5

Fifteenth Amendment (1870) | Prohibited the federal and state governments from denying citizens the right to vote based on "race, color, or previous condition of servitude." Effectively nullified in the South through literacy tests, poll taxes, and violence until the Voting Rights Act (1965). | Unit 1 [BH] [CR]

Fort Campbell | Military installation straddling the Kentucky-Tennessee border near Clarksville, Tennessee. Home of the 101st Airborne Division. One of the most deployed Army installations in the post-9/11 era, with multiple tours in Afghanistan and Iraq. | Units 9, 10 [TN]

Fourteenth Amendment (1868) | Granted citizenship to all persons born or naturalized in the United States; guaranteed "equal protection of the laws" and due process. The constitutional foundation for civil rights law. Violated by Jim Crow, upheld in Plessy, finally enforced in Brown. | Units 1, 6, 7 [BH] [CR]

Freedmen's Bureau | A federal agency (1865–1872) established to assist formerly enslaved people transition to freedom — providing food, clothing, medical care, legal assistance, and education. Underfunded and ultimately undermined by President Johnson. | Unit 1 [BH]

Freedom Riders (1961) | Interracial groups organized by CORE and SNCC to test whether the Supreme Court's ruling against segregation in interstate bus terminals would be enforced. Riders were violently attacked in Alabama. Nashville's Diane Nash organized replacements who continued the rides. | Unit 7 [TN] [BH]

free enterprise | An economic system in which private businesses compete with minimal government interference. Also called capitalism or the free market system. | All units [CR]

G

genocide | (JEN-oh-syd) | The deliberate killing of a large group of people, especially those of a particular nation, ethnicity, or religion. The Holocaust — the systematic murder of approximately six million Jews by Nazi Germany — is the defining case. | Unit 6

Gilded Age | (GILD-ed) | The period from approximately 1870–1900, marked by rapid industrialization, extreme economic inequality, and the dominance of robotic trusts. Mark Twain coined the term, suggesting the era's apparent wealth masked serious social problems. | Unit 2 [CR]

glasnost | (GLAHS-nust) | (Russian: openness) Gorbachev's policy of greater freedom of expression in the Soviet Union, begun 1985. Intended to reform the Soviet system, it released political pressures that ultimately destroyed it. | Unit 9

Glass-Steagall Act (1933) | A banking reform law that separated commercial banking from investment banking. Also created the FDIC. Repealed in 1999; some economists argue this contributed to the 2008 financial crisis. | Unit 5

globalization | The increasing interconnection of the world's economies, cultures, and populations through trade, technology, and communication. NAFTA and the Internet both accelerated globalization in the 1990s. | Unit 9

Great Depression | The severe global economic crisis that began with the 1929 stock market crash and lasted through most of the 1930s. At its worst, U.S. unemployment reached approximately 25%. Ended by WWII economic mobilization. | Unit 5

Great Migration | The movement of approximately 6 million Black Americans from the rural South to Northern and Western cities, primarily between 1910 and 1970, seeking economic opportunity and escape from Jim Crow. | Units 4, 7 [BH]

Great Society | President Lyndon Johnson's ambitious domestic reform program (1964–1968), including Medicare, Medicaid, ESEA, the Voting Rights Act, and War on Poverty programs. The most significant expansion of federal social programs since the New Deal. | Unit 7

Great Recession (2007–2009) | The severe economic contraction following the 2008 financial crisis — the worst since the Great Depression. Unemployment peaked at 10%; 8.7 million jobs lost. | Unit 10

Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964) | Congressional authorization (Public Law 88-408) passed August 7, 1964, giving the president unlimited authority to use military force in Southeast Asia — based partly on a fabricated August 4 attack. | Unit 8 [CR]

H

Harding, Warren G. (1865–1923) | President 1921–1923. His administration was marked by the Teapot Dome scandal and cronyism, though the economy boomed. Died in office. | Unit 4

Harlem Renaissance | A flowering of African American art, literature, music, and culture centered in Harlem, New York, in the 1920s. Key figures include Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Louis Armstrong, and Duke Ellington. | Unit 4 [BH]

Harry T. Burn | Tennessee state representative from Niota (McMinn County) who cast the decisive vote ratifying the 19th Amendment (women's suffrage) in August 1920. He had received a letter from his mother, Febb Burn, urging him to vote yes. | Unit 3 [TN]

Hay, John (1838–1905) | Secretary of State under McKinley and Roosevelt. Negotiated the Open Door Policy with China and helped manage U.S. foreign policy during the era of imperialism. | Unit 3

Highlander Folk School | An adult education center founded 1932 in Tennessee (originally Monteagle; now New Market) that trained generations of civil rights leaders including Rosa Parks (1955), Martin Luther King Jr., and many others. One of the first interracial institutions in the South. | Units 7 [TN] [BH]

Holocaust | (HOL-oh-kawst) | The systematic, state-organized persecution and murder of approximately six million Jews by the Nazi German government between 1933 and 1945, along with millions of Roma, people with disabilities, Poles, Soviet civilians, and others. | Unit 6

Homestead Act (1862) | A federal law offering 160 acres of public land to settlers who would farm it for five years. It accelerated westward settlement but dispossessed Native peoples and proved inadequate for farming on the semi-arid Great Plains. | Unit 2

Hoovervilles | (HOO-ver-vilz) | Shantytowns built by homeless people during the Depression, sarcastically named after President Hoover. | Unit 5

Howard Baker | see Baker, Howard [TN]

I

imperialism | (im-PEER-ee-uh-liz-um) | A policy of extending a nation's power and influence through military force, economic control, or political domination of other nations. The U.S. became an imperial power through the Spanish-American War (1898). | Unit 3

income inequality | The unequal distribution of income among a population. Extreme in the Gilded Age; reduced by New Deal/WWII; rising again since the 1970s. | Units 2, 5, 9, 10 [CR]

inflation | A general increase in prices and decrease in the purchasing power of money. The stagflation of the 1970s combined high inflation with high unemployment. | Units 8, 9

internment | (in-TURN-ment) | The imprisonment or confinement of people without trial, usually during wartime. During WWII, approximately 120,000 Japanese Americans (2/3 of them U.S. citizens) were interned in camps. | Unit 6 [CR]

Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC) | The first federal regulatory agency (created 1887) to regulate railroad rates and practices. A product of Populist and Progressive pressure for government oversight of business. | Units 2, 3 [CR]

Iran-Contra Affair | A Reagan administration scandal (revealed 1986) in which officials secretly sold weapons to Iran and used the profits to fund Nicaraguan Contra rebels — violating congressional law. Howard Baker of Tennessee became Reagan's Chief of Staff to help manage the crisis. | Units 8, 9 [TN] [CR]

Iran Hostage Crisis (1979–1981) | A diplomatic crisis in which 52 Americans were held hostage in Tehran for 444 days after Iranian revolutionaries stormed the U.S. Embassy. Dominated the final year of Carter's presidency. | Unit 8

Iron Curtain | Winston Churchill's term (1946) for the ideological and physical division between Soviet-controlled Eastern Europe and the democratic Western nations. | Unit 7

isolationism | (i-SOH-luh-shun-iz-um) | A foreign policy of avoiding entanglement in other nations' affairs. The dominant U.S. approach between WWI and WWII; challenged by WWII and abandoned by the Cold War. | Units 4, 6

J

James Lawson (b. 1928) | A Methodist minister who spent three years studying Gandhian nonviolence in India before coming to Nashville. Organized the nonviolent training workshops that prepared the Nashville Student Movement. MLK called him "the leading theorist and tactician of nonviolence in the world." | Unit 7 [TN] [BH]

January 6, 2021 | The attack on the U.S. Capitol by a mob seeking to prevent Congress from certifying the 2020 presidential election results. The first attempt in American history to prevent the peaceful transfer of presidential power through violence. | Unit 10 [CR]

Jim Crow | The system of laws and social customs that enforced racial segregation and white supremacy in the South from the end of Reconstruction through the Civil Rights Movement. Named for a demeaning minstrel character. | Units 1, 2, 3 [BH]

John Lewis (1940–2020) | Student at American Baptist Theological Seminary in Nashville; trained by James Lawson; core Nashville sit-in leader; chaired SNCC; led the march at Selma's Edmund Pettus Bridge (Bloody Sunday, 1965); served 33 years in Congress. His Tennessee years were foundational to the national movement. | Unit 7 [TN] [BH]

Johnson, Andrew (1808–1875) | 17th President (1865–1869). Tennessee Democrat who became president after Lincoln's assassination. Opposed Reconstruction, vetoed civil rights legislation, and was the first president to be impeached (1868; acquitted by one vote in the Senate). | Unit 1 [TN]

K

Kennan, George (1904–2005) | U.S. diplomat who developed the strategy of "containment" — preventing Soviet communism from spreading without attacking the Soviet Union — in his 1946 "Long Telegram" and 1947 "X Article." | Unit 7

Kent State (May 4, 1970) | The killing of four students by Ohio National Guard soldiers at Kent State University during anti-Vietnam War protests. The event shocked the nation and intensified opposition to the war. | Unit 8

Keynesian economics | (KAYN-zee-un) | An economic theory that government should use deficit spending to stimulate demand during recessions. Named for British economist John Maynard Keynes. The New Deal used Keynesian logic; Reagan's supply-side approach rejected it. | Units 5, 9 [CR]

KKK (Ku Klux Klan) | A white supremacist organization founded in Pulaski, Tennessee in 1865. Used terrorist violence — beatings, lynchings, arson — to suppress Black political participation during Reconstruction and beyond. Had a national revival in the 1920s. | Units 1, 4 [TN] [BH]

Korematsu v. United States (1944) | (kor-ee-MAT-soo) | 323 U.S. 214; the Supreme Court's 6-3 ruling upholding the constitutionality of Japanese American internment. Justice Robert Jackson's dissent warned the decision would be "a loaded weapon" for future governments. Not repudiated until Trump v. Hawaii (2018). | Unit 6 [CR]

L

Laffer Curve | The economic concept — associated with Arthur Laffer — arguing that cutting high tax rates could increase government revenues by stimulating economic activity. The theoretical basis for Reagan's tax cuts. | Unit 9 [CR]

Lawson, James | see James Lawson [TN] [BH]

League of Nations | An international organization proposed by Woodrow Wilson in his Fourteen Points and created by the Treaty of Versailles (1919). The U.S. Senate refused to ratify the treaty, and the U.S. never joined. The League failed to prevent WWII. | Unit 4

Lend-Lease Act (1941) | The law allowing the U.S. to "lend, lease, or otherwise transfer" military equipment to any nation whose defense the president deemed vital to American security. Made the U.S. the "Arsenal of Democracy" before formally entering WWII. | Unit 6

Lewis, John | see John Lewis [TN] [BH]

lynching | The extrajudicial murder (often by hanging) of a person by a mob, most commonly used against Black Americans in the South during the Jim Crow era. Ida B. Wells of Memphis documented and fought against lynching. | Units 1, 3 [TN] [BH]

M

MacArthur, Douglas (1880–1964) | U.S. Army general who commanded forces in WWII's Pacific theater and the Korean War. Fired by President Truman in 1951 for publicly disagreeing with the limited war strategy, asserting the principle of civilian control of the military. | Units 6, 7

Manhattan Project | The secret U.S. government program (1942–1945) to develop the atomic bomb. Employed approximately 130,000 workers at sites including Oak Ridge, Tennessee; Los Alamos, New Mexico; and Hanford, Washington. | Units 6 [TN]

Marshall Plan (1948) | U.S. Secretary of State George Marshall's program of approximately $13.3 billion in economic aid to rebuild war-devastated Western Europe. The strategic logic: prosperous nations are less vulnerable to communist political appeals. | Unit 7

McCarthyism | (muh-KAR-thee-iz-um) | The practice of making accusations of communist sympathy without adequate evidence, named after Senator Joseph McCarthy. His tactics destroyed thousands of careers between 1950 and 1954. | Unit 7 [CR]

Medicare and Medicaid (1965) | Medicare provides health insurance for Americans over 65; Medicaid for low-income Americans. Created by LBJ's Great Society. Both still exist and cover approximately 140 million Americans today. | Unit 7 [CR]

Muckrakers | (MUK-ray-kerz) | Progressive Era journalists who exposed corruption and social problems through investigative reporting. Upton Sinclair (The Jungle), Ida Tarbell (Standard Oil), and Ida B. Wells (lynching) are key examples. | Unit 3 [BH]

My Lai Massacre (March 16, 1968) | The murder of between 347 and 504 Vietnamese civilians by U.S. Army troops under Lieutenant William Calley. Covered up for over a year; exposed by reporter Seymour Hersh. | Unit 8

N

NAACP (National Association for the Advancement of Colored People) | Civil rights organization founded in 1909 by W.E.B. Du Bois, Ida B. Wells, and others. Its legal arm, led by Thurgood Marshall, won Brown v. Board of Education in 1954. | Units 3, 7 [BH]

NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement) | A free trade agreement among the United States, Canada, and Mexico, taking effect January 1, 1994. In Tennessee, it benefited the automotive sector (Nissan, Saturn) while harming the textile industry. | Units 9, 10 [TN] [CR]

Nash, Diane | see Diane Nash [TN] [BH]

Nashville Sit-ins (1960) | The campaign of nonviolent lunch counter sit-ins in Nashville, Tennessee, organized by James Lawson and conducted by students from Fisk University, Tennessee A&I (now TSU), and American Baptist Theological Seminary beginning February 13, 1960. Nashville was the first major Southern city to desegregate its lunch counters. The Nashville veterans (Nash, Lewis, Lafayette, Vivian, Bevel) went on to lead the national Civil Rights Movement. | Unit 7 [TN] [BH]

nationalism | Strong identification with and support for one's nation, often accompanied by the belief that national interests should be prioritized above international cooperation. Extreme nationalism contributed to WWI and fascism. | Units 3, 4, 6

NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organization) | A military alliance among the Western democracies founded April 4, 1949, committing each member to treat an attack on any member as an attack on all. The institutional expression of containment. | Unit 7

New Deal | President Franklin Roosevelt's collection of programs, agencies, and reforms (1933–1938) in response to the Great Depression. Organized around the "Three Rs" (Relief, Recovery, Reform). Permanently expanded the federal government's role in the economy. | Unit 5 [CR]

Nisei | (NEE-say) | American-born children of Japanese immigrants (second generation). Approximately two-thirds of the 120,000 Japanese Americans interned during WWII were Nisei — U.S. citizens imprisoned without individual charges. | Unit 6

nonviolent direct action | A strategy of social protest using peaceful means — sit-ins, marches, boycotts — rather than violence. Developed from Gandhi's satyagraha; theorized by James Lawson; applied by SNCC and SCLC in the Civil Rights Movement. | Unit 7 [BH]

Norris Dam | The first TVA dam, completed in 1936 near Norris, Tennessee (approximately 25 miles north of Knoxville). Created Norris Lake; provided hydroelectric power and flood control. | Unit 5 [TN]

O

Oak Ridge, Tennessee | The "Secret City" built in Anderson County, Tennessee in 1942–1943 as the primary uranium production site for the Manhattan Project. At peak, approximately 75,000 workers enriched uranium at three facilities (Y-12, K-25, X-10). The uranium enriched at Oak Ridge was used in the bomb dropped on Hiroshima. Powered by TVA electricity. | Unit 6 [TN]

Obama, Barack (b. 1961) | 44th President (2009–2017). The first African American president of the United States. Key achievements: Affordable Care Act (2010); killing of Osama bin Laden (2011); Paris Climate Agreement (2015). His presidency revealed both the progress and the limits of racial equality in America. | Unit 10 [BH]

Open Door Policy | The U.S. policy (articulated 1899–1900) calling for equal trading rights for all nations in China and opposing the carving of China into exclusive spheres of influence by European powers. | Unit 3

OPEC oil embargo (1973–1974) | The Arab-dominated OPEC nations cut off oil exports to the U.S. and other nations supporting Israel in the Yom Kippur War. Oil prices quadrupled. Triggered the stagflation and energy crisis of the 1970s. | Unit 8

P

Panama Canal | A canal across the Isthmus of Panama connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. The U.S. built it after orchestrating Panamanian independence from Colombia (1903); completed 1914. Returned to Panamanian control in 1999. | Unit 3

PATRIOT Act, USA (2001) | Signed October 26, 2001, significantly expanding government surveillance authority in response to 9/11. A major civil liberties controversy; NSA programs conducted under its authority were far broader than Congress had understood. | Unit 10 [CR]
