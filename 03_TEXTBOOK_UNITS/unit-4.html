SECTION 4.1: THE GREAT WAR — WHY AMERICA WENT TO WAR

Section Learning Targets ("I Can" Statements): - I can explain the four main causes of World War I using the MAIN acronym and apply each cause to specific historical evidence. - I can analyze the reasons for U.S. neutrality and the pressures that eroded it, identifying the specific events that led to the declaration of war in April 1917. - I can evaluate the methods used to mobilize American society for war, including the limits those methods placed on civil liberties. - I can describe how World War I affected different groups of Americans on the home front.

Narrative Text

A World Stumbles Into War

In the summer of 1914, a Serbian nationalist named Gavrilo Princip shot and killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, and his wife Sophie on a street in Sarajevo, Bosnia-Herzegovina. Within six weeks, most of Europe was at war.

This is one of the most studied sequences of events in modern history, and historians still debate whether the war was inevitable or the product of specific miscalculations by specific leaders. What is clear is that the assassination did not cause World War I in any deep sense — it triggered a conflict that the structure of European politics had made catastrophically possible.

Historians commonly identify four structural causes of the war, organized by the acronym MAIN:

M — Militarism: The major European powers had been engaged in an arms race for decades, building larger armies and more powerful navies while their military leaders developed elaborate war plans. Germany's Schlieffen Plan (calling for a rapid attack through neutral Belgium to knock out France before turning east against Russia) and other national war plans made mobilization a hair-trigger process — once one country mobilized, all had to, because the plans assumed speed was everything. [VERIFY: Schlieffen Plan details are well-documented; cite a specific military history source, e.g., Barbara Tuchman, The Guns of August (1962)]

The massive military buildup also created enormous economic interests in maintaining it — arms manufacturers, military officers, and nationalist politicians who had staked their careers and fortunes on preparedness.

A — Alliance System: Europe in 1914 was divided into two armed camps through a network of mutual defense treaties: - The Triple Entente: France, Russia, and Great Britain (with various smaller allies) - The Triple Alliance (later called the Central Powers): Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy (though Italy ultimately stayed neutral, then joined the Entente in 1915) [VERIFY: Italy's switching sides — Italy declared neutrality in 1914, citing the defensive nature of the Triple Alliance; joined the Entente in 1915 under the Treaty of London; confirm details]

These alliances meant that a local conflict between Austria-Hungary and Serbia automatically pulled in Russia (which had treaty obligations to Serbia and fellow Slavic peoples), which pulled in Germany (Austria-Hungary's ally), which pulled in France (Russia's ally), which pulled in Britain (France's ally and outraged by Germany's violation of Belgian neutrality).

I — Imperialism: The European great powers had spent the previous century competing for colonies in Africa, Asia, and the Pacific. By 1914, most of the available territory had been claimed, and competition for the remaining scraps — and for spheres of influence in economically important regions — created continuous friction. Germany in particular resented that Britain and France controlled vast colonial empires while Germany had arrived late to the imperial competition.

N — Nationalism: Intense nationalist feeling — the belief that one's nation or ethnic group was superior and deserved political self-determination — was both a cause of the war and fuel that kept it burning. Serbian nationalism (the desire to create a greater Slavic state that would absorb Austria-Hungary's Slavic populations) was the immediate trigger. Pan-German nationalism, French revanchism over the loss of Alsace-Lorraine in 1871, Austro-Hungarian fears of Slavic nationalism within its empire — all these national passions transformed a diplomatic crisis into a catastrophic war.

The assassination at Sarajevo lit the fuse. The MAIN conditions were the bomb.

The War in Europe: A New Kind of Horror

World War I was unlike any previous war. Industrial technology had produced weapons that killed at scale and speed that no military doctrine had prepared for: - Machine guns that could cut down advancing infantry by the thousands - Artillery that could bombard positions for days without ceasing - Poison gas (first used by Germany at Ypres in April 1915, then by both sides) [VERIFY: first large-scale use of gas — second Battle of Ypres, April 22, 1915; Germany used chlorine gas; confirm date and location] - Tanks (introduced by Britain in 1916 at the Battle of the Somme) [VERIFY: tanks first used at the Battle of the Somme, September 15, 1916; confirm] - Aircraft (initially used for reconnaissance, later for aerial combat and bombing) - Submarines (German U-boats that threatened to starve Britain by blockading its shipping lanes)

The result was trench warfare — a static, grinding conflict in which armies dug elaborate systems of trenches facing each other across a "no man's land" of barbed wire and artillery craters. The Western Front ran approximately 400 miles from the North Sea to the Swiss border. [VERIFY: Western Front length — commonly cited as approximately 400 miles; confirm]

The casualty figures were staggering. The Battle of the Somme (July–November 1916) produced approximately 1 million casualties on both sides, including nearly 60,000 British casualties on the first day alone. [VERIFY: Battle of the Somme casualties — approximately 1 million total casualties is the widely cited figure; confirm; the first day figure of ~57,470 British casualties is the documented figure — confirm exact number] The Battle of Verdun (February–December 1916) produced approximately 700,000 casualties. [VERIFY: Verdun casualty figures — widely cited; confirm against historical sources]

These were not the clean, decisive engagements that European military planners had imagined. This was industrial slaughter on an unprecedented scale.

American Neutrality and Its Erosion

When war broke out in Europe in August 1914, the United States declared neutrality. President Wilson urged Americans to be "impartial in thought as well as in action." [VERIFY: Wilson's neutrality proclamation language — widely cited; confirm exact wording from official text at National Archives or American Presidency Project]

Genuine neutrality was, in practice, impossible for several reasons:

Economic ties: American businesses had enormous financial relationships with the Allied powers (Britain, France, Russia). J.P. Morgan's bank served as the financial agent for Allied purchases in the United States. By 1917, the U.S. had lent the Allies approximately $2 billion compared to only $27 million to the Central Powers. [VERIFY: these loan figures — widely cited; confirm against historical financial records] American manufacturers were supplying the Allies with food, munitions, and equipment. The U.S. economy had become deeply integrated with Allied war financing. A German victory would have threatened these investments.

Cultural and linguistic ties: Most Americans traced their heritage to Britain or other Allied nations and felt cultural affinities that made genuine neutrality psychologically difficult. German-American communities (significant in number) sympathized with the Central Powers, but they were outnumbered culturally.

British propaganda: Britain's control of the transatlantic telegraph cables allowed it to dominate the flow of news from Europe to America. German atrocities (some real, some exaggerated or fabricated) received extensive coverage; Allied misconduct did not. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES: British propaganda's role in shaping American opinion toward the Allies is a matter of historical discussion; cite specific scholarly sources rather than making sweeping claims]

Submarine warfare: Germany's decision to use unrestricted submarine warfare against all shipping in the war zone around Britain — including neutral American ships — put American lives and property directly at risk and made neutrality increasingly untenable.

The Road to War: Three Triggers

Three specific events shifted American public opinion decisively toward intervention:

The Sinking of the Lusitania (May 7, 1915): The British ocean liner Lusitania was sunk by a German U-boat off the coast of Ireland, killing 1,198 of the 1,959 people aboard — including 128 Americans. [VERIFY: casualty figures — 1,198 deaths is the widely cited figure; 128 Americans is the widely cited number; confirm against ship records; the Lusitania carried munitions as well as passengers — a fact Germany advertised in warnings before the ship sailed, and a fact that complicates the simple "Germany murdered civilians" narrative; MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — note the munitions cargo in teaching]

The sinking outraged Americans and produced demands for war. Wilson sent stern diplomatic protests to Germany. Germany temporarily suspended unrestricted submarine warfare after Wilson's warnings but eventually resumed it.

The Zimmermann Telegram (January 1917): In January 1917, British intelligence intercepted and decoded a telegram from German Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann to the German ambassador in Mexico. The telegram proposed that if the U.S. entered the war, Germany would support Mexico in recovering Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona from the United States. [VERIFY: Zimmermann Telegram — sent January 16, 1917; intercepted by British Naval Intelligence Room 40; text is public domain and widely reproduced; full text in Appendix C; the proposal to Mexico is accurately described]

When the British shared the decoded telegram with Wilson and it was published in American newspapers in March 1917, American public outrage — particularly in the Southwest — surged.

Resumption of Unrestricted Submarine Warfare (February 1917): Germany announced on February 1, 1917, that it would resume unrestricted submarine warfare, sinking any ship in the war zone without warning, regardless of nationality. Germany calculated that it could starve Britain into surrender before the United States could mobilize and deploy troops. It was a gamble that proved fatal to German ambitions.

On April 2, 1917, Wilson asked Congress for a declaration of war. He framed it not as a war of national self-interest but as a moral crusade: "The world must be made safe for democracy." [VERIFY: Wilson's April 2, 1917 war message — "the world must be made safe for democracy" is among the most famous phrases in American political history; full text of the war message is public domain; available at American Presidency Project; Miller Center, UVA; National Archives]

Congress declared war on April 6, 1917. The vote in the Senate was 82 to 6; in the House, 373 to 50. [VERIFY: these vote counts — widely cited; confirm against Congressional Record]

Mobilizing America: The Home Front in World War I

The United States had to transform from a nation at peace to a major military power with extraordinary speed. This mobilization process had profound consequences for American society — some expanding opportunity, others restricting freedom.

The Selective Service Act (May 1917): The U.S. Army numbered approximately 100,000 men when war was declared — wholly inadequate for a European land war. The Selective Service Act established a military draft for the first time since the Civil War, ultimately registering 24 million men and drafting nearly 3 million. [VERIFY: draft registration and induction figures — widely cited; confirm against Selective Service historical records]

Liberty Bonds: To finance the war (which cost approximately $32 billion by its end [VERIFY]), the Treasury Department sold Liberty Bonds to ordinary Americans. The bond campaigns used celebrity endorsements, patriotic rallies, and social pressure — anyone who did not buy bonds risked being labeled unpatriotic or pro-German.

The Committee on Public Information (CPI): Wilson created the CPI under journalist George Creel to manage public opinion and promote the war effort. The CPI produced thousands of posters, pamphlets, and films; organized 75,000 "Four Minute Men" who gave short patriotic speeches in movie theaters, churches, and civic meetings; and worked to suppress dissent. [VERIFY: "Four Minute Men" figure and CPI activities — well-documented; cite James Mock and Cedric Larson's Words That Won the War (1939) or more recent scholarship]

The Espionage Act (1917) and Sedition Act (1918): These laws made it illegal to obstruct the draft, encourage insubordination, or say or print "disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive" language about the government, flag, or military. They were used to imprison critics of the war — including Eugene V. Debs, who was sentenced to 10 years in federal prison for a speech opposing the draft. [VERIFY: Debs's conviction and sentence under the Espionage Act; he was convicted in September 1918 and sentenced to 10 years; Debs ran for president from prison in 1920 and received nearly 1 million votes; confirm details]

The Supreme Court upheld the Espionage Act in Schenck v. United States (1919), with Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes writing the famous opinion that free speech does not protect someone who "falsely shout[s] fire in a crowded theatre" — establishing the "clear and present danger" test for First Amendment limits. [VERIFY: Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47 (1919); Holmes's "fire in a crowded theatre" language is one of the most quoted phrases in constitutional law; confirm the full citation and that the specific language appears in Holmes's majority opinion; note: modern constitutional law has substantially modified this doctrine]

Anti-German Hysteria: The war produced a toxic surge of anti-German sentiment. German-Americans — who numbered in the millions and had been one of the most respected immigrant communities — faced harassment, violence, and forced demonstrations of loyalty. German-language newspapers were shut down; schools stopped teaching German; sauerkraut was renamed "liberty cabbage"; dachshunds were kicked on the street. [VERIFY: these examples of anti-German sentiment are well-documented; specific examples like "liberty cabbage" and "liberty sausage" are historically documented; cite appropriate historical source]

This episode is a useful lens on the question of American identity. The same country that prided itself on being a "nation of immigrants" turned ferociously against immigrants from enemy nations the moment national security was invoked.

African Americans and the War: Approximately 370,000 African Americans served in the U.S. military during World War I. They served in segregated units under white officers, were denied combat roles in most cases, and faced discrimination both in training and overseas. [VERIFY: 370,000 figure — widely cited; confirm against military historical records]

The most celebrated Black unit was the 369th Infantry Regiment — the "Harlem Hellfighters" — who fought under French command (because the U.S. Army refused to place them in American units) for 191 days, more than any other American unit. The French awarded the regiment the Croix de Guerre for its service. [VERIFY: 369th Infantry Regiment details — well-documented; confirm specific days and French military honor details against regimental history]

When Black soldiers returned home, they returned to a society that still denied them basic rights. The brutal irony — fighting for democracy abroad while denied it at home — was not lost on Black Americans or on W.E.B. Du Bois, who both encouraged Black participation ("Close Ranks" editorial, The Crisis, July 1918) and bitterly criticized the treatment of Black veterans after the war. [VERIFY: Du Bois's "Close Ranks" editorial, The Crisis, July 1918 — this is well-documented and controversial in Du Bois scholarship; confirm; it was followed by his equally famous "Returning Soldiers" editorial, May 1919, in which he reversed course and called for Black Americans to fight for their rights at home; both are public domain]

SECTION 4.2: THE PEACE THAT FAILED AND THE RED SCARE

Section Learning Targets: - I can explain Wilson's Fourteen Points and analyze how the Treaty of Versailles diverged from Wilson's vision. - I can evaluate the Senate's rejection of the Treaty of Versailles and explain both sides of the debate. - I can analyze the First Red Scare, including the Palmer Raids and the Schenck decision, as a case study in civil liberties under pressure. - I can explain the Great Migration — its causes, patterns, and significance.

Narrative Text

Wilson's Fourteen Points: A Vision for a New World[CAUSATION]

On January 8, 1918 — before the war had ended — President Wilson addressed Congress and outlined his vision for the peace settlement. His Fourteen Points represented the most ambitious attempt to remake the international order since the Congress of Vienna in 1815.

The Fourteen Points included: - Open covenants (no secret treaties — the secret alliance system had helped cause the war) - Freedom of the seas (neutral ships should be able to sail freely) - Self-determination — the peoples of Europe (and implicitly, the world) should have the right to form their own nations based on ethnic and national identity - A League of Nations — an international organization that would resolve disputes between nations through diplomacy rather than war — Wilson's most ambitious goal and the one he cared most deeply about - Specific territorial provisions for redrawing the map of Europe along national lines

Wilson traveled to Paris for the peace conference in January 1919 — the first sitting president to travel to Europe while in office — and was greeted by European crowds as a messiah. Millions of war-weary Europeans believed Wilson's idealistic vision would produce a just and lasting peace.

[VERIFY: Wilson's status as first sitting president to travel to Europe — this is a commonly cited fact; confirm against presidential historical records]

The Treaty of Versailles: A Peace Built on Punishment

The actual treaty that emerged from the Paris Peace Conference in June 1919 diverged significantly from Wilson's vision. Britain (Lloyd George), France (Clemenceau), and Italy (Orlando) each had their own national interests — revenge, reparations, territorial expansion — that overwhelmed Wilson's idealism.

The Treaty of Versailles contained several provisions that would prove catastrophic:

The War Guilt Clause (Article 231): Germany was required to accept sole responsibility for causing the war — the "war guilt" clause. This was historically questionable (the war had multiple causes and multiple responsible parties) and politically explosive. Germans across the political spectrum experienced it as a national humiliation. [VERIFY: Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles — "war guilt clause" — is extensively documented; full text of the treaty is public domain; available at Yale Law School Avalon Project and other archives]

Reparations: Germany was required to pay enormous reparations — the final bill was set at 132 billion gold marks (approximately $31.4 billion at the time, or hundreds of billions in contemporary terms). [VERIFY: the reparations figure and conversion — the London Schedule of Payments (May 1921) set total reparations at 132 billion gold marks; confirm conversion figures and cite appropriately]

These payments decimated the German economy, created hyperinflation in the early 1920s, and produced the economic desperation that the Nazi Party would exploit a decade later.

Territorial Provisions: Germany lost approximately 13% of its territory and 10% of its population to neighboring states. The Rhineland was demilitarized. German colonies were distributed among the Allied powers as "League of Nations mandates" — which in practice meant new colonial possessions. [VERIFY: territorial loss figures — commonly cited; confirm against treaty text and historical atlases]

The League of Nations: The League of Nations was established, but with a critical flaw: the United States never joined it. Wilson had been so focused on the European negotiations that he had failed to build bipartisan support at home, and he refused to compromise with Senate critics.

The Self-Determination Contradiction: Wilson's principle of self-determination was applied selectively — to European peoples whose independence served Allied interests, but not to colonial peoples in Asia, Africa, and the Middle East. A young Vietnamese nationalist named Ho Chi Minh presented a petition for Vietnamese independence at the Paris Peace Conference. It was ignored. [VERIFY: Ho Chi Minh's petition at Paris — this is documented; he was known as Nguyen Ai Quoc at the time; he petitioned the Paris Peace Conference in 1919; confirm specific details against historical sources; this is a significant "seed of future conflict" teaching moment connecting to Unit 8 Vietnam War]

The Senate Fight: Lodge vs. Wilson

The Treaty of Versailles required a two-thirds Senate vote for ratification. The Senate divided into three groups:

Supporters: Democrats who supported Wilson and the treaty as written

Reservationists: Led by Republican Senator Henry Cabot Lodge of Massachusetts, who supported U.S. participation in an international organization but wanted modifications — particularly to Article X of the League Covenant, which obligated member nations to defend each other's territorial integrity. Lodge argued this would give an international body the power to commit American troops to war without Congressional approval.

Irreconcilables: A smaller group (mostly Republicans but some Democrats) who opposed any U.S. participation in the League of Nations under any conditions, fearing "entangling alliances."

Wilson refused to accept any significant modifications to the treaty. In September 1919, against his doctor's advice, he embarked on a national speaking tour to build public pressure on the Senate — and collapsed after a speech in Pueblo, Colorado. Days later, he suffered a massive stroke that left him partially paralyzed. [VERIFY: Wilson's stroke — October 2, 1919; the precise medical details of Wilson's condition were hidden from Congress and the public for months; confirm specifics against historical medical and political sources]

For the remaining 17 months of his presidency, Wilson was largely incapacitated. His wife Edith Wilson managed access to the president and shielded the extent of his disability from Congress and the public — a constitutional crisis in slow motion.

The Senate voted on the treaty twice — November 1919 and March 1920 — and rejected it both times. The United States never joined the League of Nations.

Consequences: The League of Nations operated without its most powerful potential member, fatally weakening its ability to maintain international peace. When Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931 and Italy invaded Ethiopia in 1935, the League proved unable to respond effectively. When Hitler began rearming Germany and expanding its borders in the mid-1930s, the League was powerless. The chain of causation from the Senate's 1919 rejection to World War II is not direct or simple, but it is real and important. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES: historians debate how much the League's failure was caused by U.S. non-membership vs. other factors; present the complexity honestly]

The Great Migration [TCA § 49-6-1006][CCOT]

While the United States was fighting in Europe, a massive demographic transformation was reshaping American society at home. The Great Migration — the movement of approximately 1.6 million Black Americans from the rural South to urban Northern and Midwestern cities between roughly 1910 and 1930 (with a second, larger wave from 1940 to 1970) — was one of the most consequential population movements in American history. [VERIFY: Great Migration figures — the first wave (roughly 1910–1930) is estimated at 1.6 million; verify against historical demographic data, e.g., Isabel Wilkerson's The Warmth of Other Suns (2010) or U.S. Census Bureau historical data]

Why They Left — Push Factors: - Jim Crow laws, disenfranchisement, and pervasive racial terror in the South - The boll weevil devastated cotton crops across the South starting around 1915, destroying the sharecropping economy [VERIFY: boll weevil's westward spread across the cotton South approximately 1915–1920; confirm timing] - Lynching and racial violence — the threat of murder for any perceived transgression - Economic poverty with no legal or political recourse

Why They Went North — Pull Factors: - Industrial jobs opened up as WWI cut off the flow of European immigration and factories needed workers - Relatively higher wages in Northern industry compared to Southern sharecropping - The Chicago Defender (the most widely read Black newspaper in America) actively recruited Southern Black readers to come North, publishing timetables, job listings, and letters from successful migrants [VERIFY: the Chicago Defender's role in the Great Migration is well-documented; cite appropriate source] - The perception — partially accurate, often not — of greater freedom and dignity in the North

Where They Went: Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, Pittsburgh, New York (particularly Harlem), and other Northern and Midwestern industrial cities. These destinations were largely determined by the railroads available from specific Southern departure points — Mississippians disproportionately went to Chicago (Illinois Central Railroad); Southerners from the Carolinas went to New York. [VERIFY: railroad routing patterns in migration is documented by Isabel Wilkerson and other historians]

Tennessee Connection: Memphis and Nashville were significant points of departure. Tennessee-born migrants moved to Chicago, Detroit, and Cleveland in significant numbers. The Mississippi and Illinois Central railroads connected Memphis directly to Chicago, making that corridor one of the most heavily traveled migration routes. Memphis's Beale Street music scene simultaneously exported musicians northward, bringing blues and jazz to Chicago and beyond.

What They Found: The North was not the promised land. Northern cities had their own racial hierarchies: restrictive housing covenants (legal agreements among white homeowners not to sell to Black buyers) confined Black migrants to overcrowded, overpriced neighborhoods; labor unions often refused Black membership; race riots (East St. Louis 1917, Chicago 1919, Tulsa 1921) killed hundreds of Black Americans. [VERIFY: race riot locations and dates — East St. Louis, July 1917; Chicago, July 1919; Tulsa, May 31–June 1, 1921; these are well-documented; Tulsa is now often called the Tulsa Race Massacre]

But migrants also built communities, institutions, churches, newspapers, and political organizations that did not exist in the South. They organized, voted (in the North), built Black political power, and created the cultural foundation for the Harlem Renaissance.

The First Red Scare: Democracy Under Pressure

In the immediate aftermath of the war, a new fear gripped the United States: the fear of communism. The Bolshevik Revolution of October 1917 had toppled the Russian Tsar and established the world's first communist state. American business leaders and politicians looked at their own labor unrest — there were more than 3,600 strikes in 1919 alone [VERIFY: this figure is widely cited; confirm against Bureau of Labor Statistics historical data] — and saw the specter of revolution.

The Palmer Raids: Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer authorized a series of mass arrests of suspected radicals, socialists, and communists — the "Palmer Raids" of 1919–1920. In one night alone (January 2, 1920), federal agents arrested approximately 10,000 people in 33 cities. [VERIFY: the January 2, 1920 raids figure — widely cited; confirm against Department of Justice historical records or scholarly sources such as Beverly Gage's The Day Wall Street Exploded (2009) or Paul Avrich's work]

Most of those arrested were immigrants. Hundreds were deported, including the prominent anarchist Emma Goldman, without trial or due process. The raids were carried out without warrants in many cases, targeting people for their political beliefs rather than for any specific criminal acts.

A young J. Edgar Hoover — who would go on to head the FBI for nearly 50 years — organized the raids as the head of the Justice Department's Radical Division. [VERIFY: Hoover's role in the Palmer Raids — well-documented; confirm his title and specific role]

The Red Scare eventually subsided when Palmer's predictions of a communist revolution on May 1, 1920, failed to materialize. But its legacy was significant: it established a template for government suppression of political dissent that would recur in the 1950s McCarthyism (Unit 7), and it demonstrated how quickly civil liberties could erode under the pressure of national security fear.

SECTION 4.3: THE ROARING TWENTIES — PROSPERITY, CULTURE, AND CONFLICT

Section Learning Targets: - I can explain the economic prosperity of the 1920s and identify its structural weaknesses. - I can describe the Harlem Renaissance and explain its cultural and political significance. - I can analyze the Scopes Trial as a case study in the cultural tensions of the 1920s. - I can explain the social and cultural conflicts of the 1920s, including Prohibition, nativism, and the KKK revival. - I can connect the economic conditions of the late 1920s to the Great Depression that followed.

Narrative Text

The Roaring Economy: Consumer Capitalism Takes Off

The 1920s were, for many Americans, a time of genuine prosperity. After the disruptions of the war, the American economy experienced a sustained expansion driven by new consumer industries and the mass adoption of technologies that had been emerging since the turn of the century.

New Consumer Industries: - Automobiles: Henry Ford's assembly line had brought car prices down to a level working-class families could aspire to. By 1929, there was approximately one car for every five Americans. The automobile industry drove demand for steel, glass, rubber, oil, and the construction of roads — the economic multiplier effect was enormous. [VERIFY: automobile ownership statistics for 1929; widely cited figures vary slightly; confirm against census or industry data] - Electricity and consumer appliances: Electric refrigerators, washing machines, radios, and vacuum cleaners transformed household labor and created new consumer markets. - Radio and mass media: KDKA in Pittsburgh broadcast the first commercial radio program in 1920. By 1929, millions of American homes had radios. [VERIFY: KDKA — confirmed as the first licensed commercial radio station; first broadcast November 2, 1920; widely documented] - Movies: The film industry boomed with the nickelodeon era giving way to grand movie palaces and the rise of Hollywood. The first sound film, The Jazz Singer, was released in 1927. [VERIFY: The Jazz Singer, 1927 — first commercially successful sound film; Al Jolson; October 6, 1927; confirm]

Installment Buying: A crucial driver of 1920s prosperity was the expansion of consumer credit — particularly installment buying, which allowed consumers to purchase expensive items (cars, appliances, furniture) by making a small down payment and paying the remainder in monthly installments.

This was a genuine economic innovation that brought consumer goods within reach of millions of Americans who could not have afforded them outright. It also meant that millions of Americans were buying goods with borrowed money — building up personal debt that made the economy vulnerable to any shock that reduced incomes or consumer confidence.

The Stock Market Boom: Stock prices rose dramatically throughout the 1920s, driven by genuine corporate profitability, speculative investment, and the widespread use of buying on margin — purchasing stocks with a small down payment and borrowing the remainder from the broker. If the stock went up, the investor pocketed the gain and repaid the loan. If the stock went down, the investor might owe more than the stock was worth.

By 1929, stock prices were dramatically disconnected from the actual earnings of the companies they represented. The market was a speculative bubble inflated by borrowed money and optimistic expectations.

The Scopes Trial: God vs. Darwin in Dayton, Tennessee[CONTEXTUALIZATION]

[TENNESSEE REQUIRED CONTENT]

In the summer of 1925, a small courthouse in Dayton, Rhea County, Tennessee became the center of the world's attention. The Scopes Trial — formally State of Tennessee v. John Thomas Scopes — was, on its surface, a case about whether a high school biology teacher had violated the Butler Act, a Tennessee state law that prohibited teaching "any theory that denies the Story of the Divine Creation of man as taught in the Bible, and to teach instead that man has descended from a lower order of animals." [VERIFY: the Butler Act — passed by the Tennessee General Assembly in March 1925; signed by Governor Austin Peay; text is public domain; available through Tennessee State Library and Archives and other archives]

Beneath the surface, it was about much more.

The Context: The Butler Act was not an isolated piece of legislation. It was part of a broader reaction — particularly in rural, Southern Protestant communities — against the forces of cultural modernism: Darwin's theory of evolution, higher Biblical criticism (academic analysis of the Bible as a historical text), urbanization, immigration, and the cultural upheaval of the 1920s. Fundamentalism — the belief in the literal truth of the Bible — was a direct response to these modernist threats.

The Setup: The ACLU (American Civil Liberties Union) had publicly offered to defend any teacher willing to test the Butler Act. Dayton civic leaders — hoping the resulting publicity would put their small town on the map — recruited 24-year-old substitute teacher John Scopes (who may not have actually taught evolution from the textbook in question, though the charge was never seriously disputed) to be arrested and tried. [VERIFY: Scopes's actual status as substitute teacher and the question of whether he actually taught evolution are discussed in historical accounts; Edward Larson's Summer for the Gods (1997) is the definitive scholarly account; cite]

The trial became a national sensation when two celebrity lawyers agreed to argue it: William Jennings Bryan (three-time Democratic presidential candidate, former Secretary of State, and passionate fundamentalist Christian) for the prosecution, and Clarence Darrow (the most famous defense attorney in America, an agnostic and champion of civil liberties) for the defense. The world's press descended on Dayton.

The Trial: The trial turned bizarre when Darrow called Bryan himself as a witness and cross-examined him on his literal interpretation of the Bible. Did Bryan believe Jonah was swallowed by a whale? That Joshua made the sun stand still? That the world was created in exactly six days? Bryan, committed to Biblical literalism, answered yes — and in doing so, appeared (to the urban press, if not to his fundamentalist supporters) to make himself look foolish.

H.L. Mencken of the Baltimore Sun — who wrote some of the most famous dispatches from the trial — described Bryan and his followers with contemptuous mockery. [VERIFY: Mencken's dispatches from the Scopes Trial, Baltimore Sun, July 1925 — well-documented; Mencken's writings from Dayton are widely available; his tone was aggressively contemptuous of fundamentalism; present this honestly — Mencken was brilliant but also elitist and antisemitic, and his coverage was not objective journalism]

Scopes was convicted and fined $100, which was later overturned on a technicality. Bryan died five days after the trial ended. Darrow had won the cultural battle in the eyes of the Northern press. But the Butler Act remained on the books in Tennessee until 1967. [VERIFY: Butler Act repealed in 1967 by the Tennessee General Assembly — confirm date and legislative action]

What the Scopes Trial Really Means: The Scopes Trial is often taught as a clash between science and religion — and it was that. But it was also: - A clash between urban and rural America, modernism and tradition - A clash about who has the authority to set educational standards — parents and communities, or state and national experts - A clash about the role of science in democratic society - A Tennessee-specific event with national resonance that illuminates the cultural tensions running through the entire decade

[MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES: The Scopes Trial should be presented with respect for multiple perspectives. The fundamentalist position was held by sincere, serious people who believed — and believe — that Biblical teaching is more important than scientific theory. The Darrow/modernist position reflected genuine scientific consensus and genuine civil liberties principles. Students should understand both positions, the specific legal question at stake (could a state prohibit the teaching of evolution?), and the cultural context that made this a national sensation. Do not mock fundamentalism; do not misrepresent evolutionary science.]

The Cultural Conflicts of the 1920s: Prohibition, Nativism, and the KKK[COMPARISON]

The Roaring Twenties were not roaring for everyone. While Harlem celebrated jazz and New York's speakeasies served bootleg gin, rural and small-town America was experiencing a different 1920s — one defined by anxiety about the changes threatening traditional culture and values.

Prohibition: The 18th Amendment (ratified 1919, Unit 3) went into effect in January 1920. The Volstead Act established the enforcement mechanism. The experiment failed — spectacularly.

Prohibition drove alcohol production and sales underground, creating the criminal infrastructure that organized crime would occupy for decades. Al Capone's Chicago organization, controlling bootlegging, gambling, and other illegal activities, grossed an estimated $60 million per year at its peak — more than many legitimate businesses. [VERIFY: Capone's revenue estimate — widely cited; confirm against historical sources; note this figure is an estimate]

Enforcement was selective and corrupt. Police and federal agents were routinely bribed. In cities with large immigrant populations (Italian, German, Irish, Eastern European) who had no cultural prohibition against alcohol, enforcement was largely futile. In rural, Protestant areas, compliance was higher.

Prohibition ultimately weakened respect for law by making millions of ordinary, law-abiding citizens into criminals by definition. It was repealed by the 21st Amendment in December 1933.

Nativism and the Immigration Act of 1924: The nativist anxieties that had produced the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882, Unit 2) returned with greater force in the 1920s. The "new immigration" from Southern and Eastern Europe was seen by nativists as threatening to the racial and cultural character of the United States.

The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 and the Immigration Act of 1924 (the National Origins Act) imposed the first comprehensive immigration restriction in American history: - Total immigration was capped at 150,000 per year [VERIFY: 1924 Act total cap — commonly cited as 165,000 or 150,000; confirm against the actual act text] - Each country received a quota proportional to its representation in the 1890 census — a formula specifically designed to favor Northwestern European countries and exclude Southern and Eastern Europeans (and exclude Asians entirely) - The effect on Jewish immigration from Eastern Europe — where pogroms continued — was devastating; the act effectively barred the kind of refugee migration that had brought millions of Jews to safety in America

The 1924 Act remained U.S. immigration law until the Immigration Act of 1965 (Unit 7), which abolished the national origins quota system.

[MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES: The 1924 Act is today widely considered discriminatory and historically shameful. Present the arguments made by its supporters (labor competition, racial "quality" of the nation — explicitly stated in the congressional debate, including by academic eugenicists) accurately while making clear these were racial prejudice dressed in pseudo-scientific language. The eugenics connection is historically significant and should be addressed.]

The Ku Klux Klan's Revival: The Ku Klux Klan of the 1920s was not the Reconstruction-era organization of former Confederate guerrillas (Unit 1). It was a new phenomenon: a mass movement of predominantly Northern, Midwestern, and Western small-town and urban white Protestants who defined Americanism in terms of whiteness, Protestantism, and nativism.

At its peak in the early-to-mid 1920s, the Klan claimed between 3 and 6 million members. [VERIFY: Klan membership estimates for the 1920s — vary widely from 3 to 6 million; cite a specific scholarly source, e.g., David Chalmers's Hooded Americanism or Kenneth Jackson's The Ku Klux Klan in the City] Its targets were not only Black Americans but also Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and people perceived as violating traditional Protestant moral codes.

Socratic Seminar — Unit 4

Preparation: Students read Wilson's War Message excerpt (Primary Source 4.1) and the Zimmermann Telegram (Primary Source 4.2) from Appendix C.

Central Question: "Wilson argued the U.S. entered WWI to 'make the world safe for democracy.' Looking at what actually happened — the Espionage Act, the treatment of Black veterans, the Red Scare, the Scopes Trial, the Klan's revival — did the war expand American democracy or did it reveal its limits? Use evidence from the primary sources and the unit narrative to make your argument."

Opening (text-grounded): 1. What specific democratic promises does Wilson make in the War Message? Are these promises about other countries, about America, or both? 2. The Zimmermann Telegram proposed that Germany give U.S. territory to Mexico. How does Germany's willingness to make this proposal reflect its assessment of American power and resolve before April 1917?

Middle rounds (analytical): 3. The Espionage Act imprisoned people for opposing the war that Wilson said was about democracy. How do you evaluate this? Was wartime suppression of dissent necessary, or did it undermine the democratic values Wilson claimed to be fighting for? 4. Black Americans served in WWI and came home to the Red Summer. How does this fact complicate Wilson's claim that the war was about democracy? 5. The Scopes Trial happened 7 years after the war. Is there a connection between WWI's aftermath (disillusionment, economic anxiety, cultural displacement) and the cultural conflicts of the 1920s? What's the causal story?

Closing: 6. The unit's essential question asks who gets to define American identity. Based on everything we've discussed, who did define American identity in this period? Was it the Harlem Renaissance, the Klan, the fundamentalists, the immigrants, the veterans? Can multiple competing definitions coexist?

Structured Academic Controversy (SAC) — Unit 4

Topic: Was the Senate right to reject the Treaty of Versailles and the League of Nations?

Position A: The Senate Was Right - Article X of the League Covenant would require Congress to commit troops without a congressional vote — a violation of separation of powers - The U.S. should not be entangled in European balance-of-power politics that were none of its business - The League without meaningful enforcement mechanisms would be ineffective regardless of U.S. membership - Sovereignty matters — no international body should have authority over American military decisions

Position B: The Senate Was Wrong - The League's weakness without U.S. participation was self-fulfilling — U.S. membership would have given it the credibility and power to act - Wilson's stubbornness drove the rejection; Lodge's reservations were largely reasonable modifications, not fundamental objections - The consequences of rejection — a League unable to stop Japan, Italy, and Germany in the 1930s — suggest the Senate's decision contributed to WWII - Isolationism was a comfortable luxury before WWI but untenable in a world where industrial warfare could kill millions in months

Consensus question: "Whether the Senate was right or wrong, could Wilson have made a different choice that might have produced a better outcome? What does this episode tell us about the relationship between presidential leadership, Senate authority, and foreign policy in American democracy?"

Critical Content Notes

The Scopes Trial — Required Tennessee Content: The Scopes Trial must receive substantial instructional time in Tennessee classrooms. It is one of the most significant events in Tennessee history and one of the most nationally resonant. Students should know: Dayton, Rhea County, Tennessee; Butler Act (1925); Bryan vs. Darrow; the deeper cultural tensions (urban vs. rural, science vs. religion, state authority vs. academic freedom). The TSLA holds extraordinary primary source materials — obtain reproductions for classroom use. If at all possible, arrange a field trip to Dayton, where the Bryan College and the Rhea County Courthouse (now a museum) preserve the site of the trial. [VERIFY: Rhea County Courthouse museum — confirm current status and educational programming]

Presenting the Scopes Trial without dismissing fundamentalism: The temptation — reinforced by Mencken's brilliant contemptuous dispatches and the film Inherit the Wind (which is based on the trial but is NOT an accurate historical document) — is to present the Scopes Trial as reason defeating ignorance. Resist this. Fundamentalism was and is a sincere, serious religious position held by intelligent people. The legal question at issue — can a democratically elected state legislature prohibit the teaching of scientific content in state-funded schools? — is a genuine constitutional question. Present both positions with respect and precision. [NOTE: Inherit the Wind (1955 play, 1960 film) is a fictionalized version that takes significant liberties with historical events; if you show or reference it, explicitly acknowledge it is NOT an accurate historical document and discuss how and why it changed the history]

The Harlem Renaissance and music as primary source: Do not teach the Harlem Renaissance only from the perspective of literature and visual art. Jazz and blues ARE primary sources — they express the historical experience of Black Americans as vividly as any written document. Play recordings in class. W.C. Handy's "Memphis Blues" (1912) and "St. Louis Blues" (1914) have a direct Tennessee connection through Beale Street. The sound of the Harlem Renaissance is available on YouTube and streaming platforms — use it.

The Great Migration as a Tennessee story: Memphis is a departure point for the Great Migration via the Illinois Central Railroad to Chicago. The blues traveled the same route as the migrants. This is a Tennessee- specific dimension of a national story that makes the abstract concrete for Tennessee students. Use the geographic connection to help students feel the historical weight of this migration.

WWI civil liberties — connecting to the present: The Espionage Act of 1917 is still in effect today and has been used to prosecute government whistleblowers in the 21st century. This is not a digression — it is evidence of the long reach of wartime legislation and a powerful way to connect Unit 4 to Unit 10. [VERIFY: the Espionage Act's contemporary use — well-documented; Ellsberg, Manning, Snowden cases — VERIFY specific cases before citing; this is a meaningful cross-unit connection for civically engaged students]

TCA § 49-6-1028(b) Connection: The Scopes Trial directly engages the relationship between democratic governance and individual rights — a constitutional republic question. The 1920s immigration restriction debate directly engages the question of who is included in the "constitutional republic." These connections should be made explicit.

UNIT 4 GLOSSARY ENTRIES

(For Appendix A — Alphabetical Glossary)

armistice | (ARM-ih-stis) | A formal agreement between opposing sides to stop fighting; a ceasefire. World War I ended with an armistice on November 11, 1918, at 11:00 AM — "the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month." | Unit 4, Section 4.1

buying on margin | A method of purchasing stocks by putting down only a fraction of the purchase price and borrowing the rest from a stockbroker. If the stock's price rose, the investor profited; if it fell, the investor could lose more than their original investment and still owe the broker money. Widespread margin buying helped create the speculative bubble of the late 1920s. | Unit 4, Section 4.3

Committee on Public Information (CPI) | A federal agency created by President Wilson in 1917 to manage public opinion and build support for U.S. participation in World War I. It produced propaganda posters, pamphlets, and films; organized 75,000 "Four Minute Men" to give patriotic speeches; and helped create a climate in which criticism of the war was seen as unpatriotic or treasonous. | Unit 4, Section 4.1

Espionage Act (1917) / Sedition Act (1918) | Federal laws passed during WWI that made it illegal to obstruct the draft, encourage military insubordination, or make "disloyal" or "abusive" statements about the government or military. They were used to imprison critics of the war, including Eugene V. Debs, who was sentenced to ten years in prison for a speech opposing the draft. | Unit 4, Section 4.1

Fourteen Points | President Wilson's January 1918 statement of war aims and vision for the post-war peace, including open covenants, freedom of the seas, self-determination for peoples, and a League of Nations. The Treaty of Versailles deviated significantly from the Fourteen Points, particularly in its punitive treatment of Germany. | Unit 4, Section 4.2

fundamentalism | (fun-duh-MEN-tuh-liz-um) | A religious movement emphasizing the literal truth of sacred texts (particularly the Bible) and rejecting modernist interpretations. In the 1920s, Protestant fundamentalism opposed the teaching of evolution and became associated with the Scopes Trial in Dayton, Tennessee. | Unit 4, Section 4.3 [TENNESSEE CONTENT]

Great Migration | The movement of approximately 1.6 million African Americans from the rural South to urban Northern and Midwestern cities roughly between 1910 and 1930 (with a second, larger wave from 1940 to 1970). Driven by Jim Crow terror, the boll weevil's destruction of the cotton economy, and the pull of industrial jobs, the Great Migration fundamentally transformed the geography of Black America. Memphis, Tennessee was a major departure point for migrants heading to Chicago. | Unit 4, Section 4.2 [TENNESSEE CONTENT] [TCA § 49-6-1006]

Harlem Renaissance | (HAHR-lem REN-uh-sahns) | A flowering of African American literature, music, visual art, and intellectual life centered in Harlem, New York, during the 1920s. Key figures include Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Claude McKay, Duke Ellington, and Louis Armstrong. The movement challenged white America's dehumanization of Black people and created a new cultural identity for Black Americans. The Memphis blues tradition, associated with W.C. Handy and Beale Street, contributed to its musical foundations. | Unit 4, Section 4.3 [TCA § 49-6-1006]

Harlem Hellfighters (369th Infantry Regiment) | The African American infantry regiment that fought under French command in World War I because the U.S. Army refused to integrate its units. The regiment fought for 191 days — more than any other American unit — and was awarded the French Croix de Guerre. Their service exemplified the "Double V" spirit — fighting for democracy abroad while denied it at home. | Unit 4, Section 4.1 [TCA § 49-6-1006]

installment buying | A method of purchasing consumer goods by making a small down payment and paying the remainder in regular monthly payments. The widespread adoption of installment buying in the 1920s fueled consumer spending but also built up household debt that made the economy vulnerable to recession. | Unit 4, Section 4.3

isolationism | (eye-SOH-luh-shun-iz-um) | A foreign policy stance favoring non-involvement in other countries' conflicts and avoiding military alliances. After WWI, many Americans supported isolationism and the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles and League of Nations. Isolationism remained a powerful force in U.S. foreign policy until the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941 (Unit 6). | Unit 4, Section 4.2

League of Nations | The international organization proposed by Wilson in his Fourteen Points and established by the Treaty of Versailles (1919) to resolve international disputes through diplomacy rather than war. The United States never joined because the Senate rejected the treaty. The League proved ineffective without U.S. membership and failed to prevent the events that led to World War II. | Unit 4, Section 4.2

Lusitania | (loo-sih-TAY-nee-uh) | A British ocean liner sunk by a German U-boat on May 7, 1915, off the coast of Ireland. 1,198 people were killed, including 128 Americans. The sinking outraged American public opinion and was one of the factors that eventually drove U.S. entry into WWI. | Unit 4, Section 4.1

Palmer Raids | A series of mass arrests of suspected radicals, anarchists, and communists authorized by Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer in 1919–1920. In a single night in January 1920, approximately 10,000 people were arrested in 33 cities. Most were immigrants; many were detained without warrants or deported without due process. The raids became a symbol of civil liberties violations during the First Red Scare. | Unit 4, Section 4.2

reparations | (rep-uh-RAY-shunz) | Payments required from Germany by the Treaty of Versailles (1919) as compensation for the damage caused by WWI. The final bill was set at 132 billion gold marks — an enormous sum that contributed to German economic instability, hyperinflation, and the political desperation that the Nazi Party would later exploit. | Unit 4, Section 4.2

Red Scare (First) | The wave of anti-communist and anti-radical fear that swept the United States in 1919–1920 in the aftermath of the Bolshevik Revolution and wartime labor unrest. It produced the Palmer Raids, mass deportations, and a chilling effect on labor organizing and political dissent. A Second Red Scare occurred in the early 1950s (Unit 7). | Unit 4, Section 4.2

Schenck v. United States (1919) | (SHENK) | A Supreme Court case upholding the Espionage Act's constitutionality. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote for the majority that free speech does not protect someone who "falsely shouts fire in a crowded theatre," establishing the "clear and present danger" test for First Amendment limits. The decision allowed the imprisonment of war critics, including Eugene V. Debs. | Unit 4, Section 4.2

Scopes Trial (1925) | Formally State of Tennessee v. John Thomas Scopes, a trial in Dayton, Rhea County, Tennessee in July 1925 in which teacher John Scopes was charged with violating the Butler Act by teaching evolution. William Jennings Bryan argued for the prosecution; Clarence Darrow argued for the defense. Scopes was convicted (and later acquitted on a technicality). The trial became a national symbol of the cultural conflict between modernism and fundamentalism, urban and rural America, science and religion. The Butler Act remained Tennessee law until 1967. | Unit 4, Section 4.3 [TENNESSEE REQUIRED CONTENT]

Selective Service Act (1917) | The law that authorized a military draft for WWI, ultimately registering 24 million men and drafting nearly 3 million. It was the first national draft since the Civil War and demonstrated the federal government's capacity to mobilize citizens for a major military effort. | Unit 4, Section 4.1

Treaty of Versailles (1919) | (vur-SY) | The peace treaty that formally ended WWI, signed on June 28, 1919. It required Germany to accept sole responsibility for the war (the War Guilt Clause), pay enormous reparations, surrender territory, and accept military restrictions. It also established the League of Nations. The U.S. Senate rejected the treaty, and the U.S. never joined the League of Nations. | Unit 4, Section 4.2

trench warfare | A form of combat characterized by elaborate systems of trenches facing each other across "no man's land." WWI's Western Front was defined by trench warfare for most of its duration. The combination of machine guns, barbed wire, and artillery made attacking across no man's land extraordinarily costly, producing the grinding attrition that killed millions. | Unit 4, Section 4.1

war guilt clause | Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, which required Germany to accept sole legal and moral responsibility for World War I. The clause was used to justify reparations payments and was widely experienced by Germans as a national humiliation. Historians generally consider it historically inaccurate — the war had multiple causes and multiple responsible parties. | Unit 4, Section 4.2

Zimmermann Telegram | A January 1917 telegram from German Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann to the German ambassador in Mexico, proposing a German-Mexican alliance against the United States in exchange for German support of Mexico's effort to "reconquer" Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. Intercepted by British intelligence and published in American newspapers in March 1917, it outraged Americans and contributed to U.S. entry into WWI. | Unit 4, Section 4.1; Primary Source Spotlight 4.2
