Q2 Model Answer: Key areas of conflict between Johnson and Radical Republicans: (1) Speed and terms of readmission — Johnson wanted rapid restoration; Radicals wanted structural change first. (2) Treatment of former Confederates — Johnson pardoned nearly all; Radicals wanted disqualification from office. (3) Rights of freedpeople — Johnson opposed Black suffrage and federal protection of Black rights; Radicals believed these were essential. (4) Constitutional authority — Johnson believed Reconstruction was an executive function; Congress believed it held this power. Students should identify at least three with supporting detail. Award 1 point per accurate area identified (3 points maximum), plus 1 point for supporting detail per area.

Q3 Model Answer: Black Codes were laws passed by Southern states in 1865–1866 to restrict the rights of formerly enslaved people and maintain white economic and social control. Acceptable specific provisions: vagrancy laws (criminalized unemployment, forced labor contracts), labor contracts (bound workers to employers for a year), restrictions on Black testimony in court, prohibition on owning firearms, apprenticeship laws for Black children, restrictions on land rental and skilled trades. Students must accurately describe the provision AND explain its controlling function to receive full credit.

Q4 Model Answer: The statement is partially accurate but incomplete and misleading. The 13th Amendment did end the legal institution of slavery. However: (1) It contained an exception allowing forced labor as criminal punishment, exploited through convict leasing. (2) Black Codes immediately replaced slavery with near-equivalent economic and legal control. (3) The failure of land redistribution left freedpeople economically dependent on former enslavers. (4) Racial terror and the eventual end of Reconstruction nullified much of what formal freedom promised. Students must provide at least two pieces of evidence for full credit.

Q5 Model Answer: The Freedmen's Bureau was a federal agency established in 1865 to help formerly enslaved people transition to freedom — providing food, clothing, medical care, legal assistance, and education. Its most significant failure was land redistribution: despite General Sherman's Special Field Order No. 15 promising "40 acres and a mule," President Johnson ordered confiscated lands returned to Confederate owners. Without land, freedpeople could not achieve economic independence and were forced into sharecropping. This failure was decisive because, as Thaddeus Stevens argued, political rights without economic resources were insufficient to produce genuine freedom.

Q6 Model Answer: This claim is unsupported by the evidence as a full explanation. While Radical Republicans did implement ambitious policies, the evidence suggests Reconstruction failed due to: insufficient federal commitment (troops were limited and eventually withdrawn), organized violent resistance (KKK), Johnson's active opposition and vetoes, the Compromise of 1877, and Northern political fatigue after the Panic of 1873. Accept arguments that acknowledge some limitations in Radical policies while identifying other causal factors. Award full credit only if student cites at least two specific pieces of evidence and constructs a logical counter-argument.

Q7 Model Answer: The 14th Amendment's protections were nullified in practice through: the withdrawal of federal troops after the Compromise of 1877, which removed the enforcement mechanism; the Supreme Court's narrow interpretation of the amendment in cases like United States v. Cruikshank (1876) [VERIFY citation], which ruled the federal government could not prosecute private individuals for civil rights violations; and the rise of poll taxes, literacy tests, and other disenfranchisement tools that technically complied with the amendment's language while violating its intent. This illustrates that constitutional rights require political will and enforcement infrastructure to be meaningful. Strong answers will identify the gap between constitutional text and practical enforcement.

Q8 Model Answer: Both perspectives contain valid elements. Accept any well-argued response that (a) accurately represents both positions, (b) clearly states a position, (c) concedes the strongest opposing point, and (d) provides at least two pieces of evidence. Strong answers will note that sharecropping did represent an improvement over gang-labor slavery in terms of family autonomy, while still arguing that the structural conditions — debt peonage, vagrancy laws, lack of land — made meaningful economic freedom essentially impossible for the majority of freedpeople.

Q9 Model Answer: The KKK targeted Black voters, officeholders, and white Republican allies in a systematic effort to restore Democratic Party control of Southern politics. The logic was straightforward: if Black voters could be terrorized away from the polls, Republican candidates would lose. Examples: KKK raids intensifying before elections; assassination of Black political leaders; burning of Black churches used for political organizing. The strategy succeeded: in areas of intense Klan activity, Republican electoral margins collapsed. Federal response under the Enforcement Acts temporarily suppressed the Klan but could not be sustained. Full credit requires identification of the political-strategic intent and at least two specific examples.

Q10 Model Answer: Students should identify what was revolutionary (first time Black men held political office, constitutional amendments establishing citizenship and equal protection, public school systems, multiracial democracy in practice) and why it was "unfinished" (land redistribution failed, constitutional rights were nullified through terror and legal manipulation, the Compromise of 1877 abandoned freedpeople, Jim Crow replaced slavery as a system of racial control). Three specific evidence items required for full credit.

CHAPTER 1 REVIEW: SPACED RETRIEVAL PRACTICE

Three-Round Review System

(Based on cognitive science research — three spaced retrieval opportunities per key concept significantly reduce forgetting; see Bibliography — Agarwal et al., 2014)

RETRIEVAL ROUND 1: IMMEDIATE REVIEW[CHRONOLOGY]

(Complete at the end of Chapter 1, before moving to Unit 2)

TEACHER DIRECTIONS: No notes, no textbook. Students answer from memory only. After completing, they check their answers against the text and note what they missed. This "desirable difficulty" strengthens long-term memory encoding even when students cannot recall the answer — the act of trying to retrieve, then receiving feedback, is itself a powerful learning event. Keep this to 10–12 minutes.

Round 1 Questions (Answer from memory — no notes):

  1. What was Abraham Lincoln's plan for Reconstruction called, and what did it require?

  2. Name TWO specific provisions of the Black Codes and explain what each was designed to do.

  3. What were the "Three Rs" of Reconstruction? Name one specific program from each category. Wait — that's New Deal, not Reconstruction. (Catch: This is a deliberate interleaving prompt. If students answer "CCC, WPA" etc., they've confused eras — which is a valuable diagnostic. Correct answer: There were no "Three Rs" in Reconstruction — this is a common confusion with FDR's New Deal. Reconstruction programs include the Freedmen's Bureau and the Enforcement Acts.) [TEACHER NOTE: This intentional mismatch is designed to build students' ability to distinguish eras — a common EOC error source. Discuss the confusion explicitly.]

  4. Explain the Compromise of 1877 in 2-3 sentences. What was traded, and what were the consequences for Black Americans in the South?

  5. The 14th Amendment included three key clauses in Section 1. Name all three and briefly explain what each guarantees.

  6. What did Frederick Douglass mean when he wrote in 1866 that without political rights, freedom was insufficient? Summarize his main argument in your own words.

  7. What was sharecropping, and how did debt peonage function within that system?

  8. Who were the "Redeemers"? What did they "redeem" the South from, and what did they replace it with?

Accountable Talk Stems

Post these in the classroom during discussion. Train students to use them explicitly — especially the "challenge" stems, which many students are reluctant to use without modeling.

To agree and build: - "I agree with _ and I'd like to add __." - "That connects to something in the text — on [section/page], it says __." - "_'s point about _ is strong because the evidence also shows ___."

To respectfully challenge: - "I want to push back on that. The evidence actually suggests __." - "I see it differently. When I read Document _, I noticed __." - "Can you point to the specific evidence? I'm not sure the text supports ___."

To ask clarifying questions: - "What do you mean when you say __?" - "Are you arguing that __? Because I want to make sure I understand your claim." - "What evidence are you using to support that? I want to see if I missed something."

To synthesize: - "So what we seem to be saying is __." - "We've heard two sides. What would we need to know to decide which is more supported?" - "How does this connect to what we learned about __?"

Socratic Seminar — Unit 1

Preparation (assign as homework the night before): Students read the full text of Frederick Douglass's "Reconstruction" (Atlantic Monthly, December 1866) from Appendix C. Students bring two prepared text-based questions to the seminar.

Central Question: "Frederick Douglass argued in 1866 that without political rights and economic security, legal freedom was insufficient. Now that we have studied what actually happened to Black Americans after Reconstruction, was Douglass right? And what does his prediction tell us about what is required for genuine freedom in a democracy?"

Opening Rounds — Text-Grounded: 1. What is Douglass's main argument? Can someone state it in one sentence? 2. What evidence does Douglass use to support his argument? 3. Who was Douglass writing for? How does knowing his audience shape his argument?

Middle Rounds — Analytical: 4. Looking at what we know happened — the Black Codes, the KKK, the Compromise of 1877, Jim Crow — was Douglass's analysis accurate? What did he predict correctly? 5. What did Douglass NOT foresee? What aspects of Reconstruction's failure did he underestimate? 6. How does the failure of land redistribution relate to Douglass's argument about freedom?

Closing Round — Contemporary Connection: 7. Douglass wrote about the conditions required for genuine freedom in a democracy. Are his standards — political rights AND economic security AND protection from violence — requirements for freedom? What would you add or remove from that list?

*[TEACHER NOTE: The final question has contemporary resonance. Handle with care to ensure discussion remains historically grounded and evidence-based rather than becoming a current events debate. Redirect to historical evidence when discussion drifts.]

Structured Academic Controversy (SAC) — Unit 1

Topic: Was the Compromise of 1877 a necessary political reality or an unconscionable betrayal of Black Americans?

Setup: Pairs of students are assigned positions. After presenting their assigned position with evidence, pairs SWITCH and argue the opposite position. The goal is to understand both sides before reaching a genuine consensus.

Position A: Necessary Political Reality Arguments to develop: - The election of 1876 was genuinely disputed; without a resolution, the constitutional crisis could have escalated to renewed violence - Reconstruction was already failing — Klan violence, Northern political fatigue, and Supreme Court rulings had weakened it before 1877 - Hayes had limited leverage; the best outcome available was a peaceful transfer of power - Some Reconstruction institutions (public schools, the constitutional amendments) survived

Position B: Unconscionable Betrayal Arguments to develop: - The Reconstruction Amendments made explicit promises to Black Americans that the government now chose not to enforce - The people who bore the cost — Black Southerners — had no voice in the negotiations - Alternative federal intervention (sustained military presence, enforcement of civil rights legislation) could have protected Black rights - The Compromise created the conditions for nearly 90 more years of legal racial terror

Consensus-Building Question (after both sides are presented): "Both sides acknowledge that Black Americans suffered enormous harm from the end of Reconstruction. What would have had to be different — in federal commitment, political will, or public support — for Reconstruction to have succeeded?"

Critical Content Notes

The "Lost Cause" mythology: Students almost certainly arrive with some exposure to Lost Cause narratives about Reconstruction — the idea that Reconstruction governments were corrupt and incompetent, that the KKK was a defensive response to "Yankee aggression," and that Reconstruction was imposed on an innocent South. These narratives were deliberately constructed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries by Southern white historians and politicians and were embedded in popular culture through films like Birth of a Nation (1915) and the Gone With the Wind novel and film.

Handle this directly. You do NOT need to attack students for holding these views — they were likely taught them, not just by their families but by prior curricula and popular culture. The approach should be: "Here is what the primary source evidence actually shows. Let's look at the documents." The KKK Congressional Hearings, the Black Codes, the actual record of Reconstruction governments — these are the antidote to mythology. Let the sources speak.

Andrew Johnson as a Tennessee figure: Johnson is simultaneously a man of enormous historical importance and a figure whose legacy is deeply troubled by his white supremacist ideology and his active work to undermine Black freedom. He is, however, a Tennessee politician — born in North Carolina, raised in Tennessee, served as military governor of Tennessee, and the only Southern senator to remain in Congress during the war. This is worth acknowledging honestly: Tennessee's political tradition includes both Johnson's Confederate-sympathetic conservatism AND the Nashville sit-in movement a century later. History is complicated, and that complexity is instructive.

The Scopes Trial "Three Rs" mismatch in Retrieval Round 1: The deliberate interleaving of a question that invites students to confuse Reconstruction-era content with New Deal content is intentional. It is a diagnostic tool: students who answer "CCC, WPA, TVA" have confused the eras. This confusion is extremely common and is a significant source of errors on the U.S. History EOC. The correction process — pointing out the error, having students articulate why the confusion occurred, explicitly distinguishing the two eras — is more instructionally powerful than simply teaching the eras separately. Note this for students explicitly: "You'll notice I tricked you. This is because I know this is one of the most common mistakes on the EOC. Now that you've made it and corrected it, you're far less likely to make it on the test."

Primary Source Copyright Compliance: The "Letter from Birmingham Jail" does NOT appear in Unit 1 — it is in Unit 7. However, when you reach Unit 7, be aware that the MLK Estate controls this copyright aggressively. Budget for a licensing fee or use the very brief excerpts permitted under fair use doctrine (typically 250–500 words for educational use). DO NOT reproduce the full letter without a license from Intellectual Properties Management (IPM). The same applies to full Langston Hughes poems in Unit 4 — obtain permission or use brief excerpts.

Common Misconceptions — Chapter 1

Misconception Accurate Understanding How to Address
"Lincoln freed the slaves with the Emancipation Proclamation" The Emancipation Proclamation (1863) freed enslaved people only in Confederate states in rebellion; it did NOT free enslaved people in border states. The 13th Amendment (1865) formally abolished slavery everywhere. Timeline activity; direct comparison of EP and 13th Amendment
"The Civil War ended slavery and everything was fine" The Black Codes, sharecropping, KKK, and Compromise of 1877 show that legal freedom did not produce genuine freedom Sharecropping math activity; primary source analysis
"Reconstruction was a failure because Black people couldn't govern" Reconstruction governments were functioning, reform-minded governments. They failed because of violent overthrow, federal abandonment, and legal manipulation — not incompetence. Primary source evidence; statistics on Reconstruction school systems; named officeholders
"The Compromise of 1877 was a reasonable resolution to a disputed election" While there is some historical argument for this position, students should understand its catastrophic cost for Black Americans and evaluate it accordingly SAC activity; Document 4 (KKK testimony) showing what followed
"Sharecropping was just a free-labor system" Debt peonage, vagrancy laws, and illiteracy made the system nearly impossible to escape for the majority of participants Math activity; primary source analysis of vagrancy laws

UNIT 1 GLOSSARY ENTRIES

(For Appendix A — Alphabetical Glossary)

Format: Term | Pronunciation Guide | Student-Friendly Definition | Used in Context | Unit Reference

13th Amendment | (thir-TEENTH uh-MEND-ment) | The 1865 addition to the U.S. Constitution that officially ended slavery throughout the United States. It stated that neither slavery nor forced labor could exist in the U.S., except as punishment for a crime. | "The 13th Amendment abolished slavery, but the Black Codes showed that legal freedom did not guarantee real freedom." | Unit 1, Section 1.2

14th Amendment | (four-TEENTH uh-MEND-ment) | The 1868 addition to the U.S. Constitution that made all people born in the United States citizens and guaranteed them equal protection of the laws and due process. It directly reversed the Dred Scott ruling. | "The 14th Amendment's equal protection clause would later become the foundation for the Brown v. Board of Education decision in 1954." | Unit 1, Section 1.2; revisited Units 3, 7, 10

15th Amendment | (fif-TEENTH uh-MEND-ment) | The 1870 addition to the U.S. Constitution that gave Black men the right to vote by prohibiting the denial of voting rights based on race, color, or previous condition of servitude. | "The 15th Amendment guaranteed Black men the right to vote, but Southern states used poll taxes and literacy tests to get around it for nearly 100 years." | Unit 1, Section 1.2; revisited Unit 7

Black Codes | (blak kōdz) | Laws passed by Southern states in 1865–1866 that severely restricted the rights of formerly enslaved people. Black Codes required freedpeople to sign annual labor contracts, criminalized unemployment (vagrancy), restricted movement, and prevented Black people from owning certain kinds of property. They were designed to keep freedpeople in a condition similar to slavery. | "When Congress saw the Black Codes, they realized that Presidential Reconstruction under Johnson had failed to protect the rights of freedpeople." | Unit 1, Sections 1.1 and 1.2

carpetbagger | (KAR-pet-bag-er) | A derogatory term used by white Southerners to describe Northerners who came to the South during Reconstruction, often to serve in government or business. The name implied they arrived with only a "carpetbag" (a cheap traveling bag), as greedy outsiders looking to exploit the South. In reality, many carpetbaggers were teachers, business people, and political reformers — though some were indeed corrupt. | "The term 'carpetbagger' was used as an insult by Redeemer Democrats who opposed Reconstruction." | Unit 1, Section 1.2

Compromise of 1877 | (KOM-pruh-myz uv 1877) | The behind-the-scenes political deal that resolved the disputed presidential election of 1876. In exchange for accepting Republican Rutherford B. Hayes as president, Republicans agreed to withdraw the last federal troops from the South, effectively ending Reconstruction and leaving Black Southerners without federal protection. | "The Compromise of 1877 was a turning point: when federal troops left, Redeemer governments dismantled the remaining protections Black Americans had gained during Reconstruction." | Unit 1, Section 1.3

debt peonage | (det PEE-uh-nij) | A system of labor control in which workers were bound to their employers by debt — they owed money that was nearly impossible to repay, and law enforcement could force them to keep working until the debt was paid. Under sharecropping and crop lien systems, this created a cycle of permanent economic dependency. | "Debt peonage was not technically slavery, but it bound formerly enslaved people to white landowners through a cycle of debt that was almost impossible to escape." | Unit 1, Section 1.2

Freedmen's Bureau | (FREED-menz BYOOR-oh) | The Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, established by Congress in 1865 to help formerly enslaved people transition to freedom. It provided food, clothing, medical care, legal assistance, and education. Its most significant failure was the inability to redistribute land to freedpeople. | "The Freedmen's Bureau set up thousands of schools for formerly enslaved people, but without land to farm independently, many freedpeople had no choice but to become sharecroppers." | Unit 1, Section 1.2

impeachment | (im-PEECH-ment) | The formal process by which the U.S. House of Representatives charges a president or other federal official with "high crimes and misdemeanors." Impeachment is NOT removal from office — that requires a two-thirds vote in the Senate. President Andrew Johnson was impeached in 1868 but acquitted by the Senate by a single vote. | "Johnson was impeached by the House in 1868, but the Senate failed to convict him by just one vote, and he served out the rest of his term." | Unit 1, Section 1.1

Jim Crow | (jim krō) | The system of laws and social customs that enforced racial segregation across the American South from roughly the 1870s through the 1960s. Jim Crow laws required separation of Black and white people in schools, transportation, restaurants, restrooms, and other public spaces. The name comes from a demeaning stage character. (Fully developed in Unit 2; introduced here in Unit 1 as an outcome of Reconstruction's failure.) | "When Reconstruction ended and federal troops left the South, the Redeemer governments began establishing the Jim Crow system." | Unit 1, Section 1.3; primary focus Unit 2/7

Ku Klux Klan (KKK) | (kyoo klux klan) | A white supremacist terrorist organization founded in Pulaski, Tennessee in 1865 by former Confederate General Nathan Bedford Forrest and others. During Reconstruction, the KKK used murder, assault, arson, and threats to prevent Black men from voting and to terrorize Black communities and their white Republican allies. Congress passed the Enforcement Acts (1870–71) to suppress the Klan, but it was revived in the 1920s. | "The KKK used systematic violence as a political strategy: by terrorizing Black voters, they helped restore white Democratic Party control of Southern governments." | Unit 1, Section 1.3

Radical Republicans | (RAD-ih-kul rih-PUB-lih-kenz) | The faction of the Republican Party in Congress that believed Reconstruction must include full civil and political rights for Black Americans and structural changes to Southern society. Led by Thaddeus Stevens in the House and Charles Sumner in the Senate, they clashed repeatedly with President Andrew Johnson. | "The Radical Republicans believed Lincoln's 10% Plan and Johnson's Reconstruction plan were dangerously lenient — they wanted real guarantees of Black political rights, not just the end of legal slavery." | Unit 1, Sections 1.1 and 1.2

Reconstruction | (ree-kun-STRUK-shun) | The period from approximately 1865 to 1877 during which the United States government worked to reintegrate the former Confederate states into the Union following the Civil War, and to define the legal status and rights of formerly enslaved people. | "Reconstruction was one of the most consequential and contested periods in American history — its successes and failures shaped the country for more than a century." | Unit 1, all sections

scalawag | (SKAL-uh-wag) | A derogatory term used by white Southern Democrats to describe white Southerners who supported the Republican Party and Reconstruction. Like "carpetbagger," it was used as an insult. Scalawags included Southern Unionists, businesspeople who welcomed Northern investment, and poor white farmers who had never supported the planter class. | "White Southerners who supported Reconstruction were called 'scalawags' by their opponents, a term meant to shame them for cooperating with federal Reconstruction policies." | Unit 1, Section 1.2

sharecropping | (SHAIR-krop-ing) | A farming system that emerged after the Civil War in which a tenant farmer (often a formerly enslaved person) worked a portion of a landowner's land in exchange for a share of the crop — typically one-third to one-half. Combined with debt and vagrancy laws, sharecropping became a system of economic control that kept millions of Black Southerners in poverty for generations. | "Without land of their own, many formerly enslaved people became sharecroppers — legally free but economically trapped in a cycle of debt." | Unit 1, Section 1.2

tenant farming | (TEN-unt FAR-ming) | Similar to sharecropping, tenant farming was a system in which a farmer rented land from a landowner, paying rent either in cash or as a portion of the crop. Tenant farmers had slightly more independence than sharecroppers but were still subject to debt cycles and economic dependency. | "Both sharecropping and tenant farming kept formerly enslaved people economically dependent on white landowners, even after legal slavery ended." | Unit 1, Section 1.2

Wade-Davis Bill | (wayd DAY-vis bil) | A 1864 congressional bill that proposed much stricter conditions for Reconstruction than Lincoln's 10% Plan — requiring a majority of white male citizens to swear loyalty oaths and demanding that new state constitutions abolish slavery. Lincoln pocket-vetoed it. | "The Wade-Davis Bill showed that even before the war ended, Congress and the President disagreed fundamentally about who had the authority to direct Reconstruction and how harsh its terms should be." | Unit 1, Section 1.1
