Section 1: The Crash and Its Causes (US.39)

On October 29, 1929 — a day that would become known as Black Tuesday — the New York Stock Exchange lost approximately 12% of its value in a single day of trading. The collapse was not sudden; it was the culmination of structural weaknesses that had been building throughout the apparent prosperity of the 1920s. Understanding the Depression requires understanding why a stock market crash became a decade-long economic catastrophe.

The first cause was overproduction. American factories and farms had been producing more goods than consumers could afford to buy. During the 1920s, this imbalance was masked by advertising, installment buying, and credit — consumers purchased goods they could not actually afford. When credit dried up, demand collapsed.

The second cause was buying on margin — purchasing stocks with borrowed money, paying only 10–15% of the stock's price and borrowing the rest. When stock prices fell, investors could not repay their loans, triggering bank failures across the country.

The third cause was the weakness of the banking system. American banks were largely unregulated, overleveraged, and poorly diversified. When depositors panicked and began withdrawing funds — a bank run — banks could not meet demand and collapsed. Between 1930 and 1933, approximately 9,000 American banks failed, wiping out the savings of millions of ordinary Americans. The FDIC did not yet exist; there was no government insurance protecting deposits.

The fourth cause was the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, which raised tariffs on over 20,000 imported goods to historic levels. Rather than protecting American industry, the tariff triggered retaliatory tariffs from trading partners, collapsing international trade by roughly 65% between 1929 and 1934. Deflation — falling prices across the economy — made it impossible for farmers and businesses to repay debts, triggering a spiral of foreclosures and business failures.

Section 2: The Human Cost — Dust Bowl and Ordinary Americans (US.40–US.41)[CAUSATION]

🌳 Geographic Awareness — SSP.06

The Dust Bowl primarily affected: Oklahoma, Kansas, Texas Panhandle, Colorado, New Mexico.

Tennessee was NOT in the Dust Bowl, but was severely affected by the broader agricultural depression. Tennessee farm income fell approximately 50% between 1929 and 1932.

The TVA (see Section 3) addressed Tennessee's agricultural crisis through rural electrification, flood control, and economic development along the Tennessee River valley.

The human cost of the Depression was not an abstraction. It was experienced in the daily lives of millions of Americans who had done nothing to cause the crisis. By 1932, approximately one in four American workers was unemployed — roughly 13 million people. Millions more were underemployed, working part-time for reduced wages. Breadlines stretched for blocks in every major American city. Hoovervilles — shantytowns of improvised shelters built by homeless Americans — appeared in public parks across the country, mockingly named after the president whose policies had failed to stem the crisis.

The Dust Bowl compounded the agricultural depression that had already devastated rural America throughout the 1920s. A combination of drought, overplowing that had destroyed the native grasslands' root systems, and fierce winds turned approximately 100 million acres of the southern Great Plains into a dust-blown wasteland. Between 1930 and 1940, an estimated 3.5 million people — the "Okies" — fled the Dust Bowl states for California and other destinations, becoming the largest internal migration in American history since the Civil War.

Section 3: The New Deal and the TVA — Tennessee Required Content (US.43)[CONTEXTUALIZATION]

Tennessee Required Content — TCA and Tennessee Academic Standards The Tennessee Valley Authority is required content under Tennessee Academic Standard US.43. The TVA is a direct, tangible, and ongoing connection between New Deal history and the lived experience of Tennesseans. Every Tennessee student should know: what the TVA was, why Tennessee was chosen, what it built, what it changed, and why it remains significant today.

Franklin D. Roosevelt took office on March 4, 1933, with the nation at the nadir of the Depression. In his First Inaugural Address, he told a terrified nation: "The only thing we have to fear is fear itself." Within 100 days, he had pushed through Congress the most sweeping legislative program in American history — the New Deal.

The New Deal was not a single program or a coherent ideology. It was a pragmatic response to crisis — an experiment in which different agencies tried different approaches. Roosevelt himself described his philosophy as "try something; if it fails, try something else." The New Deal can be understood through three goals: Relief (immediate help for the suffering), Recovery (stimulating the economy), and Reform (preventing future depressions).

The Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA)[CONTEXTUALIZATION]

The most transformative New Deal program for Tennessee — and one of the most significant public works projects in American history — was the Tennessee Valley Authority, established by the TVA Act signed by President Roosevelt on May 18, 1933.

The Tennessee River valley was chosen for several specific reasons. The region encompassed parts of seven states and included some of the most economically depressed communities in America. In 1933, only approximately 3% of Tennessee farms had electricity. Flooding along the Tennessee River regularly destroyed crops and homes. The Wilson Dam at Muscle Shoals, Alabama — built during WWI to produce nitrogen for explosives — stood idle, representing an untapped federal investment in hydroelectric potential.

Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA)
A federal corporation established May 18, 1933, authorized to construct dams, generate and sell electricity, control flooding, improve navigation, and promote economic development throughout the Tennessee River valley — an area encompassing parts of Tennessee, Alabama, Mississippi, Kentucky, Virginia, North Carolina, and Georgia. The TVA was a bold experiment in regional development and government-owned utility operation. Headquartered in Knoxville, Tennessee, it remains operational today as the nation's largest public power provider.
