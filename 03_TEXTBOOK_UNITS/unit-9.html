SECTION 9.1: THE REAGAN REVOLUTION

Section Learning Targets ("I Can" Statements): - I can explain the intellectual and political roots of the conservative movement that brought Reagan to power. - I can describe Reaganomics — supply-side tax cuts, deregulation, reduced domestic spending — and evaluate the evidence for its effects. - I can explain the Iran-Contra Affair as a constitutional crisis and evaluate how it was handled. - I can connect Reagan's domestic agenda to the long-running debate about capitalism, free markets, and the role of government. Narrative Text

The Conservative Moment: How Reagan Got There[CAUSATION]

Ronald Reagan did not emerge from nowhere. He was the product of a conservative intellectual and political movement that had been building for thirty years — since the New Deal that his generation had lived through.

The Intellectual Roots: The conservative case against the New Deal / Great Society state rested on several intellectual traditions:

  • Friedrich Hayek's The Road to Serfdom (1944): argued that central economic planning inevitably led to political authoritarianism — that economic freedom and political freedom were inseparable. [VERIFY: The Road to Serfdom — published in the UK in March 1944; an American edition followed; widely influential in conservative intellectual circles; confirm]
  • Milton Friedman's Capitalism and Freedom (1962): argued that free markets were the best guarantor of individual liberty; government intervention distorted markets and reduced freedom. [VERIFY: Capitalism and Freedom — published 1962; University of Chicago Press; confirm]
  • Barry Goldwater's The Conscience of a Conservative (1960): applied these principles to American politics; argued that both the New Deal and the emerging civil rights legislation represented government overreach that threatened freedom. [VERIFY: Goldwater's book — 1960; confirm; note that Goldwater's civil rights position — opposition based on constitutional rather than racial grounds — is genuinely contested; present accurately]

Reagan himself articulated the conservative critique in a famous 1964 speech supporting Barry Goldwater — "A Time for Choosing" (also called "The Speech"): "Government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem." [VERIFY: Reagan's "A Time for Choosing" — delivered October 27, 1964; this is the foundational statement of Reagan's political philosophy; full text is public domain at Reagan Presidential Library; confirm specific quotation]

Why 1980? The conditions that brought Reagan to power were created by the crises of the 1970s (Unit 8): Vietnam's damage to national confidence, Watergate's damage to trust in government, stagflation's damage to economic security, and Carter's "malaise." Reagan offered the antidote: optimism, strength, and the conviction that government itself was the source of the problems.

Reagan won the 1980 election with 489 Electoral College votes to Carter's 49 — a historic landslide. [VERIFY: 1980 election results — Reagan 489, Carter 49; Reagan won 44 states; 50.7% popular vote; confirm] He was 69 years old on inauguration day — the oldest president to take office at that point. [VERIFY: Reagan's age at inauguration — born February 6, 1911; inaugurated January 20, 1981; he was 69; confirm]

Reaganomics: The Theory and the Practice[COMPARISON]

Reagan's economic program — widely called Reaganomics or supply-side economics — rested on a specific theory about how economies grow:

The Theory — Supply-Side Economics: Traditional Keynesian economics (Unit 5) focused on stimulating demand — when demand was low (people weren't buying), government should spend to boost it.

Supply-side economics turned this logic upside down: cut taxes on high earners and businesses (the "suppliers" of goods and investment), and the resulting investment would generate growth that created jobs and eventually produced higher tax revenues even at lower rates. Critics called this "trickle-down" economics — the idea that benefits to the wealthy would trickle down to everyone else.

The theoretical mechanism was the Laffer Curve — an argument that tax rates could be so high that cutting them would actually increase revenues by stimulating economic activity. [VERIFY: Laffer Curve — associated with economist Arthur Laffer; Reagan's budget director David Stockman reportedly described the tax cuts as a "Trojan horse" for cutting the top tax rate; confirm this specific Stockman quotation — it appeared in an Atlantic Monthly article by William Greider in December 1981; VERIFY: confirm specific source and accuracy]

[TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — Supply-side economics and the debate it generated is the most direct contemporary case study of the virtues and limits of capitalism, free markets, and limited government that the statute requires students to engage. Present both the theory AND the evidence fairly.]

The Policy: - The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 cut the top marginal income tax rate from 70% to 50%, with further cuts later bringing it to 28%. [VERIFY: ERTA 1981 — top rate from 70% to 50%; ultimately to 28% by 1988 Tax Reform; confirm these figures] - Deregulation of financial services, airlines, telecommunications, and other industries - Significant reductions in domestic discretionary spending (though Social Security and Medicare were largely protected) - Dramatic increase in defense spending — from approximately $134 billion in 1980 to approximately $303 billion in 1988 [VERIFY: defense spending figures — confirm against Office of Management and Budget historical tables; the defense buildup is well-documented]

The Results — What Actually Happened:

What went right: - The economy did recover from the stagflation of the 1970s; inflation fell from approximately 13.5% in 1980 to 3.2% by 1983 [VERIFY: inflation figures; confirm] - A long economic expansion followed — from the end of the 1981–82 recession through most of the late 1980s - Stock market rose dramatically

What was complicated: - The 1981–82 recession — the deepest since the Great Depression — occurred immediately after the tax cuts; unemployment peaked at 10.8% in December 1982 [VERIFY: 1981-82 recession unemployment — 10.8% peak; confirm against BLS data] - The federal deficit exploded — from $79 billion in 1981 to $221 billion in 1986 [VERIFY: deficit figures — confirm against OMB historical tables] - The national debt nearly tripled during Reagan's presidency — from approximately $994 billion to $2.9 trillion [VERIFY: national debt Reagan era — approximately correct; confirm against Treasury historical data] - Income inequality grew — the Gini coefficient (a measure of income inequality) rose throughout the 1980s [VERIFY: Gini coefficient trends in the 1980s; confirm against Census Bureau historical data] - Middle-class wages remained stagnant even as overall GDP grew

[MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — The historical and economic assessment of Reaganomics is genuinely contested: conservative economists credit it with ending stagflation and producing the 1980s expansion; liberal economists argue the growth was produced by the Federal Reserve's anti-inflation policy (Paul Volcker's interest rate increases) and that the tax cuts primarily benefited the wealthy while exploding the deficit. Both positions are held by serious economists. Present the evidence and the debate honestly; TCA § 49-6-1028(b) requires genuine engagement with the arguments for capitalism and free markets — which requires also engaging with the evidence about their limitations]

Reagan's Second Term and the Budget Deal: Reagan's actual fiscal record contradicted pure supply-side theory. When the deficit exploded, Reagan signed the Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act of 1982 — a significant tax increase to reduce the deficit. [VERIFY: TEFRA — August 19, 1982; the largest peacetime tax increase in American history to that point; confirm] He signed other tax increases as well during his presidency, a fact that is often omitted from simplified accounts of the Reagan era.

Social Issues and the Culture Wars

Reagan's coalition included economic conservatives (tax cuts, deregulation) AND social conservatives — evangelical Christians, traditional Catholics, and others who were alarmed by the social changes of the 1960s and 70s.

The Moral Majority and the Religious Right: The Reverend Jerry Falwell Sr.'s Moral Majority, founded in 1979, mobilized evangelical Christians as a political force for the first time in a generation. [VERIFY: Moral Majority founded — June 1979; Falwell; confirm] The issues that motivated this movement: opposition to abortion (Roe v. Wade, 1973), opposition to gay rights, support for school prayer, opposition to the ERA, and concern about what they saw as the coarsening of American culture.

Reagan used the language of social conservatism — he personally supported a constitutional amendment restoring prayer to public schools — but delivered relatively little on the social agenda because it required congressional action he could not secure. His most lasting social conservative legacy was his appointment of conservative justices to the Supreme Court: Sandra Day O'Connor (1981, first woman on the Court), Antonin Scalia (1986), and Anthony Kennedy (1988). [VERIFY: appointment dates for all three — O'Connor confirmed September 21, 1981; Scalia confirmed September 17, 1986; Kennedy confirmed February 18, 1988; confirm]

The AIDS Crisis: One of the most morally troubling aspects of the Reagan administration was its slow response to the AIDS epidemic. By the time Reagan first publicly spoke about AIDS in a major address in May 1987, over 36,000 Americans had been diagnosed and more than 20,000 had died. [VERIFY: AIDS deaths by May 1987 — approximately 20,000 Americans had died by this point; confirm against CDC historical data]

Many Reagan administration officials connected AIDS to gay men (the disease's initial concentration in that population) and treated it as a private moral failing rather than a public health emergency. Reagan's press secretary Larry Speakes joked about AIDS in press briefings in 1982. [VERIFY: Speakes's AIDS jokes — documented in White House press briefing transcripts from October 1982; confirm from primary source]

The failure to treat AIDS as a public health emergency during the critical early years of the epidemic cost an unknowable number of lives and represents a serious moral failure of the Reagan administration. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — Conservative historians argue Reagan eventually did increase funding; liberal historians and AIDS activists argue the delay was unconscionable; present both positions with appropriate evidence; TCA § 49-6-1006 — AIDS disproportionately affected Black Americans, Latino Americans, and gay Americans; this history must be included]

Iran-Contra: Reagan's Watergate?[CAUSATION]

The Iran-Contra Affair (revealed November 1986) was the most serious constitutional crisis of the Reagan presidency. The affair involved two illegal programs connected by money:

  1. Iran: The Reagan administration secretly sold weapons to Iran — which was under an arms embargo — in exchange for Iran's help securing the release of American hostages held in Lebanon by Iranian-backed Hezbollah. This violated U.S. law and contradicted Reagan's public "no negotiations with terrorists" policy. [VERIFY: Iran arms sales — well-documented; confirm specific dates and details]

  2. Contra: Profits from the Iran arms sales were secretly diverted to fund the Nicaraguan Contra rebels — at a time when Congress had specifically prohibited such aid through the Boland Amendment. [VERIFY: Boland Amendment — first version enacted December 8, 1982; the prohibition on Contra aid that the Iran-Contra scheme violated; confirm specific provisions]

Both programs were run out of the National Security Council by Marine Lieutenant Colonel Oliver North, who testified (in uniform) before Congress and became a hero to many conservatives for his unapologetic defense of his actions. [VERIFY: North's congressional testimony — July 1987; confirm]

Howard Baker's Role: Reagan brought in Howard Baker of Tennessee as Chief of Staff in February 1987 specifically to stabilize the administration in the wake of Iran-Contra. Baker — who had demonstrated constitutional integrity in Watergate — helped Reagan manage the crisis and restore credibility. He served until July 1988. [VERIFY: Baker Chief of Staff dates — February 27, 1987 to July 1, 1988; confirm] [TENNESSEE CONNECTION — Baker's second major national constitutional moment]

Reagan denied knowledge of the Contra diversion — maintaining he did not know that profits from the Iran arms sales had been sent to the Contras. The Tower Commission report found Reagan "detached" from the decision-making that led to the scandal. [VERIFY: Tower Commission — the President's Special Review Board; report released February 26, 1987; confirm findings]

Unlike Watergate, the Iran-Contra affair did not produce a resignation. The constitutional debate about its significance continues: - Critics: Iran-Contra was a systematic violation of congressional authority, the constitutional requirement that the executive branch follow the law, and an active cover-up that paralleled Watergate - Defenders: The Boland Amendment itself was an unconstitutional infringement on the president's foreign policy prerogatives; North and others believed they were acting in the national interest against communist expansion in Central America

[MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — present both positions; TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — Iran-Contra is another case study in the tension between executive power and congressional authority in a constitutional republic]

The Welfare Reform Debate: Reagan's domestic agenda also targeted the social safety net established by the New Deal and expanded by the Great Society. He argued that welfare programs created dependency, discouraged work, and were fundamentally counterproductive — a philosophical position rooted in the conservative tradition's emphasis on individual responsibility.

The results were genuinely mixed: - Some welfare programs were reduced or made more restrictive - Poverty rates did fall during the economic expansion of the mid-1980s - But they remained higher than they had been in the late 1970s, and child poverty remained a persistent problem - The number of people without health insurance increased significantly during the 1980s

[MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — the relationship between welfare programs and poverty is one of the most debated questions in social science; present the competing evidence and arguments fairly; both the New Deal / Great Society case and the conservative case deserve honest representation]

SECTION 9.2: THE END OF THE COLD WAR

Section Learning Targets: - I can explain Reagan's approach to the Cold War — the Reagan Doctrine, the defense buildup, and the Strategic Defense Initiative — and evaluate each. - I can explain Gorbachev's reforms (glasnost and perestroika) and explain why the Soviet system was unable to sustain them. - I can describe the collapse of communist governments in Eastern Europe in 1989 and the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991. - I can evaluate multiple explanations for why the Cold War ended.

Narrative Text

Reagan's Cold War Strategy[CAUSATION]

Reagan entered the White House with a fundamentally different approach to the Soviet Union than his predecessors. Where Nixon had pursued détente — managing the Cold War competition through negotiation — Reagan described the Soviet Union as an "evil empire" and sought to put maximum pressure on it. [VERIFY: Reagan's "evil empire" speech — delivered March 8, 1983, to the National Association of Evangelicals in Orlando, Florida; full text is public domain at the Reagan Library; confirm]

The Reagan Doctrine: Reagan provided aid — covert and overt — to anti-communist movements and governments worldwide: the Contras in Nicaragua, mujahideen in Afghanistan (who were fighting the Soviet invasion), UNITA in Angola, resistance movements in Cambodia and Ethiopia. The principle: support anti-communist forces wherever they challenged Soviet-backed governments. [VERIFY: Reagan Doctrine specific applications — these are well-documented; confirm each before including specific claims about covert operations]

The Defense Buildup: Reagan's dramatic increase in defense spending was both a genuine military strategy and a calculated pressure campaign. The argument: the Soviet economy, already strained, could not match American military spending without destroying itself. The defense buildup forced Soviet leaders to choose between matching American military capacity and maintaining domestic consumption — a choice the Soviet command economy could not sustain.

Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI — "Star Wars"): Reagan announced in March 1983 a vision of a missile defense shield that would render nuclear weapons "impotent and obsolete" — a ground- and space-based system capable of intercepting Soviet ICBMs before they reached American cities. [VERIFY: SDI announcement — March 23, 1983; "Star Wars" nickname; confirm]

SDI was controversial on multiple levels: - The physics community was deeply skeptical it could work - It potentially violated the 1972 Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty - It deeply alarmed Soviet leaders, who could not afford to develop competing technology - Its very uncertainty made it strategically effective — it was one more burden the Soviet economy was asked to bear

Gorbachev and the Soviet Collapse[CAUSATION]

The Cold War ended not primarily because of American pressure but because of fundamental weaknesses within the Soviet system — and because Mikhail Gorbachev, who became General Secretary of the Communist Party in March 1985, tried to reform those weaknesses and lost control of the process. [VERIFY: Gorbachev became General Secretary — March 11, 1985; confirm]

Glasnost and Perestroika: Gorbachev introduced two reform programs: - Glasnost (openness): allowing greater freedom of expression, reducing censorship, permitting discussion of problems the Soviet system had previously suppressed - Perestroika (restructuring): economic reform — introducing limited market mechanisms into the Soviet command economy to improve efficiency

The problems: glasnost released political pressures that the Soviet system had been suppressing for decades. Once people were allowed to criticize the system, they criticized it comprehensively. Perestroika introduced enough market instability to disrupt the existing system without the market institutions to replace it.

Gorbachev intended to save Soviet communism through reform; he destroyed it through reform. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — this assessment of Gorbachev is widely shared among historians; some argue external pressure was equally decisive; present both factors honestly]

The Revolutions of 1989: Beginning in Poland in the summer of 1989, communist governments fell in rapid succession: - Poland: The Solidarity movement (trade union and political movement led by Lech Walesa) won partially free elections in June 1989. [VERIFY: Polish elections — June 4, 1989; Solidarity's sweeping victory; confirm] - Hungary: Opened its border with Austria, allowing East Germans to flee West - East Germany: Massive demonstrations demanded reform; on November 9, 1989, the East German government announced that the Berlin Wall would be opened [VERIFY: Berlin Wall opening — November 9, 1989; confirm the specific circumstances] - Czechoslovakia: The "Velvet Revolution" brought Václav Havel to power without violence [VERIFY: Velvet Revolution — November 17 – December 29, 1989; Havel became president December 29, 1989; confirm] - Romania: The only violent revolution — Nicolae Ceaușescu was executed on Christmas Day 1989 [VERIFY: Ceaușescu execution — December 25, 1989; confirm]

The Berlin Wall — November 9, 1989: The fall of the Berlin Wall is the single most vivid symbol of the Cold War's end. The Wall had divided Berlin since August 13, 1961 — the physical manifestation of the Iron Curtain. [VERIFY: Wall built — construction began August 13, 1961; confirm] When East Germany announced citizens could cross freely, crowds gathered at checkpoints. Guards — with no clear orders — allowed people through. Berliners on both sides began tearing down the Wall with hammers and pickaxes.

President George H.W. Bush — Reagan's successor since January 1989 — was notably restrained in his public response to the Wall's fall. Bush understood that triumphalism might embarrass Gorbachev and destabilize the reform process. His caution was strategically wise even if politically unsatisfying.

German Reunification (October 3, 1990): East and West Germany were reunified on October 3, 1990. [VERIFY: German reunification date — October 3, 1990; confirm] This required extraordinary diplomacy — the Soviet Union had to accept that a reunified Germany would remain in NATO, which required assurances about NATO not expanding eastward (assurances that later became a source of dispute). [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — the question of whether the U.S. and Germany promised not to expand NATO eastward is a significant ongoing historical and political debate; present accurately as a debated question]

The Soviet Dissolution (December 25, 1991): On December 25, 1991 — Christmas Day — the Soviet Union ceased to exist. Mikhail Gorbachev resigned as president of a country that no longer existed, and 15 independent republics took its place, with Russia as the largest. [VERIFY: Soviet dissolution — December 25, 1991; Gorbachev's resignation; confirm]

Why Did the Cold War End? Multiple Explanations: [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — this is a genuine historical debate; present fairly]

  1. Reagan won it (conservative view): The defense buildup and SDI broke the Soviet economy; the Reagan Doctrine undermined Soviet influence worldwide; Reagan's moral clarity — "evil empire," "tear down this wall" — delegitimized the Soviet system. [VERIFY: Reagan's "Tear down this wall" speech — delivered June 12, 1987, at the Brandenburg Gate, West Berlin; public domain; confirm]

  2. Gorbachev chose reform (alternative view): The Cold War ended because Gorbachev made specific choices — glasnost, perestroika, allowing Eastern European revolutions to proceed without Soviet military intervention — that no previous Soviet leader had made. Reagan's pressure was a factor but the decisive agency was Soviet.

  3. The Soviet system was exhausted (systemic view): Soviet communism collapsed because it was an inherently inefficient economic and political system that could not sustain competition with the dynamic capitalist economies of the West; the timing was contingent but the collapse was ultimately inevitable.

  4. The people of Eastern Europe (democratic view): The revolutions of 1989 were made by ordinary citizens — Polish workers in Solidarity, East German demonstrators, Czech students — whose desire for freedom drove the process; both the Soviets and the Americans were responding to events they did not fully control.

The Persian Gulf War (1991)

Iraq's Saddam Hussein invaded neighboring Kuwait on August 2, 1990. [VERIFY: Iraq invasion of Kuwait — August 2, 1990; confirm] President George H.W. Bush assembled a coalition of 35 nations — including Arab nations, with UN Security Council authorization — to expel Iraqi forces from Kuwait. Operation Desert Storm began with a 38-day air campaign (January 17 – February 23, 1991) followed by a 100-hour ground war that drove Iraqi forces from Kuwait. [VERIFY: Desert Storm dates — air war began January 17, 1991; ground war February 24-28, 1991; confirm all dates]

The Gulf War was a military success and a model for post-Cold War international action: a broad coalition, UN authorization, a limited objective (liberating Kuwait, not regime change in Baghdad), and overwhelming force.

Bush chose not to march to Baghdad and remove Saddam Hussein — a decision that was strategically defensible (the coalition mandate was Kuwait's liberation, not regime change; removing Hussein might have fractured the Arab coalition and created a power vacuum) but that would be second-guessed a decade later (Unit 10).

Tennessee Connection: Fort Campbell, Kentucky/Tennessee — home of the 101st Airborne Division ("Air Assault") — was a major staging base for Gulf War deployment. Tennessee's Guard and active-duty soldiers participated in Desert Storm. [VERIFY: 101st Airborne Desert Storm deployment — the division deployed to Saudi Arabia and participated in the ground campaign; confirm unit details; Fort Campbell's location straddling the TN-KY border is a Tennessee connection]

SECTION 9.3: THE CLINTON YEARS AND THE 1990S

Section Learning Targets: - I can explain Clinton's "Third Way" politics and the DLC's strategy for recapturing the center of American politics. - I can analyze NAFTA, its arguments, and its effects on American workers and the economy. - I can describe the dot-com revolution and the 1990s economy. - I can explain the Clinton impeachment and evaluate its constitutional significance. - I can identify the unresolved tensions of the 1990s that set the stage for the 21st century.

Narrative Text

Clinton's "Third Way": Triangulating Liberalism and Conservatism[COMPARISON]

Bill Clinton won the 1992 election by defeating both an incumbent president (Bush) and the most successful third-party candidacy since Theodore Roosevelt's (Ross Perot won 18.9% of the popular vote [VERIFY: Perot 1992 — 18.9% popular vote; 19 million votes; won no Electoral College votes; confirm]).

Clinton's political strategy — developed through the Democratic Leadership Council (DLC) that he had helped lead — was the "Third Way": accepting Reagan's premise that the old New Deal / Great Society liberalism was politically exhausted, and finding a new Democratic position that combined market-friendly economics with progressive social values. "It's the economy, stupid" — James Carville's campaign mantra — captured Clinton's focus. [VERIFY: "It's the economy, stupid" — Carville's phrase was internal; it became famous after the campaign; confirm the context]

What Clinton Accepted from Reagan: - Free trade (NAFTA, WTO) - Budget discipline — the Clinton era produced the first federal budget surpluses since 1969 [VERIFY: Clinton surpluses — fiscal years 1998, 1999, 2000, 2001 all showed surpluses; confirm against OMB data] - Welfare reform (see below) - A generally market-oriented approach to economic policy

What Clinton Kept from the Democratic Tradition: - Expanded the Earned Income Tax Credit for working poor families - AmeriCorps (domestic service program) - Health care reform attempt (ultimately failed) - Gun control legislation — Brady Bill (1993), Assault Weapons Ban (1994) [VERIFY: Brady Bill — Handgun Violence Prevention Act; signed November 30, 1993; Assault Weapons Ban — signed September 13, 1994; both confirmed] - Family and Medical Leave Act (1993) [VERIFY: signed February 5, 1993; confirm]

NAFTA (1993): The North American Free Trade Agreement created a free trade zone among the United States, Canada, and Mexico, eliminating most tariffs over a 15-year period. [VERIFY: NAFTA — signed December 8, 1992; ratified by Congress in November 1993; took effect January 1, 1994; confirm all dates]

Clinton pushed NAFTA through with more Republican than Democratic support — the agreement split the Democratic coalition between pro-trade Clinton Democrats and labor-backed opponents led by figures including Al Gore's future 2000 opponent, Ross Perot ("the giant sucking sound" of jobs going to Mexico). [VERIFY: Perot's "giant sucking sound" — 1992 presidential debate; confirm]

Effects of NAFTA: The effects of NAFTA remain genuinely contested: - Economists' consensus: NAFTA increased total trade significantly; produced modest net gains to the overall U.S. economy; consumers benefited from lower prices - Critics: manufacturing jobs — particularly in textiles, automobiles, and electronics — moved to Mexico where wages were lower; workers in specific industries and regions bore concentrated costs that economists' aggregate gains obscured - Mexico: significant gains in manufacturing employment; but the promised reduction in Mexican poverty and immigration was more limited than supporters had claimed; agricultural workers in Mexico were hurt by competition from subsidized American corn [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — present all three perspectives with specific evidence; TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — NAFTA is a direct case study in the arguments for and against free trade as an expression of capitalism's principles]

Tennessee and NAFTA: Tennessee's economy was specifically affected: - The automotive industry (Saturn plant in Spring Hill, Tennessee; Nissan in Smyrna; later Volkswagen in Chattanooga) benefited from integrated North American supply chains - Tennessee's textile industry — once a major employer — faced competition from lower-wage Mexican and Central American labor; many mills closed [VERIFY: Tennessee automotive plants — Saturn in Spring Hill (GM); Nissan in Smyrna; confirm establishment dates; Volkswagen in Chattanooga opened 2011 — this may be post-NAFTA relevant; confirm the NAFTA-era Tennessee-specific impacts from TDOL data]

Welfare Reform (1996): Clinton signed the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act (PRWORA) — the most significant welfare reform in American history. It: - Ended the federal entitlement to welfare benefits (AFDC) and replaced it with block grants to states (TANF — Temporary Assistance for Needy Families) - Imposed a five-year lifetime limit on federal welfare benefits - Required work activity as a condition of benefits

Supporters argued: welfare reform reduced dependency, encouraged work, and produced significant drops in welfare rolls (which fell dramatically in the late 1990s — partly because of welfare reform, partly because of the booming economy). Critics argued: the reform removed the safety net from the poorest Americans and would produce severe hardship during the next recession. Both concerns turned out to be partly right. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES; TCA § 49-6-1028(b)] [VERIFY: PRWORA — signed August 22, 1996; welfare caseloads declined approximately 60% between 1994 and 2001; confirm]

The Contract with America and the Republican Revolution (1994): In the 1994 midterm elections, Republicans swept to control of both chambers of Congress for the first time since 1954, gaining 54 House seats. [VERIFY: 1994 midterms — Republicans gained 54 House seats, 8 Senate seats; Newt Gingrich became Speaker; confirm] Newt Gingrich, the new Speaker, had campaigned on a "Contract with America" — a specific set of legislative commitments including balanced budget amendment, term limits, welfare reform, and crime legislation.

The Republican Congress and the Democratic president spent the rest of the Clinton years in a complex dance of conflict and accommodation — producing the balanced budgets and welfare reform, but also repeated government shutdowns when they could not agree on spending levels.

The Dot-Com Revolution and the 1990s Economy[CCOT]

Bush's "chicken Kiev speech" caution [VERIFY: Bush's "Chicken Kiev speech" — August 1, 1991; warned Ukraine against "suicidal nationalism"; context is that he was urging caution in Soviet dissolution; the speech was criticized as excessively cautious but is defensible as preventing chaotic breakdown; confirm] demonstrated prioritizing stability over triumphalism. The nuclear deterrence logic actually worked — the assured destruction guarantee prevented either side from using military force to resolve the Cold War's final chapter. Not predictable: no analyst in 1985 predicted Soviet collapse by 1991; the peaceful outcome required many contingent decisions.

Q8 Model Answer: Clinton as "conservative" — strong case can be made. NAFTA passed with more Republican than Democratic votes; Clinton championed free trade over labor union objections. Welfare reform ended the federal welfare entitlement — a more fundamental change than Reagan achieved in 1981. Budget surpluses — Clinton produced the first surpluses in 28 years through fiscal discipline. Deregulation of financial services (Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, 1999 [VERIFY: confirm this Clinton deregulation]) continued Reagan's project. But Clinton also: raised taxes in 1993 (which Republicans predicted would cause recession — it didn't); expanded EITC for working poor; opposed school vouchers; appointed moderate-to-liberal Supreme Court justices; attempted (and failed) major health care reform. "Third way" is a more accurate description: Clinton accepted the Reagan framework on markets and fiscal discipline while maintaining the Democratic tradition on social spending and regulation. The comparison reveals that the center of American politics had moved significantly rightward between 1980 and 1992.

Q9 Model Answer: Conservative critique of Great Society: (1) created dependency — welfare programs removed incentives for work and family stability; (2) bureaucratic inefficiency — federal programs were managed by distant administrators who didn't know local conditions; (3) unintended consequences — programs designed to help the poor allegedly made poverty worse by subsidizing out-of-wedlock birth, discouraging marriage, and reducing work incentives; (4) fiscal unsustainability — the Great Society plus Vietnam produced the inflation and debt of the 1970s. Were critiques supported? Partially: welfare dependency concerns had some empirical support (welfare rolls grew steadily 1965-1994); fiscal concerns were real. But: Great Society also produced Medicare (which reduced senior poverty dramatically), ESEA (which targeted educational inequality), and Voting Rights Act (which transformed Southern democracy) — programs that even conservatives no longer seriously propose eliminating. The oscillation arc: New Deal (government expansion) → Great Society (expansion continued) → stagflation/malaise (crisis of confidence) → Reagan Revolution (contraction) → Clinton "third way" (partial restoration). This oscillation reflects a genuine American ambivalence about government's proper role.

Q10 Model Answer: Core conservative vision: individual liberty and limited government are foundational; the market aggregates individual preferences more efficiently than government programs; excessive government creates dependency and destroys freedom; America is exceptional — a nation conceived in liberty that must defend that liberty against both external enemies (Soviet communism) and internal expansion of the state. First inaugural: government is the problem, not the solution; people capable of governing themselves need no elite managers. Berlin Wall speech: freedom wins; communism is evil; America stands with those who seek liberty against oppression. Contract: specific commitments to reduce government, balance budget, reform welfare, limit terms. Strongest counter-argument: markets, unregulated, produced the Great Depression; segregation was enforced by private actors (businesses) as well as government; government programs (GI Bill, interstate highways, TVA) enabled prosperity the market alone would not have produced; income inequality grew as government contracted, suggesting markets distribute wealth unequally without government correction; some "evils" (public health, environmental protection, civil rights) require government action that markets will not provide.

Critical Content Notes

TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — This Is the Anchor Unit for Capitalism and Free Enterprise: The statute requires that Tennessee students "be taught about the virtues of capitalism and the constitutional republic form of government." Unit 9 is the primary unit to fulfill this requirement for capitalism and free enterprise. The approach: (1) Present the genuine intellectual case for free markets and limited government (Hayek, Friedman, Reagan's philosophy) honestly and fairly (2) Present the genuine historical record of what supply-side economics achieved AND what it cost — the honest evaluation that respects students as citizens who need accurate information to participate in democracy (3) Connect to the ongoing debate: capitalism's virtues (innovation, efficiency, individual freedom) AND its documented limitations (inequality, instability, market failures) are both part of what students need to understand The statute does not require one-sided advocacy for capitalism — it requires teaching students about its virtues. Understanding both its virtues and its historical record, honestly presented, is the appropriate response.

Howard Baker — Tennessee Content: Baker appears twice in this unit: as Reagan's Chief of Staff (primary) and as the figure who brought the constitutional integrity he demonstrated in Watergate (Unit 8) to managing the Iran-Contra crisis. Students should see Baker as a recurring Tennessee figure who demonstrated that constitutional duty transcends partisan loyalty.

AIDS Crisis — Handle with Care and Historical Accuracy: The Reagan administration's inadequate response to the AIDS crisis is historically documented. Present it accurately: the deaths that occurred during years of minimal federal response were real; the disproportionate impact on gay men, Black Americans, and hemophiliacs is documented. Do not sanitize this history. At the same time, present it in historical context: the disease was not understood in the early years; the Reagan administration did eventually increase funding; the failures were partly institutional and partly ideological. Handle with sensitivity appropriate to a high school classroom while maintaining historical accuracy.

Roe v. Wade Connection: The Reagan Revolution's social conservative coalition was animated partly by opposition to Roe v. Wade (Unit 8). Reagan's Supreme Court appointments were intended to produce a more conservative Court. The eventual overturning of Roe in 2022 (Dobbs) is Unit 10 content — connect here as a preview.

It helped Republicans gain 54 House seats in the 1994 midterms — the largest midterm gain since 1946 — and made Newt Gingrich Speaker of the House. Its provisions included a balanced budget amendment, welfare reform, and term limits. | Unit 9, Section 9.3

détente | (review from Unit 8) | The policy of managing Cold War competition through negotiation, pursued by Nixon and Kissinger; partially reversed by Reagan's more confrontational approach. | Units 8 and 9

glasnost | (GLAHS-nust) | (Russian: openness) Mikhail Gorbachev's policy of greater freedom of expression and reduced censorship in the Soviet Union, begun 1985. Intended to reform the Soviet system, it released political pressures that eventually destroyed it. | Unit 9, Section 9.2

Gore, Al (b. 1948) | Tennessee senator (1985-1993) and 42nd Vice President (1993-2001). As senator, he championed federal investment in Internet infrastructure (High Performance Computing Act, 1991). Laffer Curve | The economic concept — associated with Arthur Laffer — arguing that tax rates can be so high that cutting them would increase government revenues by stimulating economic activity. It provided the theoretical basis for Reagan's supply-side tax cuts. Its practical application in the 1980s produced lower revenues than predicted, contributing to the deficit explosion. | Unit 9, Section 9.1

NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement) | A free trade agreement among the United States, Canada, and Mexico, signed 1992 and taking effect January 1, 1994. It eliminated most tariffs over 15 years. In Tennessee, it benefited the automotive sector (Nissan, Saturn) while harming the textile industry. Economists generally assess it as a modest net positive for the overall economy with concentrated costs to specific workers and communities. | Unit 9, Section 9.3 [TENNESSEE CONTENT]

perestroika | (pehr-uh-STROI-kuh) | (Russian: restructuring) Mikhail Gorbachev's program of limited economic reform intended to introduce market mechanisms into the Soviet command economy. Combined with glasnost, it destabilized the Soviet system rather than saving it. | Unit 9, Section 9.2

Reaganomics | The economic policy of the Reagan administration (1981-1989): supply-side tax cuts (top marginal rate from 70% to 28%); deregulation; reduction in domestic spending; dramatic defense spending increase. Results were mixed: inflation fell and expansion followed, but the deficit tripled, the debt nearly tripled, and income inequality grew. | Unit 9, Section 9.1

Reagan Doctrine | The Reagan administration's policy of supporting anti-communist resistance movements worldwide — in Afghanistan, Nicaragua, Angola, and elsewhere — as a means of pressuring Soviet-backed governments globally. | Unit 9, Section 9.2

Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI) | Reagan's 1983 proposal for a space- and ground-based missile defense system that would intercept Soviet ICBMs ("Star Wars"). Technologically dubious but strategically significant: it frightened Soviet planners who could not afford to develop a competing system and added pressure to the Soviet economy. | Unit 9, Section 9.2

supply-side economics | The economic theory that tax cuts on high earners and businesses stimulate investment, producing growth that benefits everyone (sometimes called "trickle-down" by critics). Distinguished from Keynesian demand-side economics by its focus on stimulating production rather than consumption. | Unit 9, Section 9.1 [TCA § 49-6-1028(b)]

"Third Way" | The political strategy of the Democratic Leadership Council and Bill Clinton — accepting Reagan's market-oriented premises while maintaining Democratic social values; combining free trade and fiscal discipline with progressive policies on education, environment, and health care. | Unit 9, Section 9.3
