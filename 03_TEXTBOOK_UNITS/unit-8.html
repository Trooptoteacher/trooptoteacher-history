SECTION 8.1: VIETNAM — AMERICA'S LONGEST WAR

Section Learning Targets ("I Can" Statements): - I can trace U.S. involvement in Vietnam from advisory role to full military commitment, explaining the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution's significance. - I can analyze the escalation of the war under LBJ and explain why the U.S. strategy failed to achieve its objectives. - I can evaluate the Tet Offensive's psychological impact on American public opinion. - I can explain the human cost of the Vietnam War including draft inequities, casualty figures, and the My Lai Massacre.

Narrative Text

How Did We Get There? The History of U.S. Involvement in Vietnam

To understand Vietnam, you must understand its history before American involvement. Vietnam had been a French colony for nearly a century. When France was defeated by Japanese forces during WWII, Vietnamese nationalists — led by Ho Chi Minh's communist Viet Minh movement — seized the opportunity to declare independence in 1945.

Ho Chi Minh's Declaration of Vietnamese Independence began with words directly quoted from the American Declaration of Independence: "All men are created equal. They are endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights." [VERIFY: Ho Chi Minh's September 2, 1945 Declaration of Independence — it does indeed open with language from the American Declaration of Independence; this is extensively documented; full text is public domain; this historical detail is politically significant and pedagogically powerful]

The United States nonetheless supported France's attempt to reestablish colonial control in Vietnam — prioritizing anti-communism over anti-colonialism. France was defeated at the Battle of Dien Bien Phu in May 1954. [VERIFY: Dien Bien Phu — May 7, 1954; French defeat; confirm]

The 1954 Geneva Accords temporarily divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel: Ho Chi Minh's communist Democratic Republic of Vietnam in the North; the U.S.-backed Republic of Vietnam in the South. The accords called for nationwide elections in 1956 to reunify the country — elections that the U.S. and South Vietnamese government blocked because they expected Ho Chi Minh would win. [VERIFY: 1956 elections — Eisenhower himself acknowledged in his memoirs that Ho Chi Minh would likely have won around 80% of the vote if elections had been held; confirm this specific claim]

Through the late 1950s and early 1960s, the U.S. sent "advisors" to support the South Vietnamese government. By 1963, there were approximately 16,000 American military advisors in Vietnam. [VERIFY: 16,000 advisors by 1963 — confirm against official military records]

The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution: The War's Disputed Beginning[CAUSATION]

On August 2, 1964, a North Vietnamese torpedo boat attacked the USS Maddox in the Gulf of Tonkin — a real incident. On August 4, 1964, the Johnson administration reported a second attack on the Maddox and the USS Turner Joy. [VERIFY: Gulf of Tonkin incidents — August 2 attack is confirmed; the August 4 "attack" is the disputed one; Secretary of Defense McNamara later acknowledged there was no August 4 attack; NSA intercepts declassified in 2005 confirmed this]

President Johnson used the (probably fabricated) second attack to ask Congress for authorization to "take all necessary measures" to repel attacks and prevent further aggression. Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution on August 7, 1964, with only two dissenting votes in the Senate (Wayne Morse of Oregon and Ernest Gruening of Alaska). [VERIFY: vote count — 88-2 in Senate; 416-0 in House; confirm; Morse and Gruening are the two Senate dissenters — confirm]

The resolution gave LBJ essentially unlimited authority to expand the war without a formal congressional declaration of war — a constitutionally significant delegation of power that would remain contested throughout and after the war. [TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — the constitutional question of war powers, Congress's role, and presidential authority is a direct civics content connection]

Escalation: Half a Million Troops and No Exit

After LBJ's 1964 re-election (won partly on the promise that American boys would not be sent to do what Asian boys should do for themselves), he rapidly escalated the war:

  • March 1965: First U.S. combat troops (Marines) land at Da Nang [VERIFY: March 8, 1965]
  • By 1968: 536,100 U.S. troops in Vietnam [VERIFY: peak troop level — approximately 536,000 in 1968; confirm against Department of Defense records]

The military strategy — "search and destroy" operations against Viet Cong guerrillas, measured by enemy "body counts" — was fundamentally flawed. The U.S. military was trying to apply conventional warfare logic to a guerrilla insurgency with deep popular support. Body counts as a measure of progress were easily manipulated; the Viet Cong could replace casualties; the war of attrition was one the U.S. could not win on its current terms.

The war was paid for in American lives: approximately 58,220 Americans died in Vietnam. [VERIFY: Vietnam War American deaths — 58,220 is the figure on the Vietnam Veterans Memorial wall; confirm this is the current official count, which has been periodically updated as records are processed]

The Draft and Its Inequities: The draft (Selective Service) was the mechanism for maintaining troop levels — but it was profoundly unequal in its application. College students could receive deferments; men with the right connections could secure positions in National Guard units that never deployed to Vietnam; wealthy families had access to medical deferments that poor families did not. [VERIFY: draft deferment inequities are extensively documented; cite a specific scholarly source, e.g., Christian Appy's Working-Class War: American Combat Soldiers and Vietnam (1993)]

The practical result: Vietnam was disproportionately a poor man's war. Black and Hispanic Americans — and working-class white Americans — served and died at higher rates than their college-educated counterparts. [VERIFY: racial and class disparities in Vietnam service — this is documented; early in the war, Black Americans were overrepresented in combat deaths (approximately 23% of deaths in 1965 when Black Americans were 11% of the population); this disparity decreased after NAACP protests led to more equitable assignments; confirm specific figures and the timeline of change; cite appropriate source]

This inequity was a direct driver of anti-war protest — particularly among Black activists who saw the draft as requiring Black men to fight for democracy abroad while denying it at home, reprising the Double V argument from WWII.

The Tet Offensive: The War on Television[CAUSATION]

On January 30, 1968 — the Vietnamese Lunar New Year holiday of Tet — the North Vietnamese Army and Viet Cong launched coordinated surprise attacks on over 100 South Vietnamese cities and towns, including the capital Saigon and the U.S. Embassy. [VERIFY: Tet Offensive date — January 30-31, 1968; over 100 cities; U.S. Embassy attack in Saigon; confirm]

The Tet Offensive was a military failure for the North Vietnamese — they were repulsed everywhere; they lost approximately 45,000 soldiers; the "general uprising" they hoped to trigger among the South Vietnamese population did not happen. [VERIFY: Tet North Vietnamese casualties — approximately 45,000 is commonly cited; confirm]

But it was a catastrophic political defeat for the Johnson administration.

For years, the administration had been telling the American public that the war was being won — the "light at the end of the tunnel" narrative. When Viet Cong fighters penetrated the U.S. Embassy compound in Saigon, those assurances were exposed as false. Television brought the images directly into American living rooms.

Walter Cronkite — the most trusted man in America according to polls at the time — returned from Vietnam and broadcast a famous editorial on February 27, 1968: "It seems now more certain than ever that the bloody experience of Vietnam is to end in a stalemate." [VERIFY: Cronkite's February 27, 1968 CBS News editorial — well-documented; confirm exact date and wording; the phrase "more certain than ever" appears in various cited versions — confirm against CBS News archives]

When LBJ saw Cronkite's broadcast, he reportedly said: "If I've lost Cronkite, I've lost middle America." [VERIFY: this LBJ quotation is widely cited but its exact provenance is disputed — some historians question whether LBJ said it exactly this way; present with appropriate attribution hedge]

On March 31, 1968, LBJ announced he would not seek re-election. The man who had won 61% of the vote just four years earlier was driven from office by a war he had chosen and could not win.

My Lai: When War Crimes Come to Light

On March 16, 1968 — six weeks after Tet — a U.S. Army unit led by Lieutenant William Calley entered the South Vietnamese village of My Lai and systematically murdered between 347 and 504 Vietnamese civilians — old men, women, children, infants. [VERIFY: My Lai Massacre — March 16, 1968; Lt. William Calley; casualty count is disputed (estimates range from 347 to over 500); the Army's Peers Report commissioned as an investigation found 347 killed; confirm] The massacre was covered up by the Army for over a year before reporter Seymour Hersh broke the story in November 1969. [VERIFY: Hersh's reporting — published November 12-13, 1969; confirm]

Calley was the only soldier convicted — of 22 counts of murder; he was sentenced to life in prison but was paroled after three years by President Nixon, whose decision generated enormous controversy. [VERIFY: Calley conviction and parole — convicted March 29, 1971; sentenced to life at hard labor; Nixon ordered his release pending appeal; Calley was paroled September 19, 1974; confirm all details]

My Lai forced Americans to confront what the war was actually doing to soldiers and to Vietnamese civilians. It was not an aberration — it was the product of a war strategy (search and destroy; body counts; difficulty distinguishing combatants from civilians in a guerrilla war) that created conditions for atrocity.

SECTION 8.2: PROTEST, NIXON, AND WATERGATE

Section Learning Targets: - I can describe the anti-war movement and analyze how it changed American politics. - I can explain Nixon's Vietnamization strategy and his approach to détente. - I can analyze Watergate — the break-in, the cover-up, and the constitutional crisis. - I can explain the 26th Amendment and connect it to Vietnam-era debates about citizenship and obligation.

Narrative Text

The Anti-War Movement: From Campus to Washington

The anti-war movement was not monolithic — it included students, veterans, civil rights leaders, religious figures, and ordinary citizens. But its most visible manifestation was on college campuses.

The Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), founded in 1962, issued the Port Huron Statement — a manifesto of the New Left that criticized both Cold War militarism and racial inequality. [VERIFY: SDS founding — 1962; Port Huron Statement — 1962; confirm]

The Teach-ins and Mobilization: Beginning in 1965, anti-war "teach-ins" at universities brought students and faculty together to discuss and debate the war. The movement grew steadily; by 1969, moratorium marches brought hundreds of thousands to Washington. [VERIFY: 1969 moratorium — the Vietnam Moratorium on October 15, 1969 was the largest single-day anti-war demonstration in American history; millions participated across the country; confirm]

Kent State (May 4, 1970): When Nixon announced the expansion of the war into Cambodia on April 30, 1970, protests erupted on campuses across the country. At Kent State University in Ohio, the National Guard was called in to control protests. On May 4, 1970, National Guard soldiers opened fire on the crowd, killing four students and wounding nine. [VERIFY: Kent State — May 4, 1970; 4 killed (Allison Krause, Jeffrey Miller, Sandra Scheuer, William Knox Schroeder); 9 wounded; confirm all names; the students killed were a mix of protesters and bystanders; confirm]

The photograph by John Filo of 14-year-old Mary Ann Vecchio kneeling over the body of Jeffrey Miller became one of the defining images of the anti-war era. [VERIFY: Filo's photograph and Vecchio's identity — this is well-documented; John Filo won a Pulitzer Prize for this photograph; Mary Ann Vecchio was 14 years old; confirm; the photograph is widely available; VERIFY copyright status for reproduction — Filo holds copyright; obtain reproduction rights from Valley Daily News/Getty Images or appropriate rights holder before printing]

Ten days after Kent State, two students were killed by police at Jackson State College in Mississippi — an event that received far less national attention, arguably because the victims were Black. [VERIFY: Jackson State — May 14-15, 1970; two killed; Phillip Lafayette Gibbs and James Earl Green; confirm]

The Pentagon Papers (1971): Daniel Ellsberg, a defense analyst who had worked on a top-secret Defense Department study of U.S. decision-making in Vietnam, leaked the documents — known as the Pentagon Papers — to the New York Times and Washington Post in June 1971.

The papers revealed that the government had consistently lied to the American public about the war's origins, prospects, and costs. Four administrations — Truman, Eisenhower, Kennedy, and Johnson — had escalated involvement while privately acknowledging the war could not be won. [VERIFY: Pentagon Papers — formally titled "History of U.S. Decision-Making in Vietnam, 1945-68"; leaked June 13, 1971; New York Times published June 13; Nixon administration sought injunction; Supreme Court ruled 6-3 in New York Times Co. v. United States (403 U.S. 713, 1971) against prior restraint; confirm all details]

The Nixon administration attempted to suppress publication through court injunctions — leading to the landmark Supreme Court case New York Times Co. v. United States (1971), in which the Court ruled 6-3 against prior restraint, affirming the First Amendment's protections for the press. [VERIFY: citation above; confirm]

Nixon's Vietnam: Vietnamization and "Peace with Honor"[CAUSATION]

Richard Nixon had won the 1968 election partly by implying he had a "secret plan" to end the war. His actual strategy — Vietnamization — was to gradually transfer combat responsibility to South Vietnamese forces while withdrawing American troops, buying time through negotiations.

Nixon also dramatically expanded the air war — including secret bombing of Cambodia and Laos — while publicly withdrawing ground troops. The combination produced the domestic political space to continue the war without the explosive domestic costs of maintaining troop levels, but it did not produce a military solution.

The Paris Peace Accords, signed January 27, 1973, provided for a ceasefire and the withdrawal of U.S. forces. Nixon called it "peace with honor." In fact, it was a face-saving exit. Within two years, North Vietnam had overrun the South; Saigon fell on April 30, 1975. [VERIFY: Paris Peace Accords — January 27, 1973; Saigon fell — April 30, 1975; confirm]

Détente: Simultaneously with Vietnam, Nixon — guided by National Security Advisor Henry Kissinger — pursued a policy of détente (French for "relaxation") with the Soviet Union and China. Nixon's 1972 visit to China — the first by an American president — was a stunning diplomatic reversal that exploited the Sino-Soviet split. [VERIFY: Nixon's China visit — February 21-28, 1972; first U.S. presidential visit to China; confirm]

The Strategic Arms Limitation Treaty (SALT I, 1972) put the first limits on the superpowers' nuclear arsenals. [VERIFY: SALT I — signed May 26, 1972; confirm]

Détente was Nixon's foreign policy achievement — a sophisticated acknowledgment that Cold War competition could be managed through negotiation rather than only through military confrontation.

Watergate: Break-In, Cover-Up, and Constitutional Crisis[CAUSATION]

On June 17, 1972, five men connected to the Committee to Re-Elect the President (CREEP) were arrested breaking into the Democratic National Committee headquarters at the Watergate hotel complex in Washington, D.C. [VERIFY: June 17, 1972; confirm]

Nixon won re-election in a landslide in November 1972. But investigative reporting by Washington Post journalists Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein — guided by a secret source known as "Deep Throat" (later revealed to be FBI Associate Director Mark Felt [VERIFY: Deep Throat revealed as Mark Felt — May 31, 2005; confirm]) — kept the story alive.

The Cover-Up: The scandal was not primarily the break-in itself — it was the cover-up. Evidence accumulated that Nixon and his aides had: - Used the CIA to obstruct the FBI's investigation of the break-in - Paid hush money to the Watergate burglars - Ordered the "plumbers" unit to conduct other illegal operations including breaking into Daniel Ellsberg's psychiatrist's office - Maintained an enemies list and used government agencies to harass political opponents - Recorded all conversations in the Oval Office on a secret taping system

The Saturday Night Massacre (October 20, 1973): Special Prosecutor Archibald Cox subpoenaed the Oval Office tape recordings. Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire Cox. Richardson refused and resigned. Deputy Attorney General William Ruckelshaus also refused and resigned. Solicitor General Robert Bork carried out the order. [VERIFY: Saturday Night Massacre — October 20, 1973; the sequence of resignations and firing is well-documented; confirm all names and their titles at the time]

The Saturday Night Massacre shocked the public and accelerated the constitutional crisis. Nixon's approval rating plummeted.

The Smoking Gun: The Supreme Court ruled unanimously in United States v. Nixon (1974) that Nixon had to surrender the tape recordings — executive privilege did not extend to evidence of criminal conduct. [VERIFY: United States v. Nixon — 418 U.S. 683 (1974); unanimous 8-0 decision (Rehnquist recused); decided July 24, 1974; confirm]

The released recordings revealed the "smoking gun" tape — a conversation from June 23, 1972 in which Nixon directed the CIA to obstruct the FBI's investigation. This was direct evidence of obstruction of justice.

The Resignation: Facing certain impeachment in the House and conviction in the Senate, Nixon resigned the presidency on August 9, 1974 — the only U.S. president ever to do so. [VERIFY: Nixon resignation — August 9, 1974; first and only presidential resignation; confirm]

Tennessee Connection — Howard Baker: Republican Senator Howard Baker of Huntsville, Tennessee was the ranking Republican on the Senate Watergate Committee. In the July 1973 hearings, Baker asked the question that defined the investigation: "What did the president know, and when did he know it?" [VERIFY: Baker's question — the specific date is July 29, 1973; some sources give different dates — confirm against the official Senate Watergate Committee hearing record; Baker is from Huntsville, Tennessee; confirm]

Baker's willingness to pursue the truth regardless of partisan considerations — he was a Republican pursuing a Republican president — became a symbol of the kind of constitutional courage that a healthy democracy requires. He is one of Tennessee's most important national political figures. [TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — Baker's Watergate role is a perfect case study for constitutional republic civics: separation of powers, congressional oversight of the executive, the principle that no one is above the law, and the role of individual conscience in constitutional accountability]

The 26th Amendment: Old Enough to Die, Old Enough to Vote

One of the Vietnam era's most direct domestic consequences was the passage of the 26th Amendment (ratified July 1, 1971), which lowered the voting age from 21 to 18. [VERIFY: 26th Amendment ratified — July 1, 1971; confirm]

The argument was simple and powerful: if 18-year-olds could be drafted to fight and die in Vietnam, they had the right to vote on whether to send people to war. The moral claim was identical to the Double V argument — you cannot ask people to sacrifice for a democracy that denies them full participation.

[TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — the 26th Amendment is a direct case study in how citizens in a constitutional republic use democratic processes to hold government accountable and expand democratic participation]

SECTION 8.3: SOCIAL MOVEMENTS AND THE 1970s CRISIS

Section Learning Targets: - I can describe the women's liberation movement and its legislative achievements including Title IX and the ERA debate. - I can explain the environmental movement and the creation of the EPA. - I can analyze the economic crises of the 1970s (stagflation, OPEC, energy crisis). - I can evaluate the Carter presidency — Camp David Accords and the Iran hostage crisis. - I can explain what a "crisis of confidence" means and how the 1970s created it.

Narrative Text

The Women's Liberation Movement

The Civil Rights Movement had demonstrated that organized collective action could change law and society. Women — many of whom had been active in the Civil Rights Movement and had experienced gender discrimination within the movement itself — applied these lessons to their own situation.

The Movement's Intellectual Foundation: Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963) identified what she called "the problem that has no name" — the suffocating constraint of the postwar domestic ideal that confined educated, capable women to housewifery and consumption. [VERIFY: publication date — 1963; confirm; NOTE on copyright: The Feminine Mystique is still under copyright; use brief excerpts only with proper attribution; do not reproduce extended passages without publisher's permission]

NOW and Organizational Politics: The National Organization for Women (NOW), founded in 1966, pursued legislative strategies similar to the NAACP's — using existing law, lobbying Congress, and filing discrimination complaints. [VERIFY: NOW founded — June 30, 1966; confirm]

Consciousness-Raising and Cultural Change: "Consciousness-raising" groups — small groups of women discussing their shared experiences — created the personal-is-political insight: that domestic arrangements, workplace treatment, and cultural expectations were political issues, not merely private choices.

Legislative Achievements: - Title IX (1972): Prohibited sex discrimination in any educational program receiving federal funding — transforming women's athletics, academic opportunities, and professional school access. [VERIFY: Title IX — Educational Amendments of 1972, signed June 23, 1972; confirm; connect to Tennessee: University of Tennessee women's athletics programs expanded dramatically after Title IX] - Equal Pay Act (1963): Required equal pay for equal work regardless of sex [VERIFY: 1963; already existed before the women's liberation movement peaked; note its limitations in practice] - Roe v. Wade (1973): The Supreme Court ruled 7-2 that women had a constitutional right to abortion in the first trimester; the decision remains one of the most contested in American constitutional history [VERIFY: Roe v. Wade — 410 U.S. 113 (1973); 7-2 decision; decided January 22, 1973; confirm; MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — present the genuine legal and moral arguments on both sides; this is and has always been deeply contested; do not take a position but present the competing constitutional and moral arguments accurately and fairly; NOTE: the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade in Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization, 597 U.S. 215 (2022) — connect to Unit 10 as preview]

The ERA: The Equal Rights Amendment — "Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex" — passed Congress in 1972 but was never ratified by the required 38 states. Phyllis Schlafly's "STOP ERA" movement mounted a successful conservative campaign against it, arguing the ERA would require women to be drafted, eliminate separate bathrooms, and undermine traditional families. [VERIFY: ERA passed Congress — March 22, 1972; ratification deadline originally 1979, extended to 1982; fell 3 states short; confirm; Schlafly's STOP ERA campaign — well-documented] [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES: Present both the feminist argument for the ERA (women need explicit constitutional equality protection) and the conservative argument against it (could produce unintended consequences; sufficiently addressed by existing law); both positions were held by sincere people making genuine arguments]

The Limits of the Movement: The women's liberation movement was substantially a white, middle-class movement that did not always account for the different experiences of women of color, working-class women, or women in the Global South. Black feminists including the Combahee River Collective argued for an intersectional understanding that the women's movement often failed to achieve. [VERIFY: Combahee River Collective Statement — published 1977; this is a significant primary source of feminist thought; the full statement is widely available; confirm if in public domain]

The Environmental Movement

The environmental movement emerged from a confluence of scientific awareness (Rachel Carson's Silent Spring, 1962, documenting the ecological effects of pesticides [VERIFY: Silent Spring — 1962; Carson documented DDT and other pesticides; still under copyright; brief excerpts only]), growing public concern about visible pollution (rivers catching fire, urban smog, oil spills), and the organizational models of the Civil Rights Movement.

Earth Day (April 22, 1970): Twenty million Americans participated in the first Earth Day — the largest civic demonstration in American history to that point. [VERIFY: Earth Day — April 22, 1970; 20 million participants; confirm]

Federal Environmental Legislation (1970-72): A remarkable burst of environmental legislation under Nixon — who was no environmentalist but recognized the political pressure — created the foundational structure of U.S. environmental law: - Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) established December 2, 1970 [VERIFY: EPA established by Executive Order 11514; December 2, 1970; confirm] - Clean Air Act (1970) — set national air quality standards [VERIFY: Clean Air Act Amendments of 1970 — December 31, 1970; confirm] - Clean Water Act (1972) — set standards for water quality [VERIFY: Federal Water Pollution Control Act Amendments of 1972 — October 18, 1972; confirm] - Endangered Species Act (1973) — protected species at risk of extinction

The Tennessee Connection — The Snail Darter: The Endangered Species Act had its most dramatic test in Tennessee. The little Tennessee River's snail darter — a small fish found only in that river — stood in the path of the TVA's Tellico Dam project. In Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill (1978), the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that the Endangered Species Act required halting the dam to protect the snail darter, regardless of the money already spent. [VERIFY: TVA v. Hill — 437 U.S. 153 (1978); the snail darter (Percina tanasi) was found only in the Little Tennessee River; Chief Justice Burger wrote the majority opinion; confirm all details; the dam was later built after Congress exempted the project from the ESA — confirm this legislative outcome]

The case became the defining test of whether environmental protection law would actually be enforced against major economic interests — and a Tennessee river, a tiny fish, and TVA were at the center of it.

The Economic Crises of the 1970s: Stagflation and the Energy Shock[CCOT]

The 1970s economic crises shattered the postwar economic consensus and created the conditions for Reagan's conservative revolution (Unit 9).

Stagflation: The word "stagflation" was coined because economists didn't have a word for what was happening — inflation and unemployment rising simultaneously. Keynesian economics held that inflation and unemployment moved in opposite directions; when inflation was high, unemployment should be low, and vice versa. Stagflation violated this understanding.

Its causes were multiple: the Vietnam War's deficit spending without a tax increase (LBJ's "guns and butter" strategy) had fueled inflation; Nixon's wage and price controls (1971) provided temporary relief that distorted markets; the oil shock produced simultaneous inflation and economic contraction. [VERIFY: Nixon's wage and price controls — announced August 15, 1971; Nixon's "New Economic Policy"; confirm]

The OPEC Oil Embargo (1973–1974): In October 1973, OPEC (the Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries) declared an embargo against the United States and other Western nations that had supported Israel in the Yom Kippur War. Oil prices quadrupled in a matter of months. [VERIFY: OPEC embargo — October 17, 1973; oil prices rose from approximately $3/barrel to $12/barrel; the embargo was lifted March 18, 1974; confirm all dates and price figures]

The oil shock revealed the profound vulnerability of the American economy — and the American lifestyle — to oil supplies the country did not control. Long lines at gas stations, odd-even rationing, speed limits lowered to 55 mph to conserve fuel — all became daily features of American life.

The Energy Crisis: The 1973 embargo was followed by a second oil shock in 1979, triggered by the Iranian Revolution. Gas prices doubled again; the economy contracted.

Carter's response included a national energy conservation program ("wearing a sweater" in the White House), deregulation of natural gas prices, and a push for renewable energy development — none of which provided immediate relief.

The Carter Years: From Camp David to Tehran

The Camp David Accords (September 1978): Carter's greatest foreign policy achievement: thirteen days of negotiations at Camp David, Maryland produced a framework for peace between Egypt and Israel. Egyptian President Anwar Sadat and Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin signed the Camp David Accords on September 17, 1978. [VERIFY: Camp David Accords — September 17, 1978; Sadat and Begin; Carter as mediator; confirm; Sadat and Begin shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 1979 — confirm] The Egyptian-Israeli peace treaty signed in March 1979 was the first peace treaty between Israel and an Arab nation — a historic achievement that Carter had made possible. [VERIFY: Egypt-Israel Peace Treaty — March 26, 1979; confirm]

The Iran Hostage Crisis (1979–1981): On November 4, 1979, Iranian student revolutionaries stormed the U.S. Embassy in Tehran and took 66 Americans hostage, demanding that the Shah of Iran — who the U.S. had admitted for cancer treatment — be returned to Iran for trial. Gulf of Tonkin (PSS 8.1): government misled Congress and the public; the beginning of the "credibility gap" that Vietnam produced. Nixon resignation (PSS 8.2): Nixon never admits wrongdoing even as he resigns; his framing shows the depth of the institutional failure — a president committed crimes and then constructed a narrative that denied any wrongdoing even in his final address. Baker (PSS 8.3): represents the constitutional republic's capacity for self-correction — a Republican pursuing a Republican president, asking the right question, prioritizing constitutional duty over partisan loyalty. Together: trust collapses when government systematically deceives (Vietnam, Nixon) and is partially rebuilt when institutions — press, Congress, courts — function as intended (Watergate resolution). Q10 Model Answer: Accept either position — women's liberation or environmental movement — with well-supported evidence. Most sophisticated position: The structural problems were real (executive power expansion, information asymmetry between government and citizens, Cold War militarism, fossil fuel dependency) AND the constitutional system showed it could address individual crises when institutions functioned — but the structural problems proved more durable, with consequences still unresolved in the present.

Critical Content Notes

Howard Baker — Required Tennessee Content: Every Tennessee student should know: Howard Baker was a Republican senator from Huntsville, Tennessee who served as ranking minority member of the Senate Watergate Committee. His question — "What did the president know, and when did he know it?" — became the defining standard of Watergate accountability and of congressional oversight more broadly. His willingness to pursue the truth regardless of partisan advantage represents constitutional courage. The Howard H. Baker Jr. Center for Public Policy at the University of Tennessee (Knoxville) is named in his honor and holds archival materials related to his career. [VERIFY: Baker Center at UT — confirm current status and contact information]

The Snail Darter Case — Tennessee Environmental Content: TVA v. Hill (1978) is the landmark Endangered Species Act case with Tennessee roots. The Little Tennessee River snail darter, the Tellico Dam project, and the Supreme Court's ruling that the ESA must be enforced regardless of sunk costs represents a genuinely important environmental law case with Tennessee at its center. The outcome — Congress later exempted the Tellico Dam project from the ESA — is also important: it shows both that courts can enforce environmental law AND that Congress can override that enforcement. Present both outcomes honestly.

Roe v. Wade — Handle with Maximum Care: Roe v. Wade was overturned in 2022 (Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization). This is among the most politically and morally contested issues in contemporary American society. The approach: (1) Present the historical decision accurately (1973, 7-2, constitutional right to abortion in the first trimester) (2) Present the genuine legal and moral arguments on both sides — the privacy/autonomy argument AND the right-to-life argument; both are held by sincere, serious people (3) Note that the Supreme Court overturned Roe in 2022 (this connects to Unit 10) (4) Do NOT advocate for either position; do NOT present one side as obviously correct (5) Focus on what the decision achieved legally, what the legal debates were, and what Dobbs did in 2022 as a preview of Unit 10 This is exactly the kind of content where the teacher's neutrality is most important and most professionally tested. [TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — citizens of a constitutional republic must understand how constitutional rights are established, contested, and overturned; this is a case study in constitutional change that serves this educational goal]

Vietnam Veterans — Sensitivity: Some students' family members served in Vietnam. Approach the war's costs — 58,220 Americans dead; 300,000+ wounded; hundreds of thousands with PTSD — with appropriate gravity and respect for service members regardless of your evaluation of the war's justification. The distinction between evaluating a war's wisdom and honoring those who fought it is essential to maintain.

TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — Constitutional Republic in This Unit: Unit 8 provides four major constitutional republic case studies: (1) Gulf of Tonkin Resolution — Congress's war powers vs. executive authority (2) 26th Amendment — democratic participation and citizen-government obligation (3) Watergate — executive privilege, congressional oversight, judicial independence, press freedom, no one above the law (4) War Powers Resolution — Congressional attempt to restore constitutional balance These are exactly the institutional mechanisms the statute intends students to understand.

26th Amendment (1971) | The constitutional amendment that lowered the voting age from 21 to 18, ratified July 1, 1971. The primary argument: if 18-year-olds could be drafted and die in Vietnam, they deserved the right to vote on whether to send people to war. The amendment directly connected citizenship obligation to democratic participation. | Unit 8, Section 8.2 [TCA § 49-6-1028(b)]

Baker, Howard (1925–2014) | Republican senator from Huntsville, Tennessee (1967–1985) who served as ranking minority member of the Senate Watergate Committee in 1973. His question — "What did the president know, and when did he know it?" — became the defining standard for congressional investigation of executive misconduct. His willingness to pursue truth regardless of partisan advantage represents a model of constitutional integrity. | Unit 8, Section 8.2; Primary Source Spotlight 8.3 [TENNESSEE CONTENT]

Camp David Accords (1978) | The framework for peace between Egypt and Israel negotiated by President Carter at Camp David, Maryland. Egyptian President Anwar Sadat and Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin signed on September 17, 1978. It was the first peace agreement between Israel and an Arab nation and Carter's most significant foreign policy achievement. | Unit 8, Section 8.3

"crisis of confidence" | President Carter's July 15, 1979 characterization of the American national mood — a loss of faith in government and institutions produced by Vietnam, Watergate, inflation, and the oil crisis. Carter was accurate in his diagnosis; his frank acknowledgment of the crisis proved politically disastrous, as voters preferred Reagan's optimistic alternative. | Unit 8, Section 8.3

détente | (day-TAHNT) | A relaxation of tension between the United States and the Soviet Union pursued by Nixon and Kissinger in the early 1970s. It included the SALT I arms limitation treaty (1972) and Nixon's historic visit to China (1972). Détente represented an acknowledgment that Cold War competition could be managed through negotiation rather than only through military confrontation. | Unit 8, Section 8.2

Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) | The federal regulatory agency established December 2, 1970 to enforce environmental law and protect air, water, and land quality. Created by President Nixon under pressure from the environmental movement following the first Earth Day. The EPA still exists and enforces environmental regulations today. | Unit 8, Section 8.3

Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964) | A joint resolution of Congress (Public Law 88-408) passed August 7, 1964, authorizing the president to take "all necessary measures" to repel attacks in Southeast Asia — in practice, unlimited authority to escalate the Vietnam War without a formal congressional declaration of war. The resolution was based partly on a fabricated August 4 attack on U.S. ships. It was repealed in 1971. | Unit 8, Section 8.1; Primary Source Spotlight 8.1

Howard Baker's Question | See Baker, Howard

Iran Hostage Crisis (1979–1981) | A diplomatic crisis that began November 4, 1979, when Iranian student revolutionaries seized the U.S. Embassy in Tehran and held 52 Americans hostage for 444 days. A rescue attempt failed in April 1980. The crisis dominated the final year of Carter's presidency and the hostages were released on January 20, 1981 — Reagan's inauguration day. | Unit 8, Section 8.3

My Lai Massacre (March 16, 1968) | The murder of 347 to 504 Vietnamese civilians — men, women, and children — by U.S. Army troops under Lieutenant William Calley in the village of My Lai. Covered up by the Army for over a year before reporter Seymour Hersh broke the story. Calley was convicted but paroled after three years. My Lai exemplified how a strategy of "search and destroy" and body counts created conditions for atrocity. | Unit 8, Section 8.1

OPEC oil embargo (1973–1974) | The Arab-dominated members of OPEC cut off oil exports to the United States and other Western nations that had supported Israel in the Yom Kippur War (October 1973). Oil prices quadrupled, producing gasoline shortages, long lines, and stagflation. The embargo revealed the profound vulnerability of the American economy to oil supplies it did not control. | Unit 8, Section 8.3

Pentagon Papers | A top-secret Defense Department study of U.S. decision-making in Vietnam, leaked by defense analyst Daniel Ellsberg to the New York Times in 1971. The papers revealed that four administrations had consistently lied to the public about the war's origins, prospects, and costs. The Nixon administration's attempt to suppress publication was rejected by the Supreme Court in New York Times Co. v. United States (1971). | Unit 8, Section 8.2

stagflation | (stag-FLAY-shun) | A combination of stagnant economic growth, high unemployment, and high inflation occurring simultaneously — a condition that Keynesian economic theory said should not exist. The 1970s stagflation was caused by Vietnam War deficit spending, Nixon's wage and price control distortions, and the OPEC oil shock. It shattered the postwar economic consensus. | Unit 8, Section 8.3

Tet Offensive (January 30, 1968) | The North Vietnamese Army and Viet Cong's coordinated surprise attacks on over 100 South Vietnamese cities and towns during the Tet holiday. A military defeat for North Vietnam but a catastrophic political defeat for the Johnson administration, which had been claiming the war was being won. It led LBJ to announce he would not seek re-election. | Unit 8, Section 8.1

Title IX (1972) | The section of the Education Amendments of 1972 that prohibits sex discrimination in any educational program or activity receiving federal funding. It transformed women's athletics, academic opportunities, and professional school access. It still governs sex discrimination in federally funded education today. | Unit 8, Section 8.3

TVA v. Hill (1978) | (Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill, 437 U.S. 153) The Supreme Court ruling (6-3) that the Endangered Species Act required halting construction of the Tellico Dam on the Little Tennessee River to protect the snail darter fish — regardless of the money already spent. Chief Justice Burger wrote the opinion. Congress later exempted the project from the ESA. The case established that environmental law would be enforced against major economic interests. | Unit 8, Section 8.3 [TENNESSEE CONTENT]

Vietnamization | (vee-et-nuh-muh-ZAY-shun) | Nixon's strategy of gradually transferring combat responsibility to South Vietnamese forces while withdrawing American troops. It provided the domestic political space to continue the war without the explosive costs of maintaining troop levels, but it did not produce a military solution. Saigon fell two years after U.S. withdrawal. | Unit 8, Section 8.2

War Powers Resolution (1973) | A federal law (Public Law 93-148) passed over Nixon's veto that requires the president to notify Congress within 48 hours of committing troops to conflict and limits unauthorized deployments to 60 days. It was Congress's attempt to restore the constitutional balance of war powers after the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution's open-ended delegation. Presidents have frequently contested its constitutionality. | Unit 8, Section 8.2

Watergate | The scandal that led to the only presidential resignation in American history. Beginning with the June 17, 1972 break-in at the Democratic National Committee headquarters, it encompassed obstruction of justice, abuse of the CIA and FBI, enemies lists, illegal campaign practices, and a systematic cover-up. Nixon resigned August 9, 1974. The scandal produced a permanent "trust gap" between Americans and their government. | Unit 8, Section 8.2; Primary Source Spotlights 8.2 and 8.3
