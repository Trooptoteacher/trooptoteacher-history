SECTION 5.1: THE CRASH — CAUSES AND CONSEQUENCES OF THE GREAT DEPRESSION

Section Learning Targets ("I Can" Statements): - I can explain multiple causes of the Great Depression using specific evidence, avoiding single-cause explanations. - I can analyze how Hoover's philosophy of "rugged individualism" shaped his response to the Depression and evaluate whether his response was adequate. - I can describe the Dust Bowl — its environmental causes, geographic scope, and human consequences. - I can connect the structural weaknesses of the 1920s economy (Unit 4) to the collapse of 1929–1933.

Narrative Text

The Floor Falls Out: October 1929

On October 24, 1929 — "Black Thursday" — the New York Stock Exchange began to unravel. Panicked investors rushed to sell shares that had been inflated far beyond their underlying value by years of speculation and margin buying. Prices plummeted. A consortium of major banks attempted to stabilize the market by making large purchases — it worked, briefly.

Five days later, on October 29, 1929 — Black Tuesday — the market collapsed catastrophically. Approximately 16 million shares were traded in a single day. [VERIFY: Black Tuesday trading volume — 16.4 million shares traded on October 29, 1929 is the widely cited figure; confirm against NYSE historical records] Prices fell so fast that the ticker tape could not keep up. Investors who had borrowed money to buy on margin found themselves owing more than their portfolios were worth. Fortunes evaporated in hours.

But the stock market crash, by itself, did not cause the Great Depression. The crash was the trigger, not the cause. Understanding the Depression requires understanding the structural conditions that made the entire economy fragile — conditions that had been building throughout the 1920s and before.

Multiple Causes: Why the Depression Was So Deep and So Long[CAUSATION]

The Great Depression was the product of multiple converging failures — financial, agricultural, policy, and structural. Students and textbooks sometimes reduce it to a single cause; the historical reality is far more complex.

1. The Speculation Bubble and Financial Fragility: As we examined in Unit 4, the 1920s stock market had been inflated by speculative buying — investors purchasing stocks with borrowed money (margin buying) and expecting prices to keep rising indefinitely. By 1929, stock prices were fundamentally disconnected from the actual earning capacity of the companies they represented. A speculative bubble, once punctured, cannot be reinflated gradually — it collapses.

When the bubble burst, investors who had bought on margin were wiped out. Brokers called in loans. Banks that had lent money to speculators found their loan portfolios suddenly worthless. This financial chain reaction spread from Wall Street to Main Street with devastating speed.

2. Banking System Fragility: The United States in 1929 had approximately 25,000 banks — most of them small, poorly capitalized, and inadequately regulated. [VERIFY: 25,000 banks figure — widely cited; confirm against Federal Reserve historical data] When depositors panicked and rushed to withdraw their savings simultaneously — a bank run — banks that held insufficient reserves could not meet the demand and failed.

Between 1930 and 1933, approximately 9,000 American banks failed. [VERIFY: bank failure figures — commonly cited range; confirm against FDIC historical data or Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz's A Monetary History of the United States] When a bank failed, depositors lost their savings entirely — there was no federal deposit insurance (the FDIC was created in 1933 as a New Deal reform). The failure of banks destroyed both savings and the credit system that businesses needed to operate and hire workers.

3. The Smoot-Hawley Tariff (1930): In June 1930, Congress passed and President Hoover signed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff, raising average tariffs on imported goods to approximately 45%. [VERIFY: Smoot-Hawley average tariff rate — varies by category; the effective rate on dutiable imports was approximately 45–50%; confirm against historical trade data] The logic was to protect American industries and farmers from foreign competition. The result was catastrophic.

Trading partners retaliated with their own tariffs on American goods. International trade collapsed — U.S. exports fell by approximately two-thirds between 1929 and 1933. [VERIFY: U.S. export decline — widely cited figure; confirm against Department of Commerce historical data] Industries that depended on exports shed workers. The global economy contracted as the trade war deepened. Most economists consider Smoot-Hawley one of the most consequential policy mistakes in American economic history. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES: some economists argue Smoot-Hawley's effect is overstated; present with appropriate nuance]

4. Overproduction and Underconsumption: American factories had been producing more goods than consumers could sustainably buy — even with installment credit propping up demand. When consumers lost income and confidence, they stopped buying. When consumers stopped buying, factories reduced production and laid off workers. When workers lost jobs, they could not buy anything. This downward spiral — economists call it a deflationary spiral or demand collapse — was self-reinforcing and extremely difficult to reverse.

Agricultural overproduction had been depressing farm prices throughout the 1920s. Farmers were already in a debt crisis before 1929. The Depression made it total.

5. The Federal Reserve's Fatal Mistake: The Federal Reserve — created in 1913 to prevent banking panics — responded to the 1929 crash by contracting the money supply rather than expanding it. This was a catastrophic policy error. By raising interest rates and reducing credit availability at the exact moment the economy needed liquidity, the Fed deepened and extended the Depression.

Economists Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz argued in their landmark 1963 study A Monetary History of the United States that the Federal Reserve's errors transformed what might have been a severe recession into the Great Depression. [VERIFY: Friedman and Schwartz thesis — well-documented in economic historiography; cite appropriately; note this is one influential interpretation; Keynesian and other economists offer different emphases]

6. Income Inequality: The extreme income inequality of the 1920s (documented in Unit 4) meant that consumer spending was concentrated in a relatively small number of wealthy households. When those households lost wealth in the crash, their spending fell dramatically. The mass of ordinary workers — who had not shared proportionally in 1920s prosperity — had little cushion to fall back on.

The Human Reality: What the Depression Looked Like[CAUSATION]

Numbers alone cannot convey what the Depression meant to real people. At its depth in 1932–1933:

  • Unemployment reached approximately 25% nationally — one in four American workers had no job. [VERIFY: peak unemployment figure — commonly cited as 24–25%; confirm against Bureau of Labor Statistics historical data; note that data collection methods were different then and the precise figure is debated by economic historians]
  • Industrial production fell by approximately 50% from 1929 levels. [VERIFY]
  • GDP contracted by approximately 30%. [VERIFY: GDP contraction — widely cited; confirm against Bureau of Economic Analysis historical data]
  • Bank deposits evaporated; savings accumulated over lifetimes were wiped out in days when banks failed.
  • Farm foreclosures occurred at epidemic rates; farmers who had worked land for generations were evicted by banks.
  • Hoovervilles — shantytowns built from scavenged materials on vacant lots and in parks — appeared in cities across the country. The name was a bitter tribute to the president widely blamed for the catastrophe.

The Depression fell hardest on those with the least: Black Americans, already economically marginalized by Jim Crow and discrimination, saw unemployment rates that may have been twice the national average in some cities. [VERIFY: Black unemployment rates during the Depression — double the white rate is a frequently cited estimate; confirm against available historical data; note that data segregated by race is incomplete] Mexican Americans faced federal "repatriation" campaigns — deportation drives that expelled approximately 500,000 people, including many U.S. citizens of Mexican descent, from the country. [VERIFY: Mexican repatriation figure — estimates range from 500,000 to over 1 million; confirm against scholarly sources such as Francisco Balderrama and Raymond Rodríguez, Decade of Betrayal: Mexican Repatriation in the 1930s (2006)]

Hoover's Response: "Rugged Individualism" and Its Limits[CAUSATION]

Herbert Hoover was not an uncaring man or an unintelligent one. As Secretary of Commerce and then as president, he had been considered one of the most capable administrators in American government. But his philosophical convictions made it impossible for him to respond adequately to the Depression.

Hoover believed deeply in what he called "rugged individualism" — the distinctively American tradition of self-reliance, voluntary cooperation, and private initiative. Government intervention in the economy, he argued, was a European approach that would destroy American character and freedom.

In a 1928 campaign speech (before the crash), Hoover articulated this philosophy: we are "nearer to the final triumph over poverty than ever before in the history of any land." [VERIFY: this Hoover quotation — commonly cited from his October 22, 1928 campaign speech; confirm against American Presidency Project or Hoover Presidential Library; note it was delivered before the Depression and became bitterly ironic afterward]

Hoover's responses to the Depression were not nothing: - He created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC) in 1932 to loan money to banks, railroads, and other businesses — "trickle-down" relief that would theoretically prevent business failures and protect jobs - He organized voluntary employer agreements to maintain wages (which mostly failed) - He organized private charity campaigns (which were wholly inadequate to the scale of need) - He opposed — and vetoed — direct federal relief payments to individuals as a violation of American principles of self-reliance

The RFC was too little, too late, and directed at institutions rather than people. Voluntary charity could not fill a gap created by a 25% unemployment rate. And Hoover's most catastrophic decision — signing the Smoot-Hawley Tariff in 1930 despite a petition from over 1,000 economists urging him to veto it — deepened the crisis he was trying to alleviate. [VERIFY: economist petition against Smoot-Hawley — the petition is well-documented; confirm the number; approximately 1,028 economists signed a petition; confirm against historical records]

The Bonus Army (1932): In the summer of 1932, approximately 17,000 World War I veterans — the "Bonus Army" or "Bonus Expeditionary Force" — marched to Washington, D.C. to demand early payment of service bonuses that Congress had promised but not yet paid out (scheduled for 1945). Many were destitute, and they camped in makeshift settlements along the Anacostia River.

Hoover ordered the Army to clear the camps. General Douglas MacArthur — accompanied by his aide Major Dwight Eisenhower — used cavalry, infantry with fixed bayonets, and tanks to drive the veterans from their camps. The camps were burned.

The image of the U.S. Army attacking homeless World War I veterans on the streets of Washington, D.C. — with bayonets and tear gas — was politically devastating for Hoover. It symbolized everything Americans felt he had done wrong: protecting institutions and banks while turning force against suffering people.

[VERIFY: Bonus Army details — July 28, 1932; MacArthur's role is well-documented; Eisenhower's presence is documented; the burning of the camps is documented; confirm specific numbers of marchers and any specific casualty figures before citing; cite a specific historical source, e.g., Paul Dickson and Thomas Allen's The Bonus Army: An American Epic (2004)]

The Dust Bowl: Environmental Catastrophe Meets Economic Catastrophe[CAUSATION]

Simultaneously with the financial crisis, the Great Plains of the United States was experiencing one of the worst environmental disasters in American history. A combination of drought, extreme heat, and decades of poor agricultural practices — specifically the destruction of native grass cover through aggressive plowing — created conditions in which topsoil became loose, dry, and vulnerable to wind erosion.

In the early 1930s, massive dust storms — called "black blizzards" — began sweeping across the southern Great Plains. A dust storm on April 14, 1935 — "Black Sunday" — stretched from the Canadian border to Texas, turned noon into darkness, and buried farmsteads under feet of red and brown dust. [VERIFY: Black Sunday — April 14, 1935; well-documented; the dust cloud reached as far east as the Atlantic Ocean; confirm specific details]

The Dust Bowl region — centered on the Oklahoma and Texas panhandles, western Kansas, southeastern Colorado, and northeastern New Mexico — became uninhabitable for tens of thousands of farming families. Approximately 3.5 million people abandoned the Great Plains during the 1930s. [VERIFY: Dust Bowl migration figure — estimates vary; confirm against available demographic data; Isabel Wilkerson and other historians have examined this migration]

These migrants — called "Okies" regardless of their actual state of origin — traveled to California and other western states, finding not the promised land they hoped for but labor camps, exploitative work conditions, and hostility from established residents who saw them as economic competition.

John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath (1939) — which tells the story of the Joad family's migration from Oklahoma to California — is the definitive literary document of the Dust Bowl experience. [NOTE: The Grapes of Wrath is still under copyright; use brief excerpts only with proper attribution; the novel is widely available in school and public libraries; short excerpts for educational analysis are generally fair use — VERIFY with copyright guidelines before extended reproduction in a commercial textbook]

The Environmental Causes — What Humans Did Wrong: The Dust Bowl was not purely a natural disaster. It was substantially caused by agricultural practices that were ecologically unsustainable: - The Homestead Act had driven settlers onto Great Plains land that received insufficient rainfall for conventional farming - "Suitcase farming" — plowing huge tracts of native grassland by absentee landowners focused on short-term profit — destroyed the root systems that held soil in place - The drought conditions of the early 1930s were severe but not unprecedented; what was unprecedented was the scale of human-caused vulnerability

The New Deal's Soil Conservation Service (created 1935) [VERIFY: establishment date — the Soil Erosion Service was created in 1933; renamed the Soil Conservation Service in 1935; confirm] addressed the Dust Bowl by paying farmers to plant cover crops, implement contour plowing, and restore grasslands — an early federal environmental protection program.

SECTION 5.2: FDR'S NEW DEAL — SAVING CAPITALISM FROM ITSELF

Section Learning Targets: - I can explain Roosevelt's New Deal using the "Three Rs" framework and identify specific programs in each category. - I can describe and evaluate the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) as a New Deal program with specific impact on Tennessee. - I can analyze critics of the New Deal from both the left (Huey Long, Father Coughlin) and the right (American Liberty League) and explain what each critique reveals about American political values. - I can evaluate the New Deal's constitutional crisis and FDR's court-packing plan. - I can explain the Social Security Act and evaluate its long-term significance.

Narrative Text

The Man Who Saved Capitalism — Maybe

Franklin Delano Roosevelt won the presidential election of 1932 by the largest margin since 1920, carrying 42 of 48 states and winning 57% of the popular vote. [VERIFY: 1932 election results — Roosevelt won 42 states and 57.4% of the popular vote; confirm against official election records]

Americans did not elect Roosevelt because they had a clear picture of what he would do. They elected him because Hoover had failed, and FDR projected optimism, confidence, and the willingness to experiment. His First Inaugural Address (March 4, 1933) set the tone:

"the only thing we have to fear is fear itself." [PUBLIC DOMAIN — FDR's First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1933; one of the most quoted phrases in American political history; full text at American Presidency Project, Miller Center; National Archives; DEVELOPER: this is confirmed public domain; reproduce this exact passage with citation; Full text in Appendix C]

Roosevelt's New Deal was not a coherent ideological program. It was, as FDR himself said, "bold, persistent experimentation" — trying things, discarding what didn't work, and trying something else. [VERIFY: FDR's "bold, persistent experimentation" quotation — from his May 22, 1932 speech at Oglethorpe University; confirm against American Presidency Project]

This pragmatic approach frustrated both conservatives (who wanted less government) and radicals (who wanted more fundamental change). But it produced the most consequential expansion of federal government in American history.

The First Hundred Days: An Unprecedented Burst of Legislation[CHRONOLOGY]

Between March 4 and June 16, 1933 — FDR's first hundred days — Congress passed fifteen major pieces of legislation at the president's request. Nothing like it had been seen before in peacetime. [VERIFY: "fifteen major pieces of legislation" in the Hundred Days — this is the widely cited number; confirm the specific legislation enacted in the Hundred Days against a list; the number varies slightly depending on what is counted]

Banking Emergency: On his first day in office, FDR declared a four-day national bank holiday, temporarily closing every bank in the country to stop the cascading bank runs. The Emergency Banking Act was passed within hours of Congress convening — members voted on a bill they had not read because the crisis was too acute. When banks reopened, FDR explained what had happened in the first of his "fireside chats" — radio addresses directly to the American people in plain, accessible language. Bank deposits recovered immediately; the public trusted FDR's calm reassurance.

Glass-Steagall Act / FDIC (1933): The Glass-Steagall Act separated commercial banking (taking deposits, making loans) from investment banking (speculating in securities) — addressing one of the structural causes of the banking collapse. It also created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), which insured bank deposits up to $2,500 (later raised dramatically). [VERIFY: original FDIC deposit insurance limit — $2,500 for 1933; confirm; current limit is $250,000] With FDIC insurance, bank runs became much less likely — depositors no longer had reason to panic when a bank was rumored to be in trouble. The FDIC still exists and still insures your bank deposits today.

Securities Exchange Commission (SEC, 1934): The SEC was created to regulate the stock market and prevent the fraudulent and speculative practices that had inflated and then burst the 1929 bubble. [VERIFY: SEC created 1934 — the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 created the SEC; confirm date] The SEC still exists and regulates U.S. securities markets today.

The Three Rs: Relief, Recovery, and Reform[COMPARISON]

Historians typically organize New Deal programs into three overlapping categories:

RELIEF — Immediate help for people in need:

Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC, 1933): The CCC employed young men (ages 18–25) in national parks, forests, and public lands, planting trees, building trails, fighting wildfires, and constructing facilities. [VERIFY: age range — 17–28 is sometimes cited; confirm the official enrollment range] At its peak, the CCC employed approximately 500,000 men at a time. [VERIFY: peak enrollment figure — widely cited; confirm against National Park Service or CCC historical records] Men sent most of their pay ($25 per month, with $22 sent home to their families) home, providing income to families in desperate need. [VERIFY: CCC pay and remittance figures — commonly cited; confirm] The CCC was racially segregated; Black enrollees served in separate units, often with inferior facilities and assignments. [TCA § 49-6-1006 — racial discrimination in New Deal programs must be addressed; VERIFY specific documentation]

Federal Emergency Relief Administration (FERA, 1933): Direct federal grants to states for relief payments — the first direct federal relief program in American history. This was the program Hoover had refused to create on principle. It provided direct cash and food relief to millions of unemployed Americans.

RECOVERY — Getting the economy moving again:

National Recovery Administration (NRA, 1933): The NRA established industry-wide codes setting minimum wages, maximum hours, and fair competition practices, with businesses that complied displaying the "Blue Eagle" symbol. It was the most ambitious attempt to manage the industrial economy ever undertaken in peacetime. [VERIFY: NRA Blue Eagle program — well-documented] The NRA was struck down by the Supreme Court in Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States (1935) [VERIFY: Schechter Poultry — 295 U.S. 495 (1935); the "sick chicken case"; the Court ruled 9-0 that the NRA unconstitutionally delegated legislative power to the executive and exceeded Congress's commerce clause authority; confirm citation and ruling details] as an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power.

Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA, 1933): The AAA paid farmers to reduce production of major crops (wheat, cotton, corn, tobacco, hogs) to drive up prices. The logic: farm overproduction was depressing prices; reducing supply would increase prices and restore farm income. The AAA's immediate problem: to reduce cotton supply, it paid farmers to plow under already-planted crops — while people were going hungry. This apparent contradiction provoked intense criticism. The AAA also benefited large farm operators disproportionately and was administered in ways that discriminated against Black tenant farmers and sharecroppers, who were often cheated out of their payments or evicted to allow landlords to receive larger payments. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES; TCA § 49-6-1006] [VERIFY: racial discrimination in AAA administration is well-documented; cite specific scholarly source, e.g., Jill Watts's work or Harvard Sitkoff's A New Deal for Blacks]

REFORM — Structural changes to prevent another Depression:

The Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA, 1933): [TENNESSEE REQUIRED CONTENT] See dedicated TVA section below.

Works Progress Administration (WPA, 1935): The WPA was the largest and most comprehensive New Deal jobs program. Between 1935 and 1943, it employed approximately 8.5 million Americans on projects including: [VERIFY: WPA employment figure — widely cited; confirm against official records] - Construction and repair of roads, bridges, schools, hospitals, airports, parks - The Federal Writers' Project — unemployed writers produced state and local guidebooks, oral histories, and the first systematic documentation of formerly enslaved people's testimonies (the Slave Narratives Project) - The Federal Art Project — murals in public buildings, posters, sculpture - The Federal Theatre Project — theatrical productions, often with political content that eventually made Congress uncomfortable - The Federal Music Project — orchestras, music education programs

The WPA also engaged in racial discrimination — Black workers were often assigned to lower-paying jobs, denied supervisory positions, and excluded from some programs in the South. [TCA § 49-6-1006; VERIFY: WPA racial discrimination — well-documented]

The Social Security Act (1935): The Social Security Act created the framework of the American social safety net. It established: - Old-Age Insurance (what we call "Social Security") — a federal insurance program funded by payroll taxes on workers and employers that provides monthly retirement benefits - Unemployment Insurance — a federal-state program providing temporary income to workers who lose their jobs - Aid to Dependent Children — federal grants to states for assistance to single-parent families (the precursor to welfare programs)

The Social Security Act was the most consequential piece of domestic legislation in American history since the Reconstruction Amendments. It permanently established the principle that the federal government has a responsibility to protect citizens from the economic risks of old age and unemployment.

Critical limitation: The original Social Security Act excluded domestic workers and agricultural workers from old-age insurance — categories that included the overwhelming majority of Black workers in the South. This exclusion was not accidental; Southern Democrats in Congress demanded it as a condition of their support. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES; TCA § 49-6-1006; VERIFY: the exclusion of domestic and agricultural workers and its racial consequences is extensively documented; cite Robert C. Lieberman's Shifting the Color Line (1998) or Ira Katznelson's Fear Itself (2013)]

National Labor Relations Act (Wagner Act, 1935): The Wagner Act guaranteed workers the right to organize unions and collectively bargain, and created the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) to enforce these rights. It was the most powerful legal protection for labor organizing in American history and directly reversed the federal government's tendency (seen in the Pullman Strike of 1894, Unit 2) to use its power against organized labor. Union membership surged after 1935, particularly through the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO), which organized industrial workers regardless of skill (a broader approach than the AFL's craft unionism).

The Tennessee Valley Authority: New Deal in Tennessee[CONTEXTUALIZATION]

[TENNESSEE REQUIRED CONTENT]

No New Deal program is more specifically and durably connected to Tennessee than the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA), established by the TVA Act (May 18, 1933) and signed by President Roosevelt.

What the TVA Did: The Tennessee River Valley in 1933 was one of the most economically depressed regions in the United States. It suffered from chronic poverty, periodic devastating floods, exhausted farmland, and virtually no electricity — in 1933, only about 3% of rural Tennessee farms had electricity. [VERIFY: rural electrification rate in Tennessee before TVA — commonly cited as less than 5% for rural areas nationally; confirm Tennessee-specific figure against TVA or Census data]

The TVA was a unified, regional approach to economic development on a scale never attempted before:

Hydroelectric power: The TVA built a system of dams on the Tennessee River and its tributaries, generating electricity and providing flood control. Eventually the TVA system included 29 dams. [VERIFY: TVA dam count — varies depending on what is counted and the time period; confirm against TVA official records] Electricity was provided to rural areas at rates lower than private utilities had ever offered, making possible the electrification of farms and homes across the region.

Flood control: Before the TVA, the Tennessee River flooded catastrophically every few years, destroying crops and homes. The TVA dam system regulated river flow, essentially ending the major floods that had plagued the valley for generations. [VERIFY: TVA flood control record — well-documented; confirm specific pre-TVA flood events and post-TVA flood prevention record against TVA historical data]

Navigation: The TVA created a nine-foot deep navigation channel along 650 miles of the Tennessee River, opening the inland valley to commercial river traffic. [VERIFY: 650 miles and nine-foot depth — commonly cited; confirm against TVA]

Economic development: Cheap electricity and flood control made the Tennessee Valley attractive to industry. Manufacturing plants — including, critically, the aluminum plants and later the Manhattan Project facilities at Oak Ridge (Unit 6) — were drawn to the region by TVA power.

Land management: The TVA operated demonstration farms, distributed fertilizer, promoted soil conservation, and worked with farmers to improve agricultural practices.

The TVA as Political Lightning Rod: The TVA was — and in some ways remains — enormously controversial. Conservatives and private utilities accused the TVA of socialist competition with private business, of driving private utilities out of business through artificially subsidized rates, and of expanding the federal government beyond its constitutional role. [TCA § 49-6-1028(b) and § 49-6-1205 — the TVA represents the New Deal's most direct challenge to the principle that utility provision is a private market function; students should understand both the genuine achievements of the TVA and the principled arguments of its critics]

Private utilities were correct that the TVA's government backing gave it advantages they could not match. The philosophical question — should the government own and operate utilities that could theoretically be provided by private markets? — is a recurring question in American political economy. The Populists (Unit 2) had demanded government ownership of railroads on similar grounds. The debate about the TVA is the same debate in a different decade.

The TVA Today: The TVA still provides electricity to approximately 10 million customers across a seven-state region, including much of Tennessee. It is headquartered in Knoxville. It is one of the largest public utilities in the United States and one of the New Deal's most enduring institutions. [VERIFY: TVA current service area, customer count, and headquarters location; TVA annual reports and website provide current data; verify specific figures]

Tennessee-Specific TVA Teaching Opportunities: - Students can visit actual TVA dams — Norris Dam (the first TVA dam, completed 1936) [VERIFY: Norris Dam completion date — December 1936; confirm] is near Norris, Tennessee, approximately 25 miles north of Knoxville - TVA-built communities (Norris, Tennessee was the first planned TVA community) still exist [VERIFY: Norris, Tennessee as planned TVA community — well-documented] - The Tennessee State Library and Archives holds extensive TVA records and photographs from the construction era - Before-and-after maps and photographs of the Tennessee River Valley are powerful visual primary sources

The New Deal's Critics: Too Much and Not Enough[CCOT]

The New Deal was attacked from both directions — by conservatives who thought it had gone too far in expanding government, and by populists who thought it had not gone far enough in challenging concentrated wealth.

Critics from the Right: The American Liberty League: The American Liberty League, funded primarily by wealthy businessmen (including members of the du Pont family), argued that the New Deal was socialist, that it violated constitutional limits on federal power, and that it was destroying the free enterprise system. [VERIFY: American Liberty League founding and prominent members — well-documented; founded 1934; du Pont family funding is documented; confirm specific facts against scholarly sources]

Their concerns were not entirely without merit — the NRA and AAA represented unprecedented federal intervention in private economic decisions. The Supreme Court agreed with some of their constitutional arguments, striking down multiple New Deal programs. [TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — the Liberty League's arguments represent one end of the recurring American debate about capitalism, government, and economic freedom; present accurately and fairly alongside New Deal arguments]

Critics from the Left:

Huey Long (1893–1935) — "Share Our Wealth": Huey Long, the populist governor and then senator from Louisiana, was one of the most dangerous political figures FDR ever faced — dangerous because Long was potentially a more radical alternative to FDR among desperate voters, not because Long was wrong about everything.

The New Deal's Legacy: A Changed America[CCOT]

How should we evaluate the New Deal? Did it end the Great Depression? Was it a success? These questions do not have simple answers, and historians continue to debate them. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — the historical assessment of the New Deal is a genuine ongoing scholarly debate; present multiple interpretations fairly]

What the New Deal Did NOT Do: The New Deal did NOT end the Great Depression. Unemployment remained in double digits throughout the 1930s — even after years of New Deal programs, unemployment was approximately 14–15% in 1937–1938. [VERIFY: 1937–38 unemployment figures — widely cited; confirm against BLS historical data] The "Roosevelt Recession" of 1937–1938 — triggered when FDR cut spending and raised taxes in an attempt to balance the budget — demonstrated that the New Deal had been managing the Depression but had not cured it. Recovery to full employment did not occur until WWII military spending (Unit 6).

What the New Deal Did Achieve: - Saved the banking system from total collapse - Provided relief to millions of desperate people - Created lasting institutions (FDIC, SEC, Social Security, TVA) that still function - Established the principle of federal responsibility for economic welfare - Transformed the relationship between citizens and government permanently - Gave ordinary Americans a stake in the federal government's continuation

The Keynesian Interpretation: British economist John Maynard Keynes argued — and many New Deal economists believed — that the solution to the Depression was deficit spending: the government should borrow and spend far more than it was taking in through taxes, "priming the pump" of economic demand until private investment recovered.

FDR was uncomfortable with large deficits and never fully embraced Keynesian spending levels. This, many economists argue, is why the New Deal managed but could not end the Depression. When WWII required spending of a scale that made New Deal programs look modest, the unemployment problem was solved in months. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES: the debate over whether more aggressive fiscal policy could have ended the Depression sooner is genuinely contested; present both the Keynesian interpretation and the concerns about government debt honestly; this connects to TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — the role of government in the economy]

The New Deal's Permanent Transformation: Regardless of debates about its economic effectiveness, the New Deal permanently transformed American political culture: - Americans came to expect the federal government to actively manage economic conditions and provide a social safety net - The Democratic Party became identified with an activist government supporting working people and the poor — a coalition that held together through the 1960s - The Republican Party became identified with opposition to this expansion — a debate that defines American politics through the present day - Federal regulatory agencies (FDIC, SEC, NLRB, FTC) became permanent features of economic life

Every subsequent debate about government's role in the economy — from Johnson's Great Society (Unit 7) to Reagan's deregulation (Unit 9) to the 2008 bailout (Unit 10) — takes place on the terrain the New Deal created.

The New Deal and Race — A Necessary Limitation: Any honest evaluation of the New Deal must acknowledge its racial failures. The New Deal disproportionately excluded Black Americans from its benefits through: - Exclusion of domestic and agricultural workers from Social Security and labor protections - Racial discrimination in local administration of relief and jobs programs - The AAA's displacement of Black tenant farmers - FDR's refusal to support federal anti-lynching legislation, out of fear of alienating Southern Democratic senators whose votes he needed for economic legislation

The New Deal represented a genuine improvement in many Black Americans' lives through direct relief and employment. But it also reinforced racial inequalities by building the social safety net around exclusions specifically designed to protect Jim Crow. This tells us: New Deal built the safety net around racial exclusions; federal benefits were more available to white workers than Black workers; racial inequality was embedded in the very structure of the New Deal's legacy institutions.

Q6 Model Answer: "Saving capitalism" claim is well-supported with nuance. Ways it saved capitalism: FDIC prevented total banking collapse; SEC prevented stock market fraud from destroying investor confidence; Wagner Act gave workers a legitimate stake in the system (reducing appeal of socialist/communist alternatives); Social Security gave ordinary workers security within the capitalist system. Ways it constrained capitalism: NRA set prices/wages; TVA competed directly with private utilities; Wagner Act gave unions real power over private business; heavy financial regulation constrained banks and securities firms. Q8 Model Answer: Was FDR right that the Court blocked democratic will? Socratic Seminar — Unit 5

Preparation: Students read the FDR First Inaugural Address excerpt (Primary Source 5.1) and the American Liberty League document (Document 3 of the DBQ) from Appendix C. Students bring one text-based question on each document.

Central Question: "When the free market catastrophically fails, what obligations does the government have to its citizens, and where are the limits of those obligations? Use evidence from the primary sources and the unit narrative to argue your position."

Opening (text-grounded): 1. What specific obligations does FDR claim the government has in the First Inaugural Address? What evidence does he provide that private charity and individual action have been insufficient? 2. What specific limits does the American Liberty League place on government obligation? What do they fear will happen if those limits are ignored?

Middle rounds (analytical): 3. FDR says the "money changers" caused the crisis. The Liberty League says the crisis requires government to respect the limits of its authority. Who is more right? What does the specific historical evidence of the Depression tell us about who caused the crisis and who was responsible for responding? 4. The Social Security Act (a permanent New Deal institution) excluded domestic and agricultural workers. Given FDR's stated commitment to helping those in need, how do you account for this exclusion? What does it suggest about whose "citizens" FDR was primarily obligated to protect? 5. Looking forward from 1933 with knowledge of what the New Deal built (and what WWII spending ultimately did): was the New Deal's level of intervention — large by historical standards, modest by what was ultimately needed — the right calibration, or should FDR have gone further?

Closing: 6. The essential question of this unit — "How do we balance economic freedom with economic security?" — remains the central debate in American politics today. Has the historical evidence of the 1930s settled the question, or does each generation have to answer it fresh? What does the Depression-era evidence tell us that should be permanent?

Structured Academic Controversy (SAC) — Unit 5

Topic: Was the New Deal the right response to the Great Depression?

Position A: The New Deal Was the Right Response Arguments to develop: - The banking system was on the edge of total collapse; immediate government action was the only force available to stabilize it - Private charity could not remotely meet the scale of human need at 25% unemployment — only the federal government had the resources - Permanent institutions (FDIC, Social Security, SEC) created genuine, lasting improvements in economic security and market stability - The New Deal preserved capitalism by demonstrating that democratic government could address capitalism's failures without abandoning it - The TVA transformed the Tennessee Valley with cheap electricity, flood control, and economic development that private markets had not provided

Position B: The New Deal Overreached or Was Inadequate Arguments to develop (this position should be nuanced — it can argue overreach from the right OR inadequacy from the left): Right critique: NRA and AAA unconstitutionally delegated legislative power; TVA competed unfairly with private utilities; court-packing plan threatened judicial independence; deficit spending was fiscally irresponsible Left critique: Social Security excluded Black workers; New Deal preserved and legitimized a racist economic system; wealth was not redistributed; the Depression was not ended — only WWII ended it; Huey Long's Share Our Wealth would have been more effective at reducing inequality

Consensus question: "Whatever we conclude about whether the New Deal went too far or not far enough, what principles should govern government's response to an economic crisis? What can the government do? What can't it do? What must it do? Who gets to decide?"

Critical Content Notes

The TVA — This Is Required Tennessee Content: The TVA must be taught in depth in Tennessee classrooms. The standard specifically addresses it. Every Tennessee student should be able to explain: (1) what the TVA did (hydroelectric power, flood control, rural electrification, navigation); (2) why it was significant for Tennessee (only ~3% of rural farms had electricity before TVA); (3) why it was controversial (competition with private utilities; accusations of socialism); (4) that it still exists and is headquartered in Knoxville.

If at all possible, plan a field trip to Norris Dam or another TVA facility. Students in East Tennessee may have family connections to the TVA era — elicit these stories. The Tennessee State Library and Archives has extraordinary photographic and documentary collections from TVA construction; obtain reproductions for classroom display.

Social Security and Racial Exclusion — Do Not Skip This: The deliberate exclusion of domestic and agricultural workers from Social Security — a policy specifically designed to exclude Black workers to satisfy Southern Democrats — is essential content that cannot be treated as a footnote. It is historically accurate, documented, and central to understanding the New Deal's actual legacy. Teaching the New Deal without this content is incomplete history.

This is not a criticism of Social Security as a program — it is a historically accurate account of how the program was designed and who it initially served. The program has been expanded over decades to cover more workers; understanding the original exclusions helps students understand why expansion was necessary. [TCA § 49-6-1006 compliance — this content is required]

TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — The Capitalism Debate Is Central to This Unit: Unit 5 is the most direct unit-level engagement with the TCA § 49-6-1028(b) requirement to teach students about capitalism and the constitutional republic. The essential question — "How do we balance economic freedom with economic security?" — is precisely the question the statute intends students to engage. Present the debate fairly: the genuine achievements of unregulated capitalism (the 1920s prosperity; the innovation and growth that created the industrial economy) AND its genuine failures (the Depression; the social costs of leaving markets entirely unregulated). Present the New Deal's approach fairly: the genuine achievements (FDIC, Social Security, TVA) AND the genuine constitutional and economic concerns of its critics. This is the kind of evidence-based engagement with capitalism's virtues and limitations that produces informed citizens.

The 2008 Comparison Activity — Handle Carefully: Student Activity 5.2 introduces Unit 10 content in a comparative framework. This is intentional — it helps students see historical patterns — but requires care. Focus strictly on the comparison between 1929 and 2008 as historical events. Do NOT engage in contemporary political debates about the 2008 response, the Obama administration, or current economic policy. The historical comparison is the instructional goal.

Dorothea Lange and Florence Owens Thompson: Tell the full story of Migrant Mother, including Florence Owens Thompson's own complex feelings about the photograph. Thompson was a strong, capable woman who did not want to be permanently defined as a symbol of victimhood. This complexity makes the photograph MORE valuable as a historical source and as a teaching tool, not less. It raises essential questions about how we represent people in poverty, who controls those representations, and what photographs can and cannot tell us about the people they depict.

UNIT 5 GLOSSARY ENTRIES

(For Appendix A — Alphabetical Glossary)

AAA (Agricultural Adjustment Administration) | (A-A-A) | A New Deal agency (1933) that paid farmers to reduce production of major crops to raise prices. It helped large farm operators recover income but discriminated against Black tenant farmers and sharecroppers, who were often cheated out of payments or evicted. It was struck down by the Supreme Court in 1936 and replaced with a modified program. | Unit 5, Section 5.2

bank run | When large numbers of depositors simultaneously try to withdraw their money from a bank, usually because they fear the bank will fail. Banks hold only a fraction of deposits in cash (most is loaned out), so a bank run can cause a solvent bank to fail. The creation of FDIC deposit insurance in 1933 dramatically reduced the frequency of bank runs. | Unit 5, Section 5.1

Black Tuesday (October 29, 1929) | The day of the catastrophic stock market crash that is considered the beginning of the Great Depression. Approximately 16 million shares were traded as prices collapsed. Combined with "Black Thursday" (October 24), Black Tuesday wiped out billions of dollars in stock value and triggered the banking crises that deepened the Depression. | Unit 5, Section 5.1

CCC (Civilian Conservation Corps) | (C-C-C) | A New Deal work-relief program (1933–1942) that employed young men in national parks, forests, and public lands. At its peak it employed approximately 500,000 men at a time, planting trees, building trails, and constructing park facilities. Men sent most of their $25 monthly pay home to their families. The CCC was racially segregated. | Unit 5, Section 5.2

court-packing plan | President Roosevelt's 1937 proposal to add up to six new justices to the Supreme Court — one for each justice over age 70 — after the Court struck down multiple New Deal programs. The plan failed in Congress because even FDR's supporters believed it threatened judicial independence. It is a significant case study in the limits of presidential power in a constitutional republic. | Unit 5, Section 5.2 [TCA § 49-6-1028(b)]

deficit spending | (DEF-ih-sit SPEN-ding) | When a government spends more money than it collects in taxes, borrowing the difference by issuing bonds. Keynesian economic theory advocates deficit spending during economic downturns to stimulate demand. The New Deal used some deficit spending, though FDR was uncomfortable with large deficits. WWII spending at a massive scale ultimately ended the Depression. | Unit 5, Section 5.3

Dust Bowl | The environmental disaster on the southern Great Plains in the 1930s in which severe drought combined with poor agricultural practices (destruction of native grasses through plowing) created conditions for massive dust storms. The most severely affected area was centered on the Oklahoma/Texas panhandles, western Kansas, and adjacent areas. Approximately 3.5 million people abandoned the region. | Unit 5, Section 5.1

FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation) | (F-D-I-C) | A federal agency created in 1933 by the Glass-Steagall Act that insures bank deposits, originally up to $2,500 (now $250,000). The FDIC ended the threat of bank runs by guaranteeing that depositors would not lose their savings if their bank failed. It still insures your bank deposits today. | Unit 5, Section 5.2

Federal Reserve | (FED-uh-rul REH-zerv) | The central banking system of the United States, created in 1913. During the Depression, the Federal Reserve made the catastrophic error of raising interest rates and contracting the money supply at the exact moment the economy needed liquidity, deepening and extending the Depression. | Unit 5, Section 5.1; created Unit 3

Glass-Steagall Act (1933) | A banking reform law that separated commercial banking (taking deposits, making loans) from investment banking (trading securities), addressing one of the structural causes of the banking collapse. It also created the FDIC. It was repealed in 1999, a decision some economists argue contributed to the 2008 financial crisis. [VERIFY: Glass-Steagall repeal via Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, November 1999; this connection to 2008 is debated among economists — present as a debated claim, not established fact] | Unit 5, Section 5.2

Great Depression | The severe global economic crisis that began with the U.S. stock market crash in October 1929 and lasted through most of the 1930s. At its worst, U.S. unemployment reached approximately 25%, GDP fell by approximately 30%, and 9,000 banks failed. It was caused by multiple interacting factors and ended only with the economic mobilization of World War II. | Unit 5, all sections

Hoovervilles | (HOO-ver-vilz) | Shantytowns built by homeless and unemployed people during the Depression, sarcastically named after President Hoover, who was blamed for the economic catastrophe. They appeared in city parks, vacant lots, and riverbanks across the country during the early 1930s. | Unit 5, Section 5.1

Keynesian economics | (KAYN-zee-un EK-uh-nom-iks) | An economic theory developed by British economist John Maynard Keynes arguing that government should use deficit spending to stimulate economic demand during recessions and depressions. The New Deal used some Keynesian logic; WWII spending demonstrated its effectiveness at much larger scale. Keynesian economics remains influential and controversial. | Unit 5, Section 5.3

Migrant Mother | The iconic 1936 photograph by Dorothea Lange of Florence Owens Thompson, a 32-year-old woman in a Californian pea-picker's camp. Thompson was a Cherokee woman and California native who had just sold her car's tires to buy food for her children. The photograph became a symbol of the human cost of the Dust Bowl and the Depression. | Unit 5, Section 5.3; Primary Source Spotlight 5.2

New Deal | President Franklin Roosevelt's collection of programs, agencies, and reforms enacted between 1933 and approximately 1938 in response to the Great Depression. Organized around the "Three Rs" (Relief, Recovery, Reform), the New Deal permanently expanded the federal government's role in the economy and established the foundations of the American social safety net. | Unit 5, all sections

Norris Dam | The first TVA dam, completed in 1936 near Norris, Tennessee (approximately 25 miles north of Knoxville). It created Norris Lake, provided flood control and hydroelectric power, and symbolized the TVA's transformation of the Tennessee Valley. The planned town of Norris, Tennessee was built to house dam workers. | Unit 5, Section 5.2 [TENNESSEE CONTENT]

Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC) | A Hoover administration agency (1932) that provided loans to banks, railroads, and other corporations to prevent their collapse. It was criticized as "trickle-down" relief — helping institutions rather than individuals. FDR later expanded the RFC's role. | Unit 5, Section 5.1

rugged individualism | (RUG-id in-DIV-ij-oo-ul-iz-um) | The belief, associated with Herbert Hoover, that Americans should succeed through individual effort, voluntary cooperation, and private initiative rather than government assistance. Hoover's commitment to this philosophy led him to resist federal direct relief during the Depression, a decision that proved politically and humanely disastrous. | Unit 5, Section 5.1

safety net | The set of government programs designed to provide a minimum level of economic support to people in need, preventing them from falling into absolute poverty. The Social Security Act (1935) created the foundation of the American safety net. | Unit 5, Section 5.2

SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) | (S-E-C) | A federal regulatory agency created in 1934 to regulate the stock market and prevent the fraudulent and speculative practices that contributed to the 1929 crash. It still regulates U.S. securities markets today. | Unit 5, Section 5.2

Share Our Wealth | Huey Long's populist program proposing to cap individual fortunes at $5 million, guarantee every American family a minimum income, and fund free education through college. It attracted millions of followers who felt the New Deal had not gone far enough in challenging concentrated wealth. Long was assassinated in September 1935 before he could mount a presidential campaign. | Unit 5, Section 5.2

Smoot-Hawley Tariff (1930) | (smoot HAW-lee TAR-if) | A federal law that raised tariffs on imported goods to approximately 45%, intended to protect American industry and agriculture. Instead, trading partners retaliated with their own tariffs; U.S. exports fell by approximately two-thirds; international trade collapsed. Most economists consider it one of the most consequential policy mistakes in American economic history. | Unit 5, Section 5.1

Social Security Act (1935) | The landmark New Deal law that created old-age insurance (Social Security), unemployment insurance, and aid to dependent children — the foundation of the American social safety net. The original act excluded domestic and agricultural workers — a racially motivated exclusion that meant most Black workers in the South were initially ineligible for old-age benefits. | Unit 5, Section 5.2 [TCA § 49-6-1006]

Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) | (T-V-A) | A federal agency created by the TVA Act (May 18, 1933) to develop the Tennessee River Valley through hydroelectric dams, flood control, rural electrification, navigation improvement, and economic development. It transformed a severely depressed region; only about 3% of rural Tennessee farms had electricity before the TVA. It is still headquartered in Knoxville, Tennessee and still provides power to approximately 10 million customers across a seven-state region. It is the New Deal's most directly Tennessee-specific program and one of its most enduring institutions. | Unit 5, Section 5.2 [TENNESSEE REQUIRED CONTENT]

Wagner Act (National Labor Relations Act, 1935) | The law that guaranteed workers the right to organize unions and collectively bargain, and created the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) to enforce these rights. It fundamentally changed the balance of power between labor and capital in the United States and led to a surge in union membership through the CIO. | Unit 5, Section 5.2

WPA (Works Progress Administration) | (W-P-A) | The largest New Deal jobs program (1935–1943), which employed approximately 8.5 million Americans on construction projects, and cultural programs including the Federal Writers' Project, Federal Art Project, and the Slave Narratives Project (documenting the testimonies of formerly enslaved people). WPA murals can still be seen in Tennessee post offices and public buildings today.
