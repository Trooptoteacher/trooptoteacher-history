Narrative Text

What Is Capitalism, and How Does a Market Economy Work?[COMPARISON]

(Concept introduction — required before vocabulary; TCA § 49-6-1028(b) compliance anchor)

Before we examine the specific figures and events of the Gilded Age, we need to understand the economic system within which they operated: capitalism.

Capitalism is an economic system in which privately owned businesses operate in competitive markets, producing goods and services for profit. Its foundational principles include:

  • Private property: Individuals and businesses can own the means of production — factories, land, machinery, raw materials
  • Free markets: Prices are determined by supply and demand rather than by government decree
  • Competition: Multiple producers compete for customers, which — in theory — drives down prices and improves quality
  • Profit motive: The possibility of profit encourages individuals and businesses to take risks, invest, and innovate
  • Wage labor: Workers sell their labor for wages rather than owning the means of production themselves

Capitalism had produced extraordinary wealth and technological innovation in the United States by the mid-19th century. The Industrial Revolution, powered by capitalist investment in railroads, factories, and new technologies, transformed the American economy from primarily agricultural to industrial within a single generation.

The Gilded Age represents capitalism operating at maximum velocity — with few restraints, enormous concentrations of wealth, spectacular innovation, and staggering inequality. Understanding what capitalism achieved in this era, and what problems it created, is essential to understanding why the Progressive Era, the New Deal, and subsequent debates about economic regulation all happened. [TCA § 49-6-1205 — free enterprise systems; this section establishes the conceptual foundation for all subsequent economic discussions in the course]

The Virtues and Tensions of Capitalism: American capitalism in the Gilded Age produced genuine achievements: - Railroad mileage expanded from approximately 35,000 miles in 1865 to over 193,000 miles by 1900 [VERIFY specific figures against historical railroad statistics; a frequently cited range — confirm against Bureau of Transportation Statistics or historical sources] - Steel production increased dramatically, making skyscrapers, bridges, and modern infrastructure possible - New consumer products and technologies (electric light, telephone, refrigeration) reached ordinary Americans - Real wages for industrial workers rose over the long run during this period, though unevenly and with significant exploitation along the way [VERIFY: economists debate the real wage gains for industrial workers in this period; cite a specific scholarly source for this claim before publication]

But capitalism also produced serious problems that would define political debate for the next century: - Extreme concentration of wealth (a small number of individuals controlled vast percentages of the economy) - Working conditions that were dangerous, exhausting, and often deadly - Child labor on a massive scale - The use of monopoly power to destroy competition and set prices artificially

The Gilded Age is where American capitalism's tensions became impossible to ignore — and where serious debate about what the government's role in the economy should be first emerged as a dominant political question.

The Railroad Revolution[CAUSATION]

No single technology did more to transform the American economy in the 19th century than the railroad. By 1869, the transcontinental railroad connected the Atlantic and Pacific coasts for the first time — the Union Pacific building west from Omaha, Nebraska and the Central Pacific building east from Sacramento, California met at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory on May 10, 1869.

The economic consequences were revolutionary: - National market integration: Goods could now move from coast to coast in days rather than months. A manufacturer in Pittsburgh could sell products in San Francisco. A California rancher could ship cattle to Chicago slaughterhouses. - Time standardization: Before railroads, every town kept its own local time based on the sun's position. Railroad schedules made this chaos unworkable. On November 18, 1883, American railroads adopted four standard time zones — the origin of the time zones we still use today. [VERIFY: date of railroad standard time adoption is well-documented; confirm November 18, 1883] - Settlement patterns: Railroads made westward settlement viable by moving people and supplies efficiently. They also shaped where towns grew — communities on rail lines thrived; those bypassed by railroads often died. - Financial innovation: The scale of railroad investment — billions of dollars — drove the development of modern investment banking, stock markets, and corporate finance. J.P. Morgan's banking empire was largely built on reorganizing bankrupt railroads.

The transcontinental railroad was built by two workforces that history often forgets: Chinese immigrants (primarily on the Central Pacific, where they made up the majority of the labor force and performed the most dangerous work — drilling and blasting through the Sierra Nevada) and Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans (primarily on the Union Pacific). Both groups were paid less than white American workers and worked under brutal conditions. [VERIFY: labor demographics of transcontinental railroad are well-documented; cite specific source, e.g., Gordon H. Chang's Ghosts of Gold Mountain: The Epic Story of the Chinese Who Built the Transcontinental Railroad (2019) for student bibliography]

But railroads also created serious economic problems. The major railroads charged artificially high rates in areas where they had no competition, gave secret discounts (called "rebates") to their largest customers (companies like Standard Oil), and manipulated stock prices to enrich insiders. The railroad corporations were, simultaneously, the engine of American economic growth and one of the most powerful instruments of economic exploitation in American history.

The Men Who Built (and Dominated) Industrial America

The Gilded Age produced a generation of extraordinarily wealthy industrialists who built empires in steel, oil, banking, and railroads. Whether these men were heroic visionaries who built modern America or ruthless exploiters who crushed competitors and exploited workers is a question historians and economists still debate. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES]

Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919) — Steel Born in Scotland, Carnegie immigrated to the United States as a child and rose from bobbin boy in a cotton factory to the wealthiest man in the world. He built Carnegie Steel into the dominant force in American steel production using vertical integration: owning every stage of steel production from the iron ore mines in Minnesota, to the Great Lakes shipping lines that transported ore, to the steel mills in Pittsburgh, to the railroads that carried finished steel to customers.

By owning the entire supply chain, Carnegie could produce steel at lower costs than any competitor — and undersell anyone who challenged him. In 1901, J.P. Morgan bought Carnegie Steel for $480 million (approximately $16 billion in 2024 dollars [VERIFY conversion]) — the largest private business transaction in American history to that point — and used it as the core of U.S. Steel, the first billion-dollar corporation.

Carnegie was also one of history's most remarkable philanthropists. After selling his business empire, he gave away approximately $350 million ($11+ billion in modern terms [VERIFY conversion]) — funding over 2,500 public libraries, Carnegie Hall in New York, and universities including Carnegie Mellon. His philanthropic philosophy was articulated in his "Gospel of Wealth" (1889) — required reading for this unit.

John D. Rockefeller (1839–1937) — Oil Rockefeller built Standard Oil into a monopoly that controlled approximately 90% of American oil refining by 1880 [VERIFY specific percentage against historical sources — commonly cited figure; confirm against scholarly work such as Ron Chernow's Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr.] He achieved this not primarily through superior technology but through horizontal integration: buying out or destroying competitors, negotiating secret railroad rebates that allowed him to undercut rivals' prices, and using predatory pricing — temporarily selling below cost in a market until competitors went bankrupt, then buying them out at distressed prices.

Rockefeller organized Standard Oil as a trust — a legal arrangement in which multiple companies transferred their stock to a board of trustees who controlled all of them collectively, eliminating competition among them while technically leaving each company separate. The "trust" form became so associated with monopolistic practices that we still call all anti-monopoly law "antitrust" law.

Standard Oil was eventually broken up by the Supreme Court in 1911 under the Sherman Antitrust Act. Its successor companies — including what became ExxonMobil, Chevron, and BP's American operations — still exist today. [VERIFY corporate succession history]

J.P. Morgan (1837–1913) — Finance If Carnegie represented production and Rockefeller represented distribution, J.P. Morgan represented finance capital — the power to organize, fund, and control the entire industrial economy through control of credit. Morgan reorganized bankrupt railroads, underwrote the formation of U.S. Steel and General Electric, and in 1907 personally organized a private bailout of the American financial system during a banking panic — a role that would later be institutionalized in the Federal Reserve System (created 1913).

Morgan exemplified a different kind of power: not just owning factories, but controlling the flow of money that made all factories possible. By the early 20th century, a congressional investigation — the Pujo Committee hearings of 1912–1913 [VERIFY] — found that Morgan and a handful of allied bankers effectively controlled much of American industry through interlocking boards of directors and control of credit.

The Ideology of Success: Social Darwinism and the Gospel of Wealth[COMPARISON]

How did the extraordinarily wealthy justify their wealth — to themselves and to the public — in an era when workers were earning starvation wages? Two related ideological frameworks provided that justification.

Social Darwinism: The English philosopher Herbert Spencer (not Charles Darwin — an important distinction) applied evolutionary language to human society. Spencer coined the phrase "survival of the fittest" and argued that human society, like nature, was governed by a struggle in which the strongest and most capable naturally rose to the top, while the weak naturally fell. [VERIFY: Spencer's "survival of the fittest" coinage — Spencer used the phrase; Darwin later incorporated it into later editions of On the Origin of Species; Spencer applied it to society; attribute carefully]

Applied to American capitalism, Social Darwinism offered a powerful justification for inequality: the wealthy were wealthy because they were more capable, harder-working, and more intelligent. Poverty was evidence of unfitness. Any government interference — welfare programs, labor regulations, wealth redistribution — would be unnatural interference with the law of the social jungle, weakening society by allowing the "unfit" to survive and reproduce.

This ideology appealed to industrialists and to the middle class because it made success feel earned and poverty feel deserved. It also conveniently ignored the role of inherited wealth, access to education, discrimination, and sheer luck in determining who succeeded. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — note that Social Darwinism has no scientific basis as a description of human society; it was ideological, not scientific, and was used to justify racial hierarchies as well as class inequality]

The Gospel of Wealth: Carnegie articulated a more morally sophisticated justification in his famous 1889 essay, "The Gospel of Wealth." Carnegie argued that: 1. Inequality in capitalism is inevitable — the accumulation of capital in fewer hands is the natural consequence of efficient economic organization 2. This concentration is beneficial — large fortunes, managed wisely, produce greater social benefit than if the same wealth were distributed in small amounts to many people 3. Therefore, the duty of wealthy men is to use their fortunes for the public good — not to leave it to heirs, not to hoard it, but to administer it as trustees of the public wealth during their lifetimes

Carnegie called excessive bequest to heirs a waste, and he called dying rich a disgrace. He practiced what he preached — giving away almost all of his fortune before he died.

The Gospel of Wealth accepted capitalism's inequality while seeking to make it morally acceptable through philanthropic obligation. It did NOT challenge the economic system that produced the inequality — it simply argued that the winners should be generous. Workers, labor leaders, and later the Progressives found this deeply inadequate: why should the solution to industrial poverty be the charitable choices of individual industrialists, rather than fair wages and decent working conditions to begin with?

The Industrial Worker: A Life of Danger and Exhaustion[CCOT]

The men, women, and children who worked in America's industrial economy in the Gilded Age did so under conditions almost unimaginable by today's standards. Understanding their daily reality is essential to understanding why the labor movement existed, why it attracted hundreds of thousands of members, and why violence erupted so regularly between workers and employers.

Hours and Wages: The standard industrial workday in the 1880s was 10–12 hours, six days a week, for wages that barely covered subsistence costs. In the steel industry, many workers worked a 12-hour day, 7 days a week — with shifts rotating between day and night on alternate weeks, creating a 24-hour "long turn" when shifts changed. [VERIFY: the 12-hour day, 7-day week in steel is documented; cite specific source, e.g., John A. Fitch, The Steel Workers (1910) from the Pittsburgh Survey]

Wages varied by industry, skill level, and ethnicity, but for unskilled industrial workers, a typical wage might be $1.00–$1.50 per day in the 1880s. Rent for a tenement apartment in a city like Pittsburgh or New York could consume 20–30% of a worker's income; food, fuel, and clothing consumed most of the rest. Savings were nearly impossible. [VERIFY wage and rent figures against Bureau of Labor Statistics historical data or period surveys like the Carroll Wright labor reports]

Danger: Industrial work was extraordinarily dangerous. There were no safety regulations, no workers' compensation, no disability insurance, and no OSHA. Workers who were injured or killed on the job had no recourse — their families received nothing.

In 1882, approximately 675 workers were killed on the job in the United States per day — a statistic estimated from available accident records. [VERIFY this figure; it is widely cited but the source varies; Bureau of Labor Statistics history or Crystal Eastman's Work-Accidents and the Law (1910) may provide verifiable figures] Steel mills, coal mines, textile factories, and railroads were particularly deadly.

In coal mines, cave-ins, explosions, and black lung disease killed and disabled workers at catastrophic rates. East Tennessee's coal mines — part of the industrial development that came to the state in this era — were no exception. [TENNESSEE CONTENT: East Tennessee coal mining is a state-specific connection — VERIFY specific Tennessee mining industry data from TSLA or Tennessee State Museum]

Child Labor: In 1900, approximately 1.75 million children under 16 were employed in industrial and agricultural work in the United States [VERIFY figure against 1900 census data]. Children worked in textile mills, coal mines (as "breaker boys" who sorted coal by hand, breathing coal dust all day), glass factories, canneries, and on the streets selling newspapers.

Child labor was not a marginal practice — it was a significant component of the industrial economy, and it was legal everywhere until Progressive Era reforms began restricting it after 1900 (and not abolished at the federal level until the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, revisited in Unit 5).

The Rise of Labor Unions[CAUSATION]

Workers organized in response to these conditions, facing enormous obstacles: employers who fired union members, courts that issued injunctions against strikes, private security forces (the Pinkerton Detective Agency was the most notorious), and state militias deployed against strikers.

The Knights of Labor (founded 1869, peak membership ~700,000 in 1886): [VERIFY founding date and peak membership figures] The Knights of Labor were unusual among labor organizations of the era in their inclusivity: they organized across craft lines (all workers, skilled and unskilled), across racial lines (they included Black members at a time when most unions did not), and included women. Led by Terence Powderly, the Knights advocated not just for higher wages and shorter hours but for a fundamental transformation of the economic system — worker-owned cooperatives, an end to child and convict labor, graduated income tax, and government ownership of railroads.

The Knights reached their peak influence in 1886 but collapsed rapidly after being (unfairly) associated with the Haymarket Affair. Their decline reflected a tension that would run through the labor movement for decades: the difference between craft-based unionism (organizing skilled workers by trade) and industrial unionism (organizing all workers in an industry regardless of skill).

The American Federation of Labor (AFL, founded 1886): [VERIFY founding date] Samuel Gompers founded the AFL on a very different philosophy than the Knights. The AFL organized only skilled, craft workers — carpenters, plumbers, cigar makers — by their specific trade. It abandoned the Knights' vision of economic transformation and focused on practical, immediate goals: higher wages, shorter hours, better conditions. Gompers called this "pure and simple" unionism.

The AFL's focused, pragmatic approach made it durable — it survived where the Knights failed. But its exclusivity was also a significant limitation. By organizing primarily skilled workers (who were predominantly white and native-born or western European), the AFL left out the majority of the industrial workforce: the unskilled immigrant workers who were doing the most dangerous and lowest-paid jobs in the economy.

Strikes and Conflict: The Violence of Industrial America

The Gilded Age was one of the most violently contested periods in American labor history. Three episodes stand out for their national significance.

The Haymarket Affair (Chicago, May 4, 1886): A rally at Haymarket Square supporting the eight-hour workday turned catastrophic when someone — never conclusively identified — threw a bomb at police, killing seven officers. Police fired into the crowd; eight workers also died. Eight anarchist labor leaders were arrested; four were hanged despite a lack of direct evidence connecting them to the bomb.

Haymarket was used by employers and conservative politicians to tar the entire labor movement as radical and violent. It effectively destroyed the Knights of Labor by association. But it also galvanized labor activism — May Day (May 1) became International Workers' Day in commemoration of the Haymarket martyrs, and the eight-hour workday became labor's central demand for the next generation. [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — note that the identity of the bomb-thrower was never established; the executions are considered by many historians a miscarriage of justice]

The Homestead Strike (Carnegie Steel, Homestead, Pennsylvania, July 1892): When Carnegie Steel's manager Henry Clay Frick cut wages by 22% and locked union workers out of the Homestead plant, the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers struck. Frick hired 300 Pinkerton detectives to break the strike; workers armed themselves and a battle erupted on the Monongahela River in which 9 workers and 7 Pinkertons were killed. [VERIFY casualty figures]

The Pennsylvania governor sent in 8,000 state militia troops. The union was crushed. The defeat at Homestead was catastrophic for steel workers — the steel industry would remain essentially union-free for the next 40 years.

The Pullman Strike (Chicago, 1894): When the Pullman Palace Car Company cut workers' wages by 25–40% while leaving rents in the company-owned town of Pullman, Illinois unchanged, workers walked out. Eugene V. Debs's American Railway Union supported the strike by refusing to handle Pullman cars — paralyzing rail traffic across the country.

President Grover Cleveland intervened by sending federal troops over the objection of Illinois Governor John Altgeld — the first time the federal government used military force to break a strike. Cleveland justified the intervention on the grounds that the strike was interfering with the U.S. mail. Debs was arrested; the strike was broken.

The Pullman Strike raised a constitutional question that would echo for decades: Could the federal government use its power to break strikes and take the side of employers against workers? For the Gilded Age, the answer was clearly yes.

The New Immigration: Who Was Coming to America, and Why?[CCOT]

Between 1870 and 1920, approximately 25 million immigrants arrived in the United States [VERIFY figure against historical immigration statistics — widely cited; confirm]. The character of immigration changed significantly during this period.

"Old" Immigration (pre-1880s): Earlier waves of immigration had come predominantly from northwestern Europe — England, Ireland, Germany, Scandinavia. These immigrants were often Protestant (except the Irish, who were Catholic), spoke languages with Germanic roots that had influenced American English, and came from countries with some tradition of democratic self-government.

"New" Immigration (1880s–1920s): By the 1880s, the dominant source countries shifted dramatically. The "new" immigrants came primarily from: - Southern Europe: Italy, Greece, Portugal - Eastern Europe: Poland, Russia, Hungary, Romania - Russia and Eastern Europe's Jewish communities (fleeing pogroms — state-sponsored and mob violence against Jewish communities, particularly intense in Russia after 1881 [VERIFY]) - Asia: China (primarily California and the West), Japan, Philippines (after 1898)

These immigrants were more likely to be Catholic, Orthodox Christian, or Jewish; to speak languages that were linguistically distant from English; and to come from countries with less democratic tradition. They settled disproportionately in urban industrial areas — New York, Chicago, Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Detroit — taking the lowest-paid industrial and service jobs.

Push and Pull Factors: Why did they come? Immigration is never a single-cause phenomenon.

Pull factors (reasons to come to America): - Industrial jobs — the United States was the fastest-growing industrial economy in the world and had insatiable demand for labor - Land (for earlier immigrants and agricultural workers) - Political and religious freedom - Chain migration — immigrants followed family members and fellow villagers who had come before, creating communities and networks

Push factors (reasons to leave home countries): - Poverty and land scarcity in Southern and Eastern Europe — industrialization was disrupting traditional agricultural economies - Political persecution — Russian Jews faced pogroms; political dissidents fled authoritarian regimes - Famine and crop failures - Lack of economic opportunity in industrializing Europe

City Life: The Tenements and Urban Poverty

Immigrants arriving in American cities found housing in tenements — densely packed apartment buildings in urban neighborhoods designed to maximize the number of paying tenants on a small lot. Jacob Riis's 1890 photographic expose, How the Other Half Lives, made the conditions of New York's Lower East Side tenements visible to middle-class Americans for the first time.

A typical tenement on New York's Lower East Side: - Was four to seven stories tall on a 25-by-100-foot lot - Housed dozens of families — sometimes 10–15 people in a two-room apartment - Had minimal or no indoor plumbing (outhouses in the rear courtyard, shared by all tenants) - Had little or no ventilation — interior rooms had no windows; in summer, heat was brutal - Was a fire hazard (flammable construction, no fire escapes on many buildings until reform) - Was surrounded by other identical buildings, creating densely packed "blocks" with no open space or parks

Disease spread rapidly in these conditions. Tuberculosis (called "consumption" or the "white plague") was epidemic in tenement neighborhoods. Cholera, typhoid, and diphtheria outbreaks were regular occurrences. Infant mortality rates in tenement districts were among the highest in the industrialized world. [VERIFY specific statistics from Riis or period public health records]

Political Machines: Urban politics in immigrant neighborhoods was often controlled by political machines — centralized party organizations that provided services (jobs, food, help with the law, small loans) to immigrants in exchange for votes. The most famous was Tammany Hall, the Democratic machine that controlled New York City politics for generations.

Political machines were corrupt — they extracted patronage, bribes, and kickbacks from city contracts. But they also provided a social safety net that the government did not: finding jobs, helping immigrants navigate bureaucracy, and building the neighborhood networks that gave immigrant communities political power. The machines were later targeted by Progressive reformers.

Nativist Backlash: Chinese Exclusion and Immigration Restriction[CAUSATION]

The arrival of millions of immigrants who were visibly different in language, religion, and culture from the existing American population triggered a powerful nativist backlash.

The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882): Chinese immigrants had come to California beginning with the Gold Rush (1848–1849) and had provided much of the labor force for the transcontinental railroad (Central Pacific). As the railroad was completed and economic depression hit California in the 1870s, white workers increasingly blamed Chinese laborers for taking jobs and depressing wages.

The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 was the first — and for 62 years, the only — law in American history to bar immigration from a specific country based on race. It prohibited Chinese laborers from entering the United States and denied Chinese immigrants already in the country the right to become citizens. It was renewed and strengthened several times before finally being repealed in 1943. [VERIFY: repeal date 1943; Chinese Americans gained the right to citizenship and a small immigration quota through the Magnuson Act, December 17, 1943]

The law is also significant as the first federal immigration restriction — understanding it is essential to understanding the restrictionist movement that culminated in the Immigration Act of 1924 (Unit 4).]

The Myth and Reality of the Frontier[CONTEXTUALIZATION]

The American West occupies a unique place in the national mythology — the frontier as the crucible of American character, the cowboy as the ultimate symbol of independence and self-reliance. Frederick Jackson Turner's 1893 essay "The Significance of the Frontier in American History" argued that the frontier experience had produced American democracy and American character — and that the closure of the frontier (declared by the Census Bureau in 1890) threatened the nation's future. [VERIFY: Turner's 1893 essay at the World's Columbian Exposition — well-documented; full text is public domain; available through LOC]

The Turner thesis was enormously influential — and also profoundly Eurocentric. It treated the West as if it were empty before white settlement. It was not empty.

The American West in 1865 was home to millions of Native American people representing hundreds of distinct nations, cultures, and languages. The "frontier" was their homeland. The "closing of the frontier" was the culmination of a century-long campaign of conquest, displacement, and cultural destruction that reduced Native American populations, land holdings, and political sovereignty to a fraction of their pre-contact levels.

U.S. Policy Toward Native Americans: The Reservation System and the Dawes Act[CAUSATION]

The Reservation System: U.S. government policy toward Native Americans in the post-Civil War era was officially described as a policy of "civilization" — assimilating Native American peoples into Euro-American culture while confining them to designated reservation lands. In practice, it was a policy of land theft, cultural destruction, and forced dependency.

The reservation system: - Confined Native American nations to specific, often undesirable tracts of land far smaller than their ancestral territories - Made reservation residents dependent on federal government food rations — rations that were frequently inadequate, late, or stolen by corrupt agents - Was frequently violated by white settlers and miners who encroached on reservation lands when valuable resources (gold, farmland) were discovered, with the federal government often acquiescing - Destroyed the economic systems (hunting, seasonal migration, trade) that had sustained Native American communities

The Dawes Act (General Allotment Act, 1887): The Dawes Act represented a new phase in federal Indian policy — one promoted by well-intentioned reformers who believed they were helping Native American peoples by forcing them to adopt Euro-American practices of individual land ownership.

Under the Dawes Act: - Reservation land held collectively by tribal nations would be divided into individual allotments — 160 acres per head of household - Native American individuals who accepted allotments and adopted "civilized" ways of life could become U.S. citizens - The "surplus" reservation land left over after allotments were distributed would be sold to white settlers

The results were catastrophic. Native American land holdings decreased from approximately 138 million acres in 1887 to approximately 48 million acres by 1934 — a loss of about 90 million acres. [VERIFY figures against DOI/BIA historical records or Vine Deloria Jr.'s scholarship]

The land loss happened through multiple mechanisms: outright sale of "surplus" lands, land fraud (Native Americans were often cheated in allotment sales), loss of land through tax forfeitures, and the erosion of tribal land bases as allotments passed to non-Native heirs through inheritance.

[MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES: The Dawes Act was supported by many sincere reformers who believed forced assimilation was in Native Americans' best interest. It was opposed by traditional Native leaders who understood it as an attack on tribal sovereignty and collective land rights. The evidence clearly shows its effects were devastating. Teachers should present the range of perspectives held at the time while making clear what the historical record shows about its consequences.]

The Ghost Dance Movement and Wounded Knee (1890): In the late 1880s, a spiritual movement called the Ghost Dance spread rapidly among Plains Indian nations. The movement, originating with a Paiute prophet named Wovoka, taught that if Native Americans performed a specific ritual dance, the dead would return, buffalo herds would be restored, white settlers would be removed, and Native peoples would be reunited with their ancestors. [VERIFY: Wovoka's identity and the Ghost Dance theology is well-documented; cite a specific scholarly source, e.g., James Mooney's contemporary 1896 study or more recent scholarship]

To white settlers and the federal government, the Ghost Dance appeared threatening — a sign of potential uprising. Reservation agents called for military intervention.

In December 1890, federal troops went to arrest Hunkpapa Lakota leader Sitting Bull at the Standing Rock reservation. Sitting Bull was killed during the arrest — in unclear circumstances — triggering a panic among his followers. A band of Miniconjou Lakota under Chief Big Foot fled toward the Pine Ridge reservation, where they hoped to find safety. Federal troops caught up with them at Wounded Knee Creek on December 29, 1890.

What happened at Wounded Knee is still debated in its details, but the outcome is clear: when soldiers attempted to disarm Big Foot's band, a shot was fired. The 7th Cavalry opened fire with rifles and Hotchkiss artillery. When the shooting stopped, between 250 and 300 Lakota men, women, and children were dead, many of them shot while trying to flee. [VERIFY: casualty figures range from 250 to 300 in most sources; confirm specific figure with cited source; 25 soldiers also died, many likely from friendly fire]

Wounded Knee is remembered as one of the most tragic episodes in the long history of conflict between the United States government and Native American peoples. It effectively ended armed Native American resistance to U.S. policy on the Great Plains.

The Farmer's Revolt: Causes of Populism[CAUSATION]

While industrialists were building empires in the cities and industrial valleys of the East and Midwest, American farmers — the backbone of the country's pre-industrial economy — were being squeezed in an economic vise.

The Agricultural Crisis: After the Civil War, American farmers faced a perfect storm of economic pressures:

  1. Falling prices: Agricultural production expanded enormously as new lands came into cultivation (including the Great Plains), driving down crop prices. Wheat prices fell from approximately $1.45 a bushel in 1866 to $0.49 by 1894. Cotton prices similarly collapsed. [VERIFY specific price figures against USDA historical data]

  2. Deflation: The federal government's decision to reduce the money supply after the Civil War (returning to the gold standard) created persistent deflation — prices fell, which seemed beneficial to consumers but was catastrophic for farmers who had borrowed money when prices were higher. A farmer who borrowed $1,000 in 1875 to buy land was repaying it in dollars worth significantly more in 1885 — a hidden increase in his real debt burden.

  3. Railroad monopolies: Farmers in the South and West often had access to only one railroad — a monopoly railroad that could charge whatever it wished to transport crops to market. When a farmer's only option for getting grain to Chicago was the Great Northern Railroad, the Great Northern set whatever rate it chose.

  4. Credit scarcity and high interest: Farmers needed credit to plant crops, buy equipment, and survive from harvest to harvest. In the rural South and West, credit was scarce and expensive. Local merchants (often the only source of credit) charged interest rates of 20–40% or more on the "crop lien" arrangements that bound farmers' future harvests to their debts.

  5. Tariff policy: High tariffs on manufactured goods (protecting Northern industry) raised the prices farmers paid for equipment while doing nothing to raise the prices they received for crops. Farmers were effectively subsidizing industrial protectionism while receiving none of its benefits.

The Farmers' Alliance and the Road to Populism: Farmers organized. The Farmers' Alliance, founded in Texas in the late 1870s, grew into a mass movement with millions of members by 1890. The Alliance organized farmer cooperatives, advocated for railroad regulation, and developed an ambitious economic analysis — the "sub-treasury plan" — that proposed using federal warehouses to allow farmers to store crops and borrow against them at low interest rates, bypassing the predatory credit system. [VERIFY: sub-treasury plan is well-documented in Alliance history; cite specific source]

When the Alliance's demands went unheeded by the two major parties, farmers took the logical next step: they formed their own.

The People's Party (1892): A Platform for Democratic Populism

The People's Party — commonly called the Populists — held its first national convention in Omaha, Nebraska on July 4, 1892 and adopted a platform that reads today as a remarkably comprehensive indictment of Gilded Age capitalism and a program for democratic reform.

The Omaha Platform [VERIFY: Omaha, Nebraska, July 4, 1892 — well-documented; full text is public domain] demanded:

Economic Reforms: - Government ownership of railroads, telegraph, and telephone systems — to prevent private monopoly exploitation - A graduated income tax — to shift the tax burden onto the wealthy - Direct election of U.S. Senators (they were then chosen by state legislatures, which were often controlled by corporate interests) - The sub-treasury plan — low-interest government credit for farmers - An increase in the money supply (free coinage of silver) — to end deflation and give debtors relief

Political Reforms: - The secret ballot (to prevent employer coercion of workers' votes) - Initiative and referendum (allowing citizens to propose and vote directly on laws) - A single-term limit for the president and vice president

Labor Provisions: - An eight-hour workday for government employees (as a model for industry) - Immigration restriction (a concession to nativist labor arguments — a significant tension in the Populist coalition) - Abolition of the "Pinkerton system" — private armies used to break strikes

The Populists' presidential candidate James B. Weaver won over 1 million popular votes and 22 electoral votes in 1892 — remarkable for a third party. [VERIFY electoral and popular vote totals for 1892 Weaver campaign]

In 1896, the Populists made a fateful decision: they fused with the Democratic Party behind William Jennings Bryan's candidacy, making silver coinage their central issue. Bryan's famous "Cross of Gold" speech at the Democratic convention — in which he thundered against the gold standard — is one of the most celebrated orations in American political history. [VERIFY: "Cross of Gold" speech, July 9, 1896 Democratic National Convention, Chicago; full text is public domain; available at multiple archives; extremely famous final line: "You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold" — PUBLIC DOMAIN, widely documented, safe to quote as direct quotation with citation]

Bryan lost to Republican William McKinley. The Populists' fusion strategy effectively destroyed the party as an independent political force. But their ideas did not die — the Populist program served as a blueprint for Progressive Era and New Deal reforms: the graduated income tax (16th Amendment, 1913), direct election of senators (17th Amendment, 1913), railroad regulation, and agricultural credit reform all reflected Populist demands.

Formative Assessment Answer Key and Scoring Notes (Teacher Edition — Not for student distribution)

Q1 Model Answer: Vertical integration = owning all stages of production (Carnegie's example: from iron ore mines through shipping, steel mills, and finished product delivery). Competitive advantage: eliminates profit margins at each stage of the supply chain; produces at lower cost than any competitor who doesn't own the whole chain. Horizontal integration = owning competing businesses at the same stage of production (Rockefeller's example: buying out competing oil refineries). Competitive advantage: eliminates competition, allows price-setting in the absence of competition.

Q2 Model Answer: A trust was a legal arrangement in which multiple competing companies transferred their stock to a board of trustees who controlled all of them collectively, eliminating competition among the nominally separate companies while technically keeping each company legally distinct. Rockefeller used this structure to legally circumvent corporate laws (at the time, many states prohibited one corporation from owning stock in another) while achieving monopoly control. Award full credit for accurate description of the mechanism and the legal rationale.

Q3 Model Answer: Comparison of Knights vs. AFL: - Who organized: Knights = all workers (skilled/unskilled, Black/white, women); AFL = skilled craft workers only (predominantly white, male) - Goals: Knights = transformation of economic system (cooperatives, public ownership); AFL = "pure and simple" unionism (higher wages, shorter hours, better conditions) - Strategy: Knights = mass organizing across industries; AFL = craft-based bargaining - Survival: AFL survived; Knights collapsed after Haymarket (unfair association with bomb throwing destroyed public credibility). AFL's focused, pragmatic approach made it more resilient to employer and public backlash.

Q4 Model Answer: Old immigration (pre-1880s): NW Europe (England, Ireland, Germany, Scandinavia), predominantly Protestant (Irish Catholic exception), settled throughout Northeast and Midwest including rural areas. New immigration (1880s–1920s): S/E Europe (Italy, Poland, Russia, Greece, Hungary) plus Jewish communities fleeing Russian pogroms, predominantly Catholic/Orthodox/Jewish, settled disproportionately in urban industrial areas. Award 1 point per accurate element for each category.

Q5 Model Answer: Dawes Act purpose: divide communal tribal lands into individual allotments (160 acres/household); remaining "surplus" land sold to white settlers. Actual result: catastrophic land loss — from ~138 million acres in 1887 to ~48 million acres by 1934; approximately 90 million acres lost. The act failed on its own terms (it did not produce economically independent Native farmers) and achieved the opposite of stated goals for Native landholdings. Accept any response that uses specific land figures and identifies the gap between stated purpose and actual outcome.

Q6 Model Answer: The claim is well-supported. Social Darwinism: (1) was articulated by Herbert Spencer, a philosopher/sociologist, not a natural scientist; (2) misapplied biological evolutionary concepts to economic and social competition; (3) served to naturalize and justify existing economic inequalities; (4) was conveniently adopted by wealthy industrialists whose wealth it protected; (5) was used to argue against government reform by claiming that helping the poor was "unnatural." Strong answers will note that real evolutionary science makes no claims about the morality or inevitability of economic inequality.

Q7 Model Answer: Homestead: Carnegie Steel cut wages 22%; workers struck; Frick hired 300 Pinkertons, battle ensued, 9 workers + 7 Pinkertons killed; Pennsylvania governor sent 8,000 militia; union crushed. Q8 Model Answer: Accept any well-reasoned comparison of two groups. Critical Content Notes

TCA § 49-6-1028(b) Compliance — Unit 2: Section 2.1 explicitly introduces capitalism as an economic system with Section "What Is Capitalism, and How Does a Market Economy Work?" This is the Unit 2 anchor for the statutory requirement to teach students the virtues of capitalism and the constitutional republic. The treatment here is balanced: capitalism's genuine economic achievements in the Gilded Age are documented alongside its genuine failures. This approach is: (a) historically accurate; (b) intellectually honest; (c) compliant with the statutory requirement, which asks that students be taught about capitalism's virtues — not that they be required to affirm it uncritically. An education that teaches only the virtues without the failures is neither historically accurate nor educationally sound. Students are better prepared to be citizens of a constitutional republic if they understand both what capitalism achieves and what constraints democracy has historically placed on it.

The "Captains of Industry vs. Robber Barons" debate: This is genuinely a [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES] moment. Economic historians continue to debate the net contribution of Gilded Age industrialists. Resist the temptation to resolve the tension definitively in lecture. Present both the genuine economic achievements (Carnegie's steel reduced the cost of building bridges and skyscrapers dramatically; Rockefeller's efficient refining genuinely reduced kerosene prices; railroads genuinely integrated the national market) AND the genuine abuses (monopoly pricing, labor exploitation, political corruption, destruction of competitors through predatory practices). Let students grapple with the complexity.

Native American content — pedagogical notes: - Use "Native American peoples" or "Native nations" (preferred contemporary usage); avoid "Indians" except when it appears in primary sources (contextualize the term when it does) - Present multiple Native American perspectives where possible; the Nez Perce, Lakota, and other nations are not monolithic — different leaders had different responses to federal pressure - Chief Joseph's statement should be handled with the SOAPS note about transcription and translation — this is a genuine scholarly question about source reliability - Wounded Knee: present the casualty figures as a range (250–300) with citation; acknowledge that the specific circumstances of the first shot remain disputed; the outcome is not disputed - Avoid both (a) a purely victimhood narrative that portrays Native peoples as passive subjects of American history and (b) a narrative that minimizes the scale of dispossession and violence; both distort the historical reality

The Coal Creek War — Tennessee content: The Coal Creek War (1891–92) in Anderson County, Tennessee is a documented historical event in which free miners rose up against the convict lease system used by the Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company (TCI). Convict leasing — renting state prisoners, disproportionately Black men arrested on minor charges, to private companies — was the intersection of Gilded Age capitalism and post-Reconstruction racial control. This is an excellent Tennessee-specific case study that connects Units 1 and 2 and demonstrates that the issues of labor exploitation and racial control were not separate systems but intertwined ones. UNIT 2 GLOSSARY ENTRIES

(For Appendix A — Alphabetical Glossary)

AFL (American Federation of Labor) | (AY-ef-EL) | A national labor organization founded in 1886 by Samuel Gompers that organized skilled workers by their specific craft or trade. Unlike the Knights of Labor, the AFL focused on practical goals — higher wages, shorter hours, better conditions — rather than transforming the economic system. | "The AFL's focused, pragmatic approach helped it survive when the more ambitious Knights of Labor collapsed." | Unit 2, Section 2.2

Carnegie, Andrew (1835–1919) | (KAR-neh-gee) | A Scottish-born industrialist who built Carnegie Steel into the dominant force in American steel production using vertical integration. He became one of the wealthiest men in the world and later gave away nearly all of his fortune to public libraries, universities, and other causes. He articulated his philanthropic philosophy in "The Gospel of Wealth" (1889). | Unit 2, Section 2.1

Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) | A federal law that prohibited Chinese laborers from entering the United States — the first and for many decades the only law to bar immigration based on a specific race or nationality. Chinese immigrants already in the U.S. were denied the right to become citizens. | "The Chinese Exclusion Act was a direct contradiction of the 14th Amendment's equal protection principle — it remained law until 1943." | Unit 2, Section 2.2

crop lien | (krop leen) | A legal arrangement in which a farmer used a future harvest as collateral for a loan or credit. Merchants and landowners could claim a farmer's crop before it was harvested. Combined with high interest rates, crop liens trapped farmers in cycles of debt. | "The crop lien system gave merchants and landowners legal control over farmers' harvests before the crops were even planted, making it nearly impossible for indebted farmers to save money." | Unit 2, Section 2.3

Dawes Act (1887) | (dawz akt) | Also known as the General Allotment Act, this federal law divided communally held tribal lands into individual allotments of 160 acres per family. "Surplus" land was sold to white settlers. The result was a catastrophic loss of Native American land — from approximately 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million acres by 1934. | Unit 2, Section 2.3

deflation | (deh-FLAY-shun) | A sustained decrease in the general price level of goods and services. While falling prices sound good for consumers, deflation hurts debtors because the real value of their debt increases — they have to repay loans in dollars that are worth more than when they borrowed them. Gilded Age farmers suffered severely from deflation. | Unit 2, Section 2.3

Ghost Dance | A spiritual movement that spread among Plains Indian nations in the late 1880s, originating with the Paiute prophet Wovoka. Participants believed the ritual dance would restore the buffalo, return the dead, and remove white settlers. Federal authorities perceived it as threatening and used it as a pretext for military intervention that led to the Wounded Knee Massacre. | Unit 2, Section 2.3

Gilded Age | (GIL-did ayj) | The period of American history roughly from the 1870s to the 1890s, characterized by rapid industrialization, extreme inequality, political corruption, and spectacular concentrations of wealth. The term was coined by Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner in their 1873 novel, suggesting that the era's glittering surface of prosperity covered deep social problems. | Unit 2, all sections

Gospel of Wealth | An 1889 essay by Andrew Carnegie (originally titled "Wealth") arguing that wealthy men had a moral obligation to use their fortunes for the public good rather than leaving them to heirs. Carnegie defended capitalism and wealth concentration while arguing that voluntary philanthropy was the proper solution to inequality — an argument later critics found inadequate. | Unit 2, Section 2.1

horizontal integration | (hor-ih-ZON-tal in-teh-GRAY-shun) | A business strategy of buying out or merging with competitors at the same stage of production, thereby eliminating competition. Rockefeller used this strategy to build Standard Oil's near-monopoly on oil refining. Contrast with vertical integration. | Unit 2, Section 2.1

Knights of Labor | A national labor organization founded in 1869 that organized all workers — skilled and unskilled, men and women, Black and white — across craft lines. Led by Terence Powderly, the Knights advocated for a transformation of the economic system. They reached peak membership of approximately 700,000 in 1886 but collapsed after the Haymarket Affair. | Unit 2, Section 2.2

monopoly | (muh-NAH-puh-lee) | When a single company or small group controls an entire industry or market, eliminating meaningful competition. Standard Oil's control of approximately 90% of U.S. oil refining is a classic example. Monopolies allow companies to set prices without competitive pressure, often at consumers' expense. | Unit 2, Section 2.1

nativism | (NAY-tiv-iz-um) | A political ideology that prioritizes the interests of native-born citizens over those of immigrants, often based on ethnic, racial, or religious prejudice. In the Gilded Age, nativism produced the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) and eventually the Immigration Restriction Act (1924, Unit 4). | Unit 2, Section 2.2

new immigration | A term used by historians and contemporaries to describe the wave of immigration that shifted in the 1880s–1920s away from Northwestern Europe toward Southern and Eastern Europe (Italy, Poland, Russia, Greece) and other regions. New immigrants were more likely to be Catholic, Orthodox Christian, or Jewish, and settled predominantly in urban industrial areas. | Unit 2, Section 2.2

Omaha Platform (1892) | The founding platform of the People's Party (Populists), adopted at their first national convention in Omaha, Nebraska on July 4, 1892. It called for a graduated income tax, government ownership of railroads, direct election of senators, free coinage of silver, and other democratic reforms. Many of its demands became law in the Progressive Era and New Deal. | Unit 2, Section 2.3

Pinkerton Detective Agency | A private security company frequently hired by industrialists to provide guards, infiltrate unions with informants, and physically break strikes. The Pinkertons became symbols of corporate anti-labor violence, particularly after the Battle of Homestead (1892) in which Pinkerton agents fought a pitched battle with striking steelworkers. | Unit 2, Section 2.2

Populism | (POP-yuh-liz-um) | A political movement that claims to represent ordinary working people — farmers, laborers, small business owners — against wealthy and powerful elites. In the U.S. context, the Populist movement of the 1890s specifically represented farmers against railroad monopolies, bankers, and the gold-standard monetary system. | Unit 2, Section 2.3

reservation system | The U.S. government policy of confining Native American nations to specific tracts of land (reservations) while opening the remaining territory to white settlement. The reservation system destroyed traditional economic systems, created dependency on federal rations, and was systematically violated when valuable resources were discovered on reservation land. | Unit 2, Section 2.3

Rockefeller, John D. (1839–1937) | (ROK-uh-fel-er) | An industrialist who built Standard Oil into a monopoly controlling approximately 90% of American oil refining through horizontal integration, secret railroad rebates, and predatory pricing. He was the first billionaire in American history and also a significant philanthropist. Standard Oil was broken up by the Supreme Court in 1911. | Unit 2, Section 2.1

Social Darwinism | (SO-shul DAR-win-iz-um) | An ideological framework (NOT a scientific theory) associated with Herbert Spencer that applied the language of evolutionary "survival of the fittest" to human society. It argued that inequality was natural and that government assistance to the poor was counterproductive. It was widely used to justify Gilded Age inequality and to oppose labor reform. | Unit 2, Section 2.1

tenement | (TEN-uh-ment) | A densely packed urban apartment building, typically housing many families in small units with minimal light, ventilation, and sanitation. Tenements were the primary housing for immigrants and poor workers in Gilded Age cities. Jacob Riis's photographs documented their conditions. | Unit 2, Section 2.2

trust | (trust) | In the Gilded Age business context, a legal arrangement in which multiple companies transferred control of their stock to a board of trustees, eliminating competition among them while technically remaining separate companies. Rockefeller's Standard Oil Trust is the most famous example. The widespread abuse of this structure led to antitrust legislation. | Unit 2, Section 2.1

vertical integration | (VER-tih-kul in-teh-GRAY-shun) | A business strategy of owning multiple stages of a product's production and distribution, from raw materials to finished product. Carnegie's ownership of iron ore mines, shipping lines, steel mills, and railroads is the classic example. Vertical integration reduces costs by eliminating middlemen. | Unit 2, Section 2.1

Wounded Knee Massacre (1890) | An engagement on December 29, 1890, in which U.S. Army troops killed between 250 and 300 Miniconjou Lakota men, women, and children at Wounded Knee Creek in present-day South Dakota. The massacre effectively ended armed Native American resistance to U.S. policy on the Great Plains. | Unit 2, Section 2.3
