September 11, 2001: The Day That Changed Everything[CAUSATION]

At 8:46 AM Eastern time on Tuesday, September 11, 2001, American Airlines Flight 11 crashed into the North Tower of the World Trade Center in New York City. Seventeen minutes later, United Airlines Flight 175 struck the South Tower. At 9:37 AM, American Airlines Flight 77 struck the Pentagon in Arlington, Virginia. At 10:03 AM, United Airlines Flight 93 crashed in Shanksville, Pennsylvania — brought down by passengers who had learned via phone calls what had happened to the earlier flights and attempted to retake the aircraft. [VERIFY: All four flight times and crash times — these are well-documented in the 9/11 Commission Report; confirm against the official report, publicly available at 9/11commission.gov or through the National Archives]

In 102 minutes, 2,977 people were killed — the deadliest attack on American soil since Pearl Harbor. The attackers were 19 members of the terrorist organization al-Qaeda, led by Osama bin Laden. [VERIFY: 2,977 deaths — this is the current official figure; it has been updated over the years as additional deaths were attributed to 9/11-related causes; confirm]

The Immediate Response: President George W. Bush addressed the nation that evening; on September 14, standing at Ground Zero in New York, he promised through a bullhorn: "I can hear you! The rest of the world hears you! And the people who knocked these buildings down will hear all of us soon." [VERIFY: Bush's Ground Zero statement — September 14, 2001; this is widely documented; confirm exact language against primary source]

Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force (AUMF) on September 18, 2001, authorizing the president to use "all necessary and appropriate force" against those who "planned, authorized, committed, or aided" the September 11 attacks. [VERIFY: AUMF — Public Law 107-40; signed September 18, 2001; passed 98-0 in Senate; 420-1 in House (the single dissenting vote was Representative Barbara Lee of California); confirm] The AUMF was written so broadly that it was still being used to justify military operations in multiple countries more than two decades later.

Afghanistan — A Twenty-Year War: The Afghanistan War became America's longest war — lasting from October 2001 until the chaotic withdrawal of U.S. forces in August 2021. [VERIFY: withdrawal date — final U.S. troops left August 30, 2021; Taliban retook Kabul August 15, 2021; confirm; this is very recent history near the knowledge cutoff] Approximately 2,448 U.S. service members died in Afghanistan; over 20,000 were wounded. The USA PATRIOT Act and Civil Liberties[COMPARISON]

Six weeks after 9/11, Congress passed the USA PATRIOT Act (Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism) — a 342-page document that significantly expanded government surveillance powers. [VERIFY: PATRIOT Act — signed October 26, 2001; full name and acronym are correct; 342 pages; confirm; many members of Congress admitted they had not read the bill before voting for it — this is documented]

Key provisions: - Expanded wiretapping authority to include the Internet - "Roving wiretaps" that tracked a person rather than a specific phone number - National Security Letters allowing the FBI to demand records without court approval - Expanded sharing of intelligence between law enforcement and intelligence agencies - The "material support" provision, broadly defining aid to terrorist organizations

The civil liberties debate: For the PATRIOT Act: The attacks demonstrated that existing surveillance tools were inadequate; the FBI and CIA had failed to share information that might have prevented 9/11; the provisions were subject to court oversight; national security requires tools appropriate to the threat.

Against the PATRIOT Act: The law was passed without adequate debate or public deliberation; it expanded government surveillance in ways that could suppress political dissent; the "material support" provision could criminalize innocent associations; NSA surveillance programs conducted under the PATRIOT Act's authority (revealed by Edward Snowden in 2013) were far broader than Congress understood. [VERIFY: Snowden revelations — June 2013; the Guardian and Washington Post published NSA surveillance documents; confirm]

[MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES: this is a genuine, unresolved tension between security and civil liberties; present both positions fairly; TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — the constitutional republic's balancing of individual rights and national security is core civics content; connect to Korematsu (Unit 6) and the "loaded weapon" problem]

The Iraq War: Decision, Justification, and Consequence

On March 20, 2003, the United States and a "coalition of the willing" invaded Iraq. The stated justifications: 1. Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction (WMDs) 2. Iraq had links to al-Qaeda and September 11 3. Saddam Hussein's regime was a threat to regional and global security

None of these justifications was adequately supported by the available evidence: - UN weapons inspectors had found no evidence of active WMD programs - The CIA and DIA assessments of the Iraq-al-Qaeda link were much more qualified than the administration's public statements suggested - The "Downing Street Memo" (leaked British intelligence document, 2005) indicated that by July 2002, American officials had already decided to invade and were "fixing the intelligence and facts" around the policy decision [VERIFY: Downing Street Memo — dated July 23, 2002; published by the Sunday Times (UK) May 1, 2005; describes a meeting between British PM Blair and intelligence officials; confirm and cite appropriately]

The war itself: U.S. forces reached Baghdad within three weeks; Saddam Hussein was captured in December 2003 and executed in December 2006. [VERIFY: Baghdad fell — April 9, 2003; Hussein captured — December 13, 2003; executed — December 30, 2006; confirm all] But no WMDs were found. [VERIFY: Iraq Survey Group final report — October 2004; found no WMD stockpiles; confirm]

The occupation produced a prolonged insurgency, sectarian civil war between Sunni and Shia Muslims, and the eventual rise of ISIS (Islamic State in Iraq and Syria). Approximately 4,431 American service members died in Iraq. [VERIFY: Iraq War American deaths — 4,431 is the widely cited official figure; confirm against Department of Defense records]

The Iraq War fundamentally damaged American credibility — the world's leading power had invaded a country on false pretenses, with catastrophic regional consequences that persist to the present.

Abu Ghraib and "Enhanced Interrogation": In 2004, photographs from Abu Ghraib prison in Iraq showed U.S. soldiers abusing Iraqi detainees — forced nudity, human pyramids, simulated sexual acts, use of dogs to terrify prisoners. The images shocked the world. [VERIFY: Abu Ghraib photos — published April 28, 2004 by CBS News and the New Yorker; confirm date and publication source]

Simultaneously, the Bush administration authorized "enhanced interrogation techniques" (EITs) — a term that critics argued was a euphemism for torture. Techniques authorized included waterboarding (simulated drowning), sleep deprivation, stress positions, and "walling" (slamming detainees against a specially constructed wall). [VERIFY: "Torture memos" — the "Bybee memos" drafted by OLC attorneys John Yoo and Jay Bybee in August 2002; the Senate Intelligence Committee report on torture published December 2014; confirm the specific techniques that were authorized]

The legal, moral, and strategic debate: - The Bush administration argued these techniques were legally defensible; had produced actionable intelligence; were not "torture" under U.S. law - Critics argued they violated the Convention Against Torture (which the U.S. had ratified), domestic law, and basic human dignity; that they produced unreliable intelligence; and that they damaged American credibility and recruitment for radical groups - The Senate Intelligence Committee's 2014 report concluded that the EITs did not produce unique intelligence that prevented attacks [VERIFY: Senate Intelligence Committee report — "Committee Study of the Central Intelligence Agency's Detention and Interrogation Program"; released December 9, 2014; chaired by Senator Dianne Feinstein; confirm]

[MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — present both positions accurately; TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — constitutional republic and the rule of law apply even in national security contexts]

SECTION 10.2: ECONOMIC CRISIS AND POLITICAL POLARIZATION

Section Learning Targets: - I can explain the causes of the 2008 financial crisis — including subprime mortgages, securitization, and regulatory failure. - I can analyze the government's response to the crisis and evaluate its effectiveness and fairness. - I can describe the election and presidency of Barack Obama. - I can analyze the forces driving political polarization in 21st-century America.

Narrative Text

The 2008 Financial Crisis: "Too Big to Fail"[CAUSATION]

The 2008 financial crisis was the worst economic catastrophe since the Great Depression. Its causes were structural — built into the American financial system over decades of deregulation and financial innovation.

The Housing Bubble and Subprime Mortgages: Through the early 2000s, low interest rates, financial innovation, and regulatory loosening produced a dramatic expansion of home lending — including to borrowers who could not actually afford the loans they were receiving ("subprime" mortgages).

The mechanism: 1. Banks made subprime mortgages to borrowers with poor credit 2. These mortgages were "securitized" — bundled into complex financial instruments (mortgage-backed securities, CDOs) and sold to investors worldwide 3. Rating agencies gave these instruments high safety ratings they did not deserve 4. When housing prices stopped rising and borrowers began defaulting, the instruments' value collapsed — and with them the financial institutions that held them

The Collapse (September–October 2008): The crisis cascaded rapidly: - Lehman Brothers, a 158-year-old investment bank, filed for bankruptcy on September 15, 2008 — the largest bankruptcy filing in U.S. history [VERIFY: Lehman bankruptcy — September 15, 2008; largest bankruptcy in U.S. history at the time; confirm] - The federal government rescued AIG (a massive insurance company) — to the tune of $182 billion — because its failure could have collapsed the global financial system [VERIFY: AIG rescue — initial commitment was $85 billion in September 2008; total eventually exceeded $180 billion; confirm] - Congress passed the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) — $700 billion in authority to purchase distressed assets and inject capital into banks [VERIFY: TARP — Emergency Economic Stabilization Act; signed October 3, 2008; $700 billion authority; most was eventually repaid; confirm] - The stock market fell approximately 54% from its 2007 peak to its 2009 low [VERIFY: market decline — the S&P 500 fell approximately 57% peak to trough; confirm specific figures] - 8.7 million jobs were lost in the Great Recession [VERIFY: job losses — approximately 8.7 million jobs lost from December 2007 to June 2009; confirm against BLS data]

The Political Response — Obama's Stimulus: Barack Obama, who was elected president in November 2008 amidst the crisis, signed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (ARRA) — an $831 billion stimulus package — within a month of taking office. [VERIFY: ARRA — signed February 17, 2009; $831 billion is the CBO estimate; confirm]

The stimulus' effects are debated: - Most mainstream economists credit it with preventing a second Great Depression and accelerating recovery - Critics argued it was too large, too much of it went to consumption rather than investment, and it produced an unsustainable increase in the national debt - The recovery was the slowest since WWII — unemployment remained above 8% for three years [VERIFY: recovery timeline — unemployment peaked at 10% in October 2009; confirm; the slow recovery is well-documented]

[MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES; TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — connect to New Deal comparison from Unit 5; the TARP bailout of banks echoes the RFC in a different ideological context]

The "Too Big to Fail" Debate: The government's rescue of banks deemed "too big to fail" — meaning their collapse would threaten the entire financial system — generated enormous political anger across the political spectrum.

Conservatives argued: rescuing private institutions from the consequences of their own bad decisions was a violation of free market principles; it rewarded recklessness and created incentives for future risk-taking ("moral hazard").

Liberals argued: the banks had taken excessive risks enabled by deregulation; the people most harmed by the crisis (homeowners, workers) received far less relief than the banks that caused it; the system protected Wall Street while Main Street suffered.

Both critiques contained genuine insight. The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (2010) attempted to address some underlying causes — creating new consumer protection mechanisms and attempting to limit "too big to fail" risks — but its adequacy remains debated. [VERIFY: Dodd-Frank — signed July 21, 2010; confirm]

Barack Obama: History Made, History Complicated[CCOT]

Barack Obama's election on November 4, 2008 was a historic event: the first African American president of the United States, less than 50 years after the Civil Rights Act of 1964 that John Lewis and Diane Nash had helped produce.

For many Americans, Obama's election felt like the fulfillment of the long arc toward racial equality that the course has traced from Reconstruction through the Civil Rights Movement. [TCA § 49-6-1006 — connect explicitly to the full course narrative; Unit 7 Nashville leaders; Unit 1 14th Amendment; etc.]

What His Election Meant: - A Black man with the middle name Hussein won the presidency of a country that had enslaved Black people 140 years earlier and maintained legal segregation 50 years earlier - His campaign mobilized young voters, Black voters, and new voters at historic rates - He won 52.9% of the popular vote and 365 electoral votes [VERIFY: 2008 election results — confirm]

The Limits of "Post-Racial" America: Obama's election prompted some commentators to declare that America had become "post-racial" — that race was no longer a significant barrier to achievement. The evidence did not support this conclusion:

  • Obama faced racialized opposition from the "birther" movement — the false claim, amplified by Donald Trump among others, that Obama was not born in the United States and was therefore ineligible for the presidency. Obama released his birth certificate in April 2011. [VERIFY: Obama birth certificate release — April 27, 2011; confirm; Obama was born in Honolulu, Hawaii on August 4, 1961; this is documented] [MULTIPLE PERSPECTIVES — present the birther claim accurately as a disproven conspiracy theory, not as a contested factual question; note its racial dimension without attributing motivation beyond what is documented]

  • Racial disparities in wealth, income, health, education, and criminal justice persisted and in some cases widened during the Obama years

  • The Black Lives Matter movement emerged during Obama's presidency in response to high-profile killings of Black men and women by police officers

Obama's Major Achievements: - The Affordable Care Act (ACA, 2010) — the most significant expansion of health insurance coverage since Medicare/Medicaid (1965); extended coverage to approximately 20 million previously uninsured Americans through insurance market regulation, Medicaid expansion, and subsidized exchanges [VERIFY: ACA — signed March 23, 2010; 20 million new insured is the commonly cited figure for peak ACA coverage; confirm against CBO and HHS data] - The killing of Osama bin Laden (May 2, 2011) - Ending the Iraq War — all combat troops withdrawn December 2011 [VERIFY: Iraq withdrawal — December 18, 2011; confirm] - The Dodd-Frank financial reform - The Paris Climate Agreement (2015) [VERIFY: Paris Agreement — December 12, 2015; U.S. signed April 22, 2016; confirm]

The Tea Party and Political Polarization: The Tea Party movement emerged in 2009 as a conservative grassroots reaction to the stimulus, the ACA, and what its supporters called government overreach. [VERIFY: Tea Party — emerged February 2009; first organized protests February 27, 2009; confirm]

The Tea Party accelerated political polarization that had been building for decades: the geographic sorting of Americans into politically homogeneous communities; the collapse of ideological overlap between the two parties (the "moderate" Republican or Democrat became an endangered species); the rise of partisan media (Fox News on the right, MSNBC on the left) that provided audiences with different factual worlds.

Economic Inequality: The Long-Run Problem

The United States has experienced growing economic inequality since the 1970s — a pattern that accelerated through the Reagan years (Unit 9) and has continued in the 21st century.

Key data points: - The share of income going to the top 1% of earners rose from approximately 10% in 1980 to approximately 20% by the 2010s [VERIFY: income share data — Emmanuel Saez's income inequality research at UC Berkeley provides the most authoritative data; confirm specific figures against that database] - Median household income, adjusted for inflation, was approximately the same in 2018 as it was in 2000 [VERIFY: median household income stagnation — confirm against Census Bureau data] - The Gini coefficient (measuring income inequality) has risen consistently since the 1970s [VERIFY: U.S. Gini coefficient trend — confirm against Census Bureau data] - Wealth inequality is even more extreme than income inequality: the top 1% own approximately 30-35% of all wealth; the bottom 50% own approximately 2% [VERIFY: wealth distribution figures — confirm against Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts data]

The causes are debated: technological change that rewards high-skill workers; globalization that allows companies to shift production to low-wage countries; declining union power; the Reagan-era tax restructuring; the financialization of the economy; education gaps; housing costs in high-productivity cities.

The consequences are clear: the sense that hard work no longer guarantees a middle-class life has fueled political anger on both the left (Sanders-style democratic socialism) and the right (Trump-style economic nationalism) and contributed to the polarization that defines 21st-century American politics.

Democratic Institutions Under Stress

Several major events in the 2010s and 2020s raised serious questions about the health of American democratic institutions:

Social Media and Democratic Discourse: The rise of social media has transformed how Americans receive information, engage in political discourse, and form political identities. The effects are contested:

Negative: Disinformation spreads faster than corrections; algorithms amplify outrage and division; "filter bubbles" expose users only to confirming viewpoints; foreign actors (particularly Russia) have used social media platforms to amplify divisions; social media platforms' role in January 6 is documented.

Black Lives Matter and the Ongoing Civil Rights Conversation

The Black Lives Matter movement emerged in 2013 following the acquittal of George Zimmerman in the shooting death of 17-year-old Trayvon Martin. [VERIFY: BLM founding — Black Lives Matter was founded by Alicia Garza, Patrisse Cullors, and Opal Tometi in 2013; Trayvon Martin shooting — February 26, 2012; Zimmerman acquitted — July 13, 2013; confirm all dates]

The movement gained national prominence following: - The police killing of Michael Brown in Ferguson, Missouri (August 9, 2014) and the protests that followed [VERIFY: Ferguson — August 9, 2014; confirm] - The police killing of Eric Garner in New York City (July 17, 2014) [VERIFY: Garner — July 17, 2014; "I can't breathe"; confirm] - The police killing of George Floyd in Minneapolis, Minnesota (May 25, 2020) — which triggered the largest protest movement in American history, with demonstrations in all 50 states and internationally [VERIFY: Floyd killing — May 25, 2020; officer Derek Chauvin convicted of murder April 20, 2021; protests estimated at 15-26 million participants — the largest protests in U.S. history; confirm all figures]

Q1 Model Answer: 9/11: September 11, 2001; four coordinated attacks; 2,977 killed; 19 al-Qaeda hijackers; Osama bin Laden; two planes into World Trade Center; one into Pentagon; one crashed in Shanksville, PA after passenger resistance. Immediate response: Bush addressed nation; Congress passed AUMF September 18, 2001 (98-0 Senate; 420-1 House); AUMF authorized force against "those who planned, authorized, committed, or aided" the attacks. Constitutional significance: the AUMF was written so broadly — without naming a specific enemy state or geographic limit — that it was still being cited to justify military operations more than 20 years later in multiple countries Congress never specifically approved; it essentially delegated broad war power to the executive, raising the same constitutional questions as the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (Unit 8).

Q2 Model Answer: 2008 causes: subprime mortgages (loans to borrowers with poor credit who could not actually afford them); securitization (mortgages bundled into complex financial instruments — mortgage-backed securities, CDOs — and sold to investors who did not fully understand the underlying risk); rating agencies gave these instruments false high-safety ratings; housing prices stopped rising and borrowers defaulted; instruments collapsed; financial institutions holding them failed. Lehman Brothers bankrupt September 15, 2008; AIG rescued ($182B+); TARP — $700B authority to purchase distressed assets and inject bank capital; signed October 3, 2008. Comparison to Depression: both involved banking collapse; both required massive government intervention; 2008 did not reach Depression unemployment levels (10% vs. ~25%) partly because of faster, larger government response; both created new permanent regulatory institutions (FDIC from Depression; Dodd-Frank/CFPB from 2008).

Q4 Model Answer: Shelby County v. Holder (2013): 5-4 decision; struck down Section 4(b) of the Voting Rights Act — the formula that determined which jurisdictions needed federal preclearance before changing their voting laws. State of democratic institutions: January 6 revealed that the constitutional republic can survive an attack on its processes only if sufficient numbers of officials — legislators, judges, military commanders, election officials — do their constitutional duty; in this case, they did, but the near-miss raised serious questions about the durability of democratic norms.

Critical Content Notes

September 11 — Sensitivity and Historical Accuracy: September 11 is within living memory for many students' families and some older students. Advance notice before teaching this content; create space for students to share if appropriate; provide alternative engagement for students with direct family connections who may find the content distressing. Present the historical facts clearly and accurately — 2,977 deaths; 19 hijackers; al-Qaeda; bin Laden. The policy debates (PATRIOT Act; Iraq War; enhanced interrogation) are where multiple perspectives are most important and most needed.

January 6 — Constitutional Focus: January 6 is very recent, politically charged, and personally meaningful to many students and their families. The approach: (1) Present the facts clearly and accurately — the Capitol was attacked; Congress's constitutional function was temporarily disrupted; this is documented fact (2) Focus on the CONSTITUTIONAL PRINCIPLE — peaceful transfer of power is fundamental to republican government; this principle transcends partisan politics (3) Do not adjudicate questions of culpability that are still subject to legal proceedings or genuine dispute (4) Connect to the course's recurring theme of constitutional fragility and resilience (5) TCA § 49-6-1028(b) — this is precisely the constitutional republic content the statute requires: what happens when the system is stressed?

Climate Change — Science vs. Policy: Climate change presents a unique pedagogical challenge because the scientific question and the policy question are often conflated. Be explicit about the distinction: - The scientific consensus that human-caused climate change is occurring and poses serious risks is overwhelming — this is not a matter of political opinion - The appropriate policy response (carbon tax, cap and trade, regulation, investment, nuclear energy, adaptation) is genuinely debated and involves real tradeoffs - Teaching climate change accurately means teaching the science accurately while presenting the policy debate fairly - Do NOT present the scientific reality of climate change as if it is "one side" in a two-sided debate

Dobbs and Roe — Balanced and Politically Neutral: The overturning of Roe v. Wade in 2022 is recent, deeply contested, and personally significant to many students. The approach: (1) Present the historical arc: Roe was decided in 1973; it was contested throughout its existence; Dobbs overturned it in 2022 (2) Present both constitutional arguments fairly (as instructed in Unit 8) (3) Note that this is now a state-level policy question — different states have adopted very different policies; Tennessee's specific legal situation may be appropriate to address factually [VERIFY: Tennessee's abortion law post-Dobbs — confirm current Tennessee law before discussing; the law has changed since Dobbs] (4) Maintain strict neutrality on the underlying policy question

TCA § 49-6-1028(b) Capstone: Unit 10 is the capstone for the full-course statutory requirement. By the end of this unit, students should be able to: - Identify and explain the three branches of government - Explain how laws are enacted - Name fundamental founding documents and their significance - Explain ways citizens shape government - Evaluate the virtues and limits of capitalism and constitutional government using historical evidence from the full course
